## Lecture 0 — The Molecule Nobody Will Sell You (the pencil-and-paper case)

Close the catalog. No store in this book's premise stocks hydrazine — not the pharmacy, not the hardware store, not the photographer's shelf. But the pencil case in front of you holds everything the arithmetic needs, and the household shelf holds everything the chemistry needs. That is the whole premise of this chapter in one sentence: the molecule is refused at every counter, and its recipe is public knowledge, in a patent and a forum publication, and the gap between those two facts is the Atlas itself.

Start on paper, the way the chapter's labs will end. Urea — the garden aisle's 46.65% nitrogen fertilizer, Chapter 20's own priced shelf — is one carbonyl with two amines hanging on it. Bleach — the chloralkali chapter's own product, sodium hypochlorite at 74.44 g/mol — is an oxidizer looking for something mild to oxidize. The net reaction between them under strongly alkaline water is the classical one, and it balances to the atom:

**CO(NH₂)₂ + NaOCl + 2 NaOH → N₂H₄ + H₂O + NaCl + Na₂CO₃**

Run the bookkeeping before any bench exists: 60.056 g of urea, 74.442 g of hypochlorite, and 79.994 g of drain-cleaner lye — 214.492 g in, all of it from the shelf — give 32.046 g of free hydrazine plus salt, water, and soda ash out. The pencil case has just done the impossible-sounding thing: it has *composed* a rocket-fuel precursor from fertilizer and bleach, on one line, with every atom accounted. Lab 46.1's B1 row will machine-verify this exact balance; for now count it on your fingers — two nitrogens in, two out; six hydrogens left, six right; the sodiums, the chlorine, the carbon, the four oxygens, all matched.

## Lecture 1 — The Species and the Fence (the salt you may weigh, the base you may not)

Hydrazine, N₂H₄, is ammonia's aggressive sibling: the same nitrogen-hydrogen frame with one hydrogen replaced by an entire second −NH₂. The molecule that replacement builds is a liquid at room temperature — melting point 1.4 °C, boiling point 113.5 °C, density 1.021 g/mL [convergent, the web battery's fresh sources] — which means the pure substance spends half the winter as a solid on an unheated porch. It smells of ammonia, dissolves in water in every proportion, and burns its own kind of blue. It is also one of chemistry's great reducers: so generous with its electrons that the book's thermodynamic meter prices its "lazy fall" — the two-electron slide back down to ammonia in basic water, N₂H₄ + 2 H₂O + 2 e⁻ → 2 NH₃(aq) + 2 OH⁻ — at −21.2 kJ, Chapter 20's own recognition-only row, cross-pinned this session at its owner's page. A reducing agent that eager does not survive its own synthesis pot unless someone protects it, which is Lecture 2's entire subject.

The fence around this species is older and firmer than any in the Organic Bench, and this chapter did not build it. Chapter 20 drew the line at the ammonia chapter's own bench: *hydrazine is refused* — the two register faces there held at recognition level, the meter's row priced, benched nowhere. Chapter 22 held the same line at the dyes bench: the luminol synthesis's other feedstock is hydrazine, and the page prints it **composed for bookkeeping only, benched nowhere** — 32.046 g/mol, computed, the cross-pin this chapter re-proves. The health face closes the fence: hydrazine is rated IARC 2A, *probably carcinogenic to humans* [convergent]; the U.S. workplace limits run from NIOSH's recommended 0.03 ppm two-hour ceiling to OSHA's permissible 1 ppm, with the immediately-dangerous level at 50 ppm [convergent, the NIOSH pocket guide]. And the conversion that makes those limits concrete is the book's own arithmetic: 1 ppm of hydrazine vapor is 1.31 mg/m³, which is nothing but 32.046 g/mol over Chapter 10's 24.465 L/mol working pin — 1.3099 before the round, L17 in Lab 46.2, the exposure limit priced on the book's own constants.

The sulfate is the fence's own instrument. Hydrazine sulfate — better named hydrazinium hydrogensulfate, (N₂H₅)(HSO₄), the formula N₂H₆SO₄ summing to 130.118 g/mol on the held basis [computed] — is a white crystalline salt: melting (with decomposition) at 254 °C, density 1.37, and only sparingly soluble in cold water — 2.87 g per 100 g at 20 °C, rising to 14.4 at 80 °C [convergent]. In the salt, the free base's eagerness is banked: no vapor pressure worth the name, no blue flame, a jar on a shelf. That is why every route in this chapter, without exception, ends at the sulfate and stops there — the performers do not distill the base back out, and this chapter never will. The salt is never heated to its decomposition, never dried hot, never confined, never scaled past the consumer sizes, and the safety table at the chapter's end holds those rows binding. What the sulfate gives the bench is the weighable currency of the whole lineage: three performers report their yield in grams of *this* salt, and grams of salt are the only numbers this chapter's ledgers accept.

## Lecture 2 — The 1907 System (why the recipe needs a ketone at all)

The industrial route — the Olin Raschig process, first announced in 1907 [convergent] — is the household route's skeleton: ammonia plus sodium hypochlorite, no ketone, no catalyst. It runs in two steps, and both are the book's own neighbors:

**NH₃ + NaOCl → NH₂Cl + NaOH** — the bleach makes chloramine, that toxic, faint-swimming-pool gas Chapter 28 owns at recognition level (51.476 g/mol, its 4.48 g row cross-pinned this session at its owner's page);

**NH₂Cl + NH₃ → N₂H₄ + HCl** — the chloramine chlorinates a second ammonia, and the two nitrogens are married. The hydrochloric acid is bought back by the alkali in the pot (HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O).

So far, so simple. The problem — and it is the problem the entire ketazine art exists to solve — is that the pot is lying to itself. The product, hydrazine, is a stronger reducing agent than the ammonia the process feeds on, and the same hypochlorite that gently makes chloramine from ammonia will oxidize hydrazine *faster than the pot can save it* — back to nitrogen gas and water, the yield vanishing into the headspace. The meter already priced this eagerness at Lecture 1: the species falls −21.2 kJ at the mere offer of electrons. Every yield the 1907 system ever lost was lost to this: product and reagent in one pot, with the reagent hungrier than the feedstock.

The fix is the book's P16, **protective-intermediate staging**, in the move the pattern is named for: *a reactive species that cannot survive its own workup is masked as a stable, separable intermediate and unmasked at the end.* In hydrazine's case the mask is a ketone — MEK, methyl ethyl ketone, the lacquer thinner at 72.107 g/mol [computed]. One hydrazine and two MEK molecules condense, dropping two waters:

**N₂H₄ + 2 C₄H₈O → C₈H₁₆N₂ + 2 H₂O**

The product is butanone azine — 140.230 g/mol [computed] — an oily, water-hating liquid that no residual hypochlorite in the pot can touch. The hydrazine has not been consumed; it has been *escorted out of the fight*, phase-separated from the brine underneath, and held in a form the workup can purify. The unmasking is the reverse trip, run in acid: the azine plus sulfuric acid plus water gives back two MEK molecules and the hydrazine, which the same acid immediately banks as the sulfate salt:

**C₈H₁₆N₂ + H₂SO₄ + 2 H₂O → 2 C₄H₈O + N₂H₆SO₄**

Read that line again slowly, because it contains the lineage's whole economy: the MEK is *regenerated* — the ketone is not a reagent but a carrier, cycling batch to batch, and the register's own performers say so out loud ("methyl ethyl ketone is regenerated," the ketazine episode's own line). P15, **literature-recipe recovery**, and P16, staging, are not alternatives here; the lineage this chapter prices runs them *in concert*: the recipe is recovered from the literature (the patent, the publication), and the recipe's own architecture stages the species through its workup. That double move is the chapter's one idea, and every lecture after this one is the same idea wearing a different date.

## Lecture 4 — The Workup, Priced (the recipe's arithmetic, and its two seams)

Take the publication's recipe at its own numbers and run the book's ledgers on it — every figure in this lecture is a Lab 46.2 row, computed once, exactly, at the printed precision.

**The charge book.** 314 g of 12.5% sodium hypochlorite carries 39.25 g of the active salt — 0.5273 mol (L01), and it, not the urea, is the batch's limiting reagent. The 53 g of drain-cleaner sodium hydroxide is 1.3251 mol (L02): roughly 2.5 equivalents, the strong alkalinity that keeps the pot in the Hoffmann regime where hypochlorite degrades urea rather than chlorinating it to destruction — and the ice bath that chills the bleach before addition is there because hypochlorite itself decomposes when hot, a hazard face the publication states at its own step. The 39 g of garden urea is 0.6494 mol (L03), a deliberate 23% excess over the hypochlorite: the urea is the cheap feed, and its excess pushes the chlorinating equivalent through the pot as chlorourea chemistry rather than as free chlorine. The 600 mg of gelatin is 1.54% of the urea charge (L19) — a viscolizer in the classical art, suppressing the foam that the pot's nitrogen evolution would otherwise climb out of the vessel on; the register's own cross-paper datum is the Doug's Lab episode's chapter list, where "Foaming" gets its own timestamp (14:17). Foam is a datum both performers' hands agree on.

**The computed surprise.** The MEK charge is 77 g = 1.0679 mol (L04) — and the naive read says "huge excess of solvent." The stoichiometry says otherwise: every azine demands two ketones, so 0.5273 mol of hydrazine demands 1.0546 mol of MEK = 76.04 g (L05). The publication's 77 g is the calculated minimum **plus 1.3%** (L06, the ratio 1.0127). The charge is not a pour of solvent; it is the condensation's own arithmetic, carried out to its last decimal — whoever wrote that recipe had done this exact division before typing the charge, and the chapter's engine re-derives their pencil work from first principles. This is the quiet pleasure of register chemistry: the recipe's numbers are a transcript of someone else's scratch paper, and the ledgers can read it back.

**The theoretical, the stated, and the basis lesson.** The arithmetic ceiling on this batch is the limiting hypochlorite carried through: 0.5273 mol of hydrazine banked as 68.61 g of the sulfate (L07). The publication's hands held 29.229 g — 0.2246 mol (L08) — and prints "42%." Against which basis? Both, honestly named: 29.229 g over 68.61 g is **42.60%** on the NaOCl basis (L09), and 29.229 g over the urea basis's 84.52 g is **34.59%** (L10). The publication's "42%" is honest arithmetic on the limiting-reagent basis — the correct convention — and this chapter prints the urea-basis number beside it so the basis can never again hide. A yield percent is a fraction with a denominator, and the denominator is a *decision*: state it, or the number means nothing.

**The byproducts and the acid.** The batch's salts are 30.81 g of NaCl and 55.88 g of Na₂CO₃ (L11/L12) — the brine that saturates the aqueous layer and forces the azine out of it, the publication's own "nearly saturated with NaCl from the reaction" line priced on the scale. The acid charge is 40 mL of 18 M sulfuric acid diluted into water — 0.72 mol against the 0.5273 mol the batch actually made, a 36.56% excess (L13) that the crystallization spends gladly: sulfate ion in excess drives the salt out of its own sparing solubility, the same solubility curve (2.87 g per 100 g at 20 °C) that makes the cold filtration the yield's friend.

**The azeotrope seam, named.** The publication's finishing move is azeotropic: distill the pot, take the MEK/water azeotrope overhead, and the loss of MEK from the mixture drives the azine hydrolysis to completion — the azeotrope fraction recycled to the next batch, the carrier economy closing its loop — the route's own third-party evaluation, verbatim in the register: "greatly reduces acid wastage and is partially recyclable." The publication prints that azeotrope at 77.3 °C. The standard tables print the MEK-water azeotrope near 73 °C at roughly 89% MEK [convergent, the fresh-source battery]. This chapter prints both numbers and declines to average them: the publication's figure is the performer's print, held verbatim in-source per the register's discipline; the tables' figure is the reference value; the gap — about 4 °C — is a seam of the source class this book has priced before (Chapter 44's max-density spread), named here at its own lecture and left for the review to re-verify. Nothing about the recycle logic depends on which number wins: either way the azeotrope leaves wet and the salt stays dry.
