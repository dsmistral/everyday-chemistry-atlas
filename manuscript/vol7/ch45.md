## Lecture 0 — The Organic Bench Gate (the stage, not the actor)

Forty-four chapters have made things IN liquids. This chapter makes the liquids.

The distinction sounds small and is not. A solvent is the stage, not the actor — the register's own teaching image is the perfect wingman, the one who brings parties together "without the chemistry between them" (N170, the solvent-theory lecture: liquid, high-boiling, moderately polar, inert, cheap, safe — the property list of a stage). The Isolation Campaign's eight chapters hunted elements and compounds; every one of them bubbled, melted or distilled inside some medium that was bought, poured from a jug, or simply tap water — and none of those media could do what the Organic Bench now demands: carry a reaction that has a polar face and an organic face at once. Three solvents, three relationships to the household, and — for the first time in seventeen chapters of make-or-buy pricing — two of the three are MAKE-WINS, priced by the register's own makers, and the third is not a target at all but a genre map. ## Lecture 1 — Glacial Acetic Acid, the Solid-Carrier Strategy (the acid that will not concentrate)

The problem first, because the problem is the whole chapter's first lesson in why solvents are hard. Vinegar is 5% acetic acid in water (the book's own basis, Chapter 4: ρ 1.006, 50.30 g/L); the store's strongest is 10%. Glacial acetic acid is the same molecule with the water gone — 99%+ and the word "glacial" earned honestly: it freezes at 16.6 °C, which is a winter windowsill, and the ice-like crystals the acid throws when it does are the name's own etymology (N159's demo lecture: the acid "actually gets its name from its ability to freeze at a really high temperature," and the glass-rod nucleation trick — "when I stirred it with a glass rod which provided a nucleation point a lot of small crystals appeared" — is the household's cleanest way to watch a pure liquid freeze on purpose). Between the 10% jug and the 16.6 °C crystal sits the whole difficulty: a still cannot get you there. Acetic acid boils at 118 °C against water's 100 °C — an 18-degree gap that looks distillable and is not (N212's route-choice lecture: the 100-vs-118 proximity "makes fractionation hopeless"; N159's own framing is blunter: "for a chemist, it is pretty intuitive to think that the acetic acid in vinegar can be concentrated to be nearly anhydrous using just distillation, but if this was the case this video would be like 5 minutes long so unfortunately, acetic acid is one of these nasty chemicals that just really don't cooperate with chemists, and in order for it to be very concentrated it has to go through a few chemical transformations").

The transformation route is the register's cleanest statement of the carrier logic, and it is worth printing in full because it generalizes past this acid: "turn the acetic acid into a solid that is not volatile and can relatively easily be separated from water... and capable of being turned back into a liquid" (N159). The solid is sodium acetate — "my good old friend sodium acetate," the hot-ice salt of Chapter 26, the campaign's own inventory promoted to carrier. The chain runs:

**Rung 2 — the evaporation and the yellow.** "An extremely long process, but I can't think of any better one" — water out, slowly, and an honest unknown in the middle of it: "at some point, the solution turned yellow and cloudy for some reason which was rather bad, but I just pretended like the yellow wasn't there." ## Lecture 2 — The Freezer Test and the Honest Title (purity priced by a melting point)

The second glacial maker is a different channel, a different year, and a different honesty: the title itself refuses the claim — CarbonTwelve's "Turning Vinegar into Dangerous Acid! (Not \"Glacial\" Acetic Acid)" (N212), the word "Not" doing the work a whole verification section usually does. The chain is the same carrier chain (grocery vinegar → baking soda → the ~100-g sodium-acetate target → dried → 105 mL of 98% H₂SO4 through an addition funnel, "the dense white smoke" → distillation → 48 mL of product), and the episode's finest face is its purity instrument: not a titration, not a burn, but a freezer.

The theory the test runs on is the same physics that names the acid. Pure acetic acid melts at 16.6 °C, and water is its classic depressant: near the pure end, a tenth of a percent of water lowers the melting point by roughly 0.2 °C — the near-pure slope the test calibrates on. The maker's own assay: the product "froze at 1 °C," read against the published freezing-point curve as ~85% acid — and the diagnosis follows in-source with the register's own arithmetic honesty: 95–98% acid would carry 2–5 mL of water in 48 mL of product, and that water "perfectly explains 85%." The engine prices the naive end of the same test: a 15.6 °C depression on the linear 0.2-per-0.1% slope is 78 steps of 0.1% — 7.8% water, ~92.2% acid on the straight line (L11) — while the real curve, which steepens as the water grows, prices the same freeze at the maker's ~85%. The straight line and the curve disagree in the same direction for the same reason the test works at all: the depression is real, the slope is not constant, and the honest instrument is the curve, not the line. The chapter prints both and holds the register's read.

And then the machine pass, the fragment flag the register itself filed. The episode claims "98%" for its 48 mL. The shown charges do not close on it: 730 mL of 10% vinegar is 73 g of acetic acid at the flat basis (the register's own pass), and 48 mL at ρ 1.049 is 50.35 g (L07a) — 69.0% as-shown on the flat basis (L07b), 68.6% on the density-extended basis (730 mL × ρ 1.006 × 0.10 = 73.44 g, L07c). Even at 100% conversion the shown charges cannot make 48 mL of 98% acid from 73 g of acetic content; the likely seam is the target — the stated ~100-g sodium-acetate goal against the 73-g theoretical is a 1.37× over-plan the episode's bookkeeping never reconciles, and the freezer test's own ~85% is the number the charges actually support. The claim is flagged, not asserted as an in-source error: the register's fragment-flag discipline, worn here as the chapter's second instrument — three instruments (the curve, the charge arithmetic, the linear slope), three answers (~85%, ~69% as-made-then-assayed, ~92% naive-linear), and the honest title standing over all of them: not glacial, and its maker said so first.

The cross-channel comparison the episode itself makes is the shelf's own economics: "others reach 95% via drier reagents" — which is N159's whole point, the constraint-driven 96%-drain-cleaner selection, the two makers' yields differing by exactly the water their acid selections carry. The 98%-acid route (N212's bottle) buys a drier displacement; the 96%-drain-cleaner route (N159) accepts 95% as the price of the only acid the store sells without water. The shelf's first verdict, priced by its two makers: glacial acetic acid is a make-win because the alternative is a 5% jug, and the yield you get is the yield your drain cleaner's water content prints on the product — the reagent's purity IS the product's assay.

## Lecture 4 — Dioxane, the Azeotrope and the Antifreeze (the solvent nobody will sell you)

The shelf's third member is its reason for existing. Dioxane — 1,4-dioxane, the six-membered cyclic diether, C₄H₈O₂ at 88.106 — is the solvent that dissolves water and organics alike ("dioxane dissolves both water, polar and organic substances so it can be used for experiments requiring the combination of a diverse set of reagents," N270; the perfect-wingman lecture of N170), boils at 101.1 °C, carries a possible-carcinogen classification (IARC 2B; NIOSH REL C 1 ppm/30-min with the IDLH at 500 ppm, both wearing the "Ca" carcinogen notation), and forms explosive peroxides on storage with air — the hazard pair that keeps it off the consumer shelf and the property pair that keeps the organic bench wanting it. And it carries the book's own coincidence on its formula line: C₄H₈O₂, 88.106, is ethyl acetate's constitutional isomer — Chapter 21's ester and Chapter 45's ether, the same atoms arranged in a ring instead of a chain, the same molar mass priced at two owners (the digit identity is the coincidence, the molecules are strangers; named here and never laundered).

The make is the register's most completely priced synthesis — two makers, one route, one honest zero, and one pycnometer.

**The route.** Dehydrate ethylene glycol twice over: 2 C₂H₆O₂ → C₄H₈O₂ + 2 H₂O (B04, 124.136 both sides — two glycol molecules are "essentially two halves of one dioxane molecule," N270's two-halves teaching image), sulfuric acid the catalyst, the products both lighter-boiling than the feed, distilling off and pulling the equilibrium with them — the product-removal-as-driver lecture, Le Chatelier without the name. The charge is the store's own: antifreeze, with the register's label rule ("Make sure THE LABEL SAYS IT'S MADE WITH ETHYLENE GLYCOL.
