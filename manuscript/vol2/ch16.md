# Vol II · Chapter 16 — Oxidizers of the Household: The Upper End of the Redox Ladder

## Lecture 0 — The shelf's other half, read before anything is poured

Chapter 11 built the electron court and let the metals sue each other for their compounds; Chapter 12 put a price on the electron and showed the cell paying it; Chapter 15 climbed the acid ladder to the strong-acid bench. This chapter walks to the *other* end of the activity series — the end that takes electrons rather than gives them — because the ruined world sells that end ready-made, and it is the most dangerous thing on any household shelf. **The thesis, and it is the volume's hinge stated a second time: an oxidizer is a reagent that has already been paid for — the world's shelf sells concentrated electron demand by the bottle, and the discipline this chapter teaches is not how to make that demand stronger but how to read what strength the bottle actually holds, keep it from spending itself on the wrong shelf-mate, and step it up only where the chemistry is honest and the door is licensed.**

Sit at the kitchen table with the notebook and pour nothing. The pencil-and-paper census, before any apparatus:

- **The brown bottle** — drugstore hydrogen peroxide, 3% w/w, the dark plastic bottle with the venting cap; the 12% "40-volume" hair developer at the beauty supply; and behind the pharmacy counter in some countries, the 35% class this book never schedules.
- **The white jug** — household bleach, sodium hypochlorite at 5.25% (the traditional regular class) or 8.25% (the concentrated class), always sold basic, always sold unstable, always sold with an expiration nobody prints.
- **The pool shelf** — calcium hypochlorite granules at 65–70% available chlorine; the trichloroisocyanuric acid pucks at ~90%; the "non-chlorine shock" bucket, which is potassium peroxymonosulfate — an oxidizer so polite the label says *spa*.
- **The purple crystal** — potassium permanganate, sold for iron removal in well water and stump treatment at the farm store, gram-for-gram the strongest oxidizer an ordinary person can buy without a question asked.
- **The dry box** — sodium percarbonate (the "oxygen bleach" laundry booster, a cocrystal that is one-third hydrogen peroxide by mass), the free-flowing powder that makes the liquid peroxide bottle look like what it is: a bottle of water with a reagent dissolved in it.

That is five families and four shelves, and not one of them is sold as a *chemical* — every one is sold as a *product*, with its assay hidden inside a marketing convention. The chapter's five questions, in bench order: **what does each label's number actually mean (the assay conventions), which bottle is strongest (the redox meter at the top), how does the shelf's weakest family get stepped up (hypochlorite's two upgrades: the boiling pot and the cell), what does one purple reagent do in three different pots (permanganate's three faces), and how do you keep any of them — stored, dated, and separated from everything that burns?**

One boundary before any apparatus is unpacked, and it is the book's binding line for this entire chapter, inherited from Chapter 15's nitric line and the durable energetics resolution: **every oxidizer in this Atlas is benched as a reagent — an oxidant, a titrant, an oxygen source, an assay tool — and nothing else. No oxidizer-plus-fuel mixture procedure appears anywhere in this book: no compositions, no ignition recipes, no energetic demonstrations; the corpus's one energetic-material face (N75, the guncotton video) remains cited at metadata level only, through the hazard-recognition lens of Chapter 28, and the lens itself is the lesson.** The chapter prints the arithmetic of the strongest things on the household shelf because a complete book prices the doors; it opens none of them.

## Lecture 1 — The oxidizer census: reading labels that were written to hide the assay

**How you could have found it.** Buy one bottle of each family, read the label, and try to answer the only question that matters at the bench: *how many moles am I holding?* The label will not say. The oxidizer shelf is sold in four mutually incompatible conventions, and the first skill of this chapter is translating all four into the one currency the bench speaks — molarity, by way of the three-way arithmetic of Chapter 4 and Lab 15.1: **C = 10·wt%·ρ/M**.

**Convention one: weight percent.** The peroxide bottle (3% w/w) and the bleach jug (5.25% or 8.25% sodium hypochlorite) sell by mass of active ingredient per mass of product. Straightforward — except the bleach jug's ρ is its own quiet number (tabled: 1.093 g/mL at 5% solution, rising to 1.21 at 14%), and the label prints neither the density nor the date that would make the number true.

**Convention two: "available chlorine."** The pool shelf sells hypochlorite products by the mass of Cl₂ that would do the same oxidizing — a convention from the water-treatment industry, where what matters is oxidizing equivalents, not chemical identity. The conversion is one honest multiplication: a mole of NaOCl carries the oxidizing work of a mole of Cl₂, so the factor is 70.906/74.442 = 0.9525 — and the elegant accident of the regular jug is that **5.25% sodium hypochlorite *is* 5.00% available chlorine, which is why both numbers appear on both labels and generations of homeowners have assumed they were the same thing**. The trap runs the other way at the pool store: calcium hypochlorite products quote available chlorine *directly* (65–70% for the granular shock class) — and the pure salt's own ceiling is 2×70.906/142.982 = 99.2% available chlorine, so the pool label's number is a *product assay*, not a conversion, and the label's remainder is water, calcium chloride, and the salt's own slow decay (Lecture 5).

**Convention three: "volumes."** The beauty-supply developer sells peroxide in *volumes of oxygen* — the volume of O₂ (at STP, the 22.414 L/mol of Chapter 10's table, not the 24.5 working pin) released per volume of reagent, a convention from the bleaching trade. The honest arithmetic runs 10×wt%·ρ/M, halved for the O₂, times 22.414: the 3% bottle computes to 9.98 ≈ **10 volume**, the 6% developer to 20.2 ≈ **20 volume**, the 12% to 41.1 ≈ **40 volume** — the label classes are the STP convention rounded, and the drafting session's own engine initially ran the chain on the book's RTP pin and read 10.9 for the 10-volume bottle: the catch is printed as found, because the conventions are the lesson (Lab 16.1 Run 5).

**Convention four: the purple crystal's none at all.** Potassium permanganate is sold by the pound with no assay convention whatsoever — 99% class crystals, and the bench makes its own solutions and standardizes them itself (Lecture 4: the self-indicating titration). The strongest bottle on the household shelf is also the only one sold without a number.

**The census itself, in the bench's currency.** Run every label through the composition engine (the same three-way arithmetic, all masses from the held element table — Lab 16.1, executed and verbatim below): the regular jug is 0.769 M sodium hypochlorite; the concentrated jug 1.258 M (on the two tabled density datums, interpolated); the 3% peroxide bottle 0.891 M; the 40-volume developer 3.669 M; and the quietest label in the house — the laundry booster's cocrystal 2Na₂CO₃·3H₂O₂ — computes to **32.50% hydrogen peroxide by mass**, ten times the drugstore bottle, sold as a powder you store next to the detergent.

Three census lessons, each load-bearing. **First, the strongest number on the shelf is the one nobody prints.** The percarbonate box and the permanganate jar both outrank the liquid bottles by an order of magnitude; the shelf's *danger* ranking and its *label* ranking are unrelated, which is Chapter 28's recognition lesson stated as a shopping observation. **Second, every liquid oxidizer is a decomposition waiting to be dated.** Peroxide decays to water and oxygen on a shelf-life clock; hypochlorite decays on a faster one — and the assay discipline of Chapter 15 transfers whole: the dated titration is the only sentence in the system that knows what the jug holds *today*. **Third, the two solid "polite" oxidizers are the network's future reagents.** The percarbonate is the peroxide source that never needs concentrating (dissolve, and the soda ash is a spectator Chapter 14 already owns); the oxone bucket (the triple salt 2KHSO₅·KHSO₄·K₂SO₄, 49.5% peroxymonosulfate by mass, computed) is the solid *acid* oxidizer — and its peroxymonosulfate is exactly the species Chapter 15's audit-only ceiling (9.26% H₂SO₅, the Caro door) priced from the other side: **the shelf sells, pre-made and stable, what the from-scratch route walls at 9.26% — the oxidizer chapters' first inherited flag, honored here by buying instead of making, and the door itself is priced at audit level in Lab 16.2's B6.**

## Lecture 1 — The oxidizer census: reading labels that were written to hide the assay

**How you could have found it.** Buy one bottle of each family, read the label, and try to answer the only question that matters at the bench: *how many moles am I holding?* The label will not say. The oxidizer shelf is sold in four mutually incompatible conventions, and the first skill of this chapter is translating all four into the one currency the bench speaks — molarity, by way of the three-way arithmetic of Chapter 4 and Lab 15.1: **C = 10·wt%·ρ/M**.

**Convention one: weight percent.** The peroxide bottle (3% w/w) and the bleach jug (5.25% or 8.25% sodium hypochlorite) sell by mass of active ingredient per mass of product. Straightforward — except the bleach jug's ρ is its own quiet number (tabled: 1.093 g/mL at 5% solution, rising to 1.21 at 14%), and the label prints neither the density nor the date that would make the number true.

**Convention two: "available chlorine."** The pool shelf sells hypochlorite products by the mass of Cl₂ that would do the same oxidizing — a convention from the water-treatment industry, where what matters is oxidizing equivalents, not chemical identity. The conversion is one honest multiplication: a mole of NaOCl carries the oxidizing work of a mole of Cl₂, so the factor is 70.906/74.442 = 0.9525 — and the elegant accident of the regular jug is that **5.25% sodium hypochlorite *is* 5.00% available chlorine, which is why both numbers appear on both labels and generations of homeowners have assumed they were the same thing**. The trap runs the other way at the pool store: calcium hypochlorite products quote available chlorine *directly* (65–70% for the granular shock class) — and the pure salt's own ceiling is 2×70.906/142.982 = 99.2% available chlorine, so the pool label's number is a *product assay*, not a conversion, and the label's remainder is water, calcium chloride, and the salt's own slow decay (Lecture 5).

**Convention three: "volumes."** The beauty-supply developer sells peroxide in *volumes of oxygen* — the volume of O₂ (at STP, the 22.414 L/mol of Chapter 10's table, not the 24.5 working pin) released per volume of reagent, a convention from the bleaching trade. The honest arithmetic runs 10×wt%·ρ/M, halved for the O₂, times 22.414: the 3% bottle computes to 9.98 ≈ **10 volume**, the 6% developer to 20.2 ≈ **20 volume**, the 12% to 41.1 ≈ **40 volume** — the label classes are the STP convention rounded, and the drafting session's own engine initially ran the chain on the book's RTP pin and read 10.9 for the 10-volume bottle: the catch is printed as found, because the conventions are the lesson (Lab 16.1 Run 5).

**Convention four: the purple crystal's none at all.** Potassium permanganate is sold by the pound with no assay convention whatsoever — 99% class crystals, and the bench makes its own solutions and standardizes them itself (Lecture 4: the self-indicating titration). The strongest bottle on the household shelf is also the only one sold without a number.

**The census itself, in the bench's currency.** Run every label through the composition engine (the same three-way arithmetic, all masses from the held element table — Lab 16.1, executed and verbatim below): the regular jug is 0.769 M sodium hypochlorite; the concentrated jug 1.258 M (on the two tabled density datums, interpolated); the 3% peroxide bottle 0.891 M; the 40-volume developer 3.669 M; and the quietest label in the house — the laundry booster's cocrystal 2Na₂CO₃·3H₂O₂ — computes to **32.50% hydrogen peroxide by mass**, ten times the drugstore bottle, sold as a powder you store next to the detergent.

Three census lessons, each load-bearing. **First, the strongest number on the shelf is the one nobody prints.** The percarbonate box and the permanganate jar both outrank the liquid bottles by an order of magnitude; the shelf's *danger* ranking and its *label* ranking are unrelated, which is Chapter 28's recognition lesson stated as a shopping observation. **Second, every liquid oxidizer is a decomposition waiting to be dated.** Peroxide decays to water and oxygen on a shelf-life clock; hypochlorite decays on a faster one — and the assay discipline of Chapter 15 transfers whole: the dated titration is the only sentence in the system that knows what the jug holds *today*. **Third, the two solid "polite" oxidizers are the network's future reagents.** The percarbonate is the peroxide source that never needs concentrating (dissolve, and the soda ash is a spectator Chapter 14 already owns); the oxone bucket (the triple salt 2KHSO₅·KHSO₄·K₂SO₄, 49.5% peroxymonosulfate by mass, computed) is the solid *acid* oxidizer — and its peroxymonosulfate is exactly the species Chapter 15's audit-only ceiling (9.26% H₂SO₅, the Caro door) priced from the other side: **the shelf sells, pre-made and stable, what the from-scratch route walls at 9.26% — the oxidizer chapters' first inherited flag, honored here by buying instead of making, and the door itself is priced at audit level in Lab 16.2's B6.**

Straightforward — except the bleach jug's ρ is its own quiet number (tabled: 1.093 g/mL at 5% solution, rising to 1.21 at 14%), and the label prints neither the density nor the date that would make the number true.

**Convention two: "available chlorine."** The pool shelf sells hypochlorite products by the mass of Cl₂ that would do the same oxidizing — a convention from the water-treatment industry, where what matters is oxidizing equivalents, not chemical identity. The conversion is one honest multiplication: a mole of NaOCl carries the oxidizing work of a mole of Cl₂, so the factor is 70.906/74.442 = 0.9525 — and the elegant accident of the regular jug is that **5.25% sodium hypochlorite *is* 5.00% available chlorine, which is why both numbers appear on both labels and generations of homeowners have assumed they were the same thing**. The trap runs the other way at the pool store: calcium hypochlorite products quote available chlorine *directly* (65–70% for the granular shock class) — and the pure salt's own ceiling is 2×70.906/142.982 = 99.2% available chlorine, so the pool label's number is a *product assay*, not a conversion, and the label's remainder is water, calcium chloride, and the salt's own slow decay (Lecture 5).

**Convention three: "volumes."** The beauty-supply developer sells peroxide in *volumes of oxygen* — the volume of O₂ (at STP, the 22.414 L/mol of Chapter 10's table, not the 24.5 working pin) released per volume of reagent, a convention from the bleaching trade.
