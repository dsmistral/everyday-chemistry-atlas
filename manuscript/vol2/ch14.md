# Vol II · Chapter 14 — Alkalis and Carbonates: The Caustic Ladder

Volume I built the lab and its instruments: safety, glassware, solutions, the separation train, fire, pH, the precipitation network, gases, redox, electrochemistry, and finally the wet scheme that names an unknown powder with nothing but a drop counter and a plan. Volume II is why all of that machinery exists. **This is the reagent network: the volume in which the lab stops *identifying* what the world hands it and starts *building* what it needs.** And the network, like every real network, has load-bearing stones — the nodes from which most of the edges leave. The alkalis are the first of them. Nearly every synthesis later in this volume begins with one of six white powders: baking soda, washing soda, lye, limestone, quicklime, slaked lime — plus potassium's quieter branch of the same family — and nearly every one of those powders can be bought at a supermarket or hardware store, or made from another of them in a kitchen oven or a soup pot. The corpus has been holding this chapter's raw material since the survey's third batch: the full lime cycle burned, slaked, and re-carbonated in a homemade kiln (N18); the baking soda converted to washing soda in a kitchen oven with a before-and-after weighing as the entire assay (N19); the carbonate hub performed twice over as the byproduct-clean building block that turns copper sulfate into copper carbonate (N59 → N58); the drain-opener shelf as the honest label class of 100% sodium hydroxide crystals; and the zincate bath of Chapter 12 as proof that a home chemist's sodium hydroxide is a genuine reagent, not a cleaner (N76). **The thesis of this chapter, and of Volume II: the strong bases are not bought and not found — they are *stepped up to*, rung by rung, along a ladder of carbonates that the nineteenth century climbed with nothing but fire, water, and limestone; and the ladder's driving force is not a bigger furnace but a single subtraction of two solubility equilibria.** The chapter's five questions, in bench order: **what counts as an alkali at the shelf, how does the carbonate ladder run up (and down), how does the lime cycle turn a rock into a base and back, how is a mild base made caustic — and how do you keep what you have made?**

## Lecture 0 — The oven on the shelf, before any notation

Close the notebook. Take a box of baking soda from the kitchen and an ordinary sheet pan. Weigh out 100 grams of the powder — the kitchen scale will do; write the number on the pan's edge with a grease pencil. Put it in the oven at 200 °F (about 93 °C) and wait fifteen minutes. Weigh again: nothing has happened, and the recipe on the box promised as much — baking soda *is* the recipe's stability. Now push the oven to 400 °F and give it fifteen more minutes. Weigh a third time: the pan has lost about **37 grams**. One hundred went in; sixty-three came out. The powder that remains is not baking soda anymore — it tastes sharply soapy where baking soda tasted flat, it turns phenolphthalein pink where baking soda barely blushes it, and it strips grease from a frying pan that baking soda only coated. Something has been *made* — in a kitchen oven, from a grocery-store box, with no apparatus beyond a scale and a sheet pan — and the proof of the making was written by the scale itself, before any chemistry was invoked at all.

That is the whole of this chapter in one pan, and it is worth naming what the weighing proved before a single formula appears. First: **the missing 37 grams went somewhere.** Matter does not evaporate in the moral sense; two invisible vapors left — one you can breath-test into limewater (Chapter 10's cloud), one that fogs a cold glass held over the warm pan. Second: **the loss is a fixed fraction**, not an accident. Run the pan again with 50 grams and the loss will be about 18½ grams; run it with a full box and the loss scales. A fixed fraction of mass lost on heating is a *stoichiometry speaking before we know its grammar* — the signature of a decomposition, not a drying. Third: the product is *more alkaline than the reactant*, which is the strange part. Usually fire makes things less reactive, not more. Here heat has made the powder stronger — and that inversion is the door into Volume II. Nothing in this paragraph needed a mole, a formula, or a pH meter. The 37 grams needed only a scale and the honesty to weigh before and after. (The oven program, both programs, and the fifteen-minute negative control at 200 °F are the corpus's own experiment, run exactly this way: N19, whose two temperature programs land on the same product, and whose method is the mass loss itself.)

Now the shelf census, still without notation. Walk the household and collect the white powders that claim to clean, deodorize, de-scale, or unclog: the yellow box of baking soda; the larger box of washing soda (same store, different aisle, "laundry booster"); the crystal drain opener whose label says *sodium hydroxide* and whose label's warnings are the harshest on the shelf; the garden lime, of which the shelf quietly sells *two different chemicals under one name* — stone lime and hydrated lime, a distinction this chapter will make-load-bearing; the antacid tablets; the chalky water-softener salt. Six white powders, one hardware store, and — as we will spend this chapter showing — one small family. **Baking soda, washing soda, and lye are three strengths of the same base's ladder; limestone, quicklime, and slaked lime are the same stone at three temperatures. The two ladders meet in the middle, and the meeting is the single most consequential reaction in the history of household chemistry.**

## Lecture 1 — The alkali census: reading the shelf as a reagent catalog

### 1.1 What "alkali" has meant, and what it means at this bench

The word arrived before the theory. *Al-qaly* — the roasted ashes — was what the Arab chemists called the water-soluble, acid-neutralizing, grease-cutting residue of burned plants, and the word survived every theory change since: Arrhenius's hydroxide-releasing bases, Brønsted's proton acceptors, and the everyday operational test this bench uses, which Chapter 8 already built. An **alkali** at this lab is a substance whose water solution carries a high concentration of hydroxide (or of a species that makes it: carbonate's second bite, Chapter 8's washing-soda rung). The three questions of the census are the P1 questions — *read the label* (pattern **P1**, the sourcing pattern): what is the active ingredient, at what assay, at what price, in what physical form — and the P10 question that rides with them: *make or buy?* (pattern **P10**, route economics: score routes by availability, apparatus demand, hazard, yield, and separability of byproducts).

The census itself, run honestly:

| Shelf item | The actual chemical | Assay class | The bench verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Baking soda | NaHCO₃ (sodium bicarbonate) | Food grade, ~100% | Buy: it is the cheapest pure chemical in any house |
| Washing soda | Na₂CO₃ (sodium carbonate; the crystal box is the decahydrate Na₂CO₃·10H₂O) | "Laundry" grade; the box's crystal is 37% carbonate by mass | Buy or bake: one oven run converts baking soda (L0's 63%) |
| Crystal drain opener | NaOH (sodium hydroxide, "lye," "caustic soda") | Label class: **100% sodium hydroxide** (Roebic, Rooto, Instant Power and their shelf-mates all print it) | Buy: the 100%-lye class is the cleanest OTC strong base in the modern world; **never** the liquid drain openers, which are other chemicals entirely |
| Garden lime, bag 1 ("limestone," "calcitic lime") | CaCO₃ (calcium carbonate) | Impure stone: 90-98% | Buy only as lime-cycle feedstock |
| Garden lime, bag 2 ("hydrated lime," "slaked lime") | Ca(OH)₂ (calcium hydroxide) | ~90-95% | Buy or slake your own (Lecture 3) |
| Wood ash (the fireplace's gift) | Mostly K₂CO₃ + insolubles | Wildly variable | Make: the potash leach is the chapter's one from-nothing extraction |
| Antacid tablets (CaCO₃ class) | CaCO₃ + binders | Pharmaceutical | Knowledge source only: binder-laden, and the medicine cabinet is not a reagent supplier |

Three census lessons, each load-bearing for the volume. **First, the two-lime trap:** the hardware shelf sells CaCO₃ and Ca(OH)₂ under the near-identical word "lime," and they are *different rungs of a ladder* — a confusion that in Lecture 3 will mean the difference between a cool suspension and a boiling one (P1's discipline is not pedantry; it is burn prevention). **Second, the assay convention:** alkalis are not bought and sold on percent-of-powder but on *alkalinity as Na₂O* — soda ash is assayed at **58.48% as Na₂O** (61.979 g of Na₂O per 105.988 g of Na₂CO₃), lye at **77.48% as Na₂O** (61.979 g per 79.994 g of NaOH), and potash at **68.16% as K₂O**. The convention looks archaic and is in fact the industry's common currency: it prices *neutralizing power*, so that one assay number compares carbonate, hydroxide, and crystal hydrate on one scale (Lab 14.1's assay battery derives each). For the home lab the convention pays an immediate dividend — it explains why the label arithmetic of a washing-soda recipe never matches the box: the crystal you weigh is only **37.04% Na₂CO₃ by mass**; the other 62.96% is water of crystallization that the assay counts as nothing. **Third, the make-or-buy ladder is already visible in the price column:** baking soda is cheap, washing soda is cheaper per unit of alkalinity, lye is cheapest of all per mole — *when bought as the 100% crystal class*. The rest of this chapter is the answer to the question the census provokes: what can you do when the shelf is empty — when there is no drain-opener aisle and no hardware store, only a kitchen, a fireplace, and the know-how?

### 1.2 The founding economics: why this chapter exists at all

Run the P10 scoring on "a strong base in a ruined world," and the routes fall into a startlingly short list. You can **buy** hydroxide (the drain-opener class) — availability score high, apparatus zero, hazard moderate, and the byproblem is none. You can **electrolyze** brine for NaOH — but Chapter 12 already established the corpus's honest boundary there: the video corpus contains *no* successful bench alkali-by-electrolysis (N78's sodium, N100's lithium, N105's cesium are all displacement routes; the chlor-alkali cell is industrial knowledge-only, and its chlorine co-product is exactly the hazard Chapter 28 exists to teach you to respect). Or you can do what every alkali from 1800 to 1920 did: **climb the carbonate ladder and then trade lime for caustic** — a two-step route whose feedstocks are baking soda (or the fireplace's own potash) and a rock, whose apparatus is an oven and a jar, whose only byproduct is the limestone you started with, recoverable for the next batch. That last clause is not a bonus; it is the historical reason the route won. When the day's work is done, the CaCO₃ you filtered out is calcined back to CaO and slaked back to Ca(OH)₂ for tomorrow — the lime cycle closes, and the only true consumables are fuel and the carbonate feed. The paper mills of the Kraft process run this exact loop at ten-thousand-times scale, and the survey's register pinned their own honest number: **80-83% causticizing efficiency in practice** against the ideal model's 99.7%+ — a gap this chapter will derive, not wave away.

And the route's economy explains a century of history in two sentences, printed here as knowledge-only context (P10's census exemplar, the register's Leblanc-vs-Solvay datum). The world's alkali appetite was fed first by burning barilla plants and leaching wood ash (the potash economy — Lecture 4), then by the Leblanc process (salt + sulfuric acid + coal + limestone, a filthy route whose byproducts were the birth of the chemical-waste problem), then by Solvay's 1861 ammonia loop, whose equations S1-S3 of Lab 14.1 are balanced in this chapter's ledger because their *shape* — NaCl in, NaHCO₃ out, NH₃ recovered by lime — is the caustic ladder wearing an industrial suit. The Atlas never benches Solvay (its ammonia handling belongs to Chapter 20, its economics to a world with tanker trucks); but the home route in Lecture 4 is Solvay's step *without* the ammonia crutch, and the reason the home route works at all is the subject of the next two lectures: carbonates that step up on heating, and a lime cycle that never runs out of stone.

## Lecture 2 — The carbonate ladder: one anion, five strengths

### 2.1 The rungs

Chapter 8 gave carbonate its pH engine; this lecture gives it a *staircase*. The carbonate anion CO₃²⁻ is the strongest base the kitchen can buy at food-grade purity, and the family's members differ only in what stands beside the CO₃²⁻ and in how many waters tag along. The ladder, bottom to top, with the rung-spacings as reactions (all in Lab 14.1's balance ledger):

- **NaHCO₃ — bicarbonate.** The bottom rung: the amphiprotic compromise, pH 8.35 in solution (Chapter 8's engine, reproduced in Lab 14.1's cross-pin: pH = (pKa₁ + pKa₂)/2 = (6.367 + 10.328)/2). Its alkalinity is real but half-spent — the anion is its own conjugate acid's partner, which is exactly why it belongs in food.
- **Na₂CO₃ — carbonate.** One oven run up (E1: 2 NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O). pH of a 0.10 M solution: 11.66 by the shortcut, 11.65 by the quadratic — Chapter 8's RA-1 pair, re-derived this session. **This is the rung the oven reaches.** N19's two programs — low-and-slow (about an hour at 200 °F) and hot-and-fast (400 °F, fifteen minutes) — land on the same product, and the register's honest negative control is a lesson in itself: fifteen minutes at 200 °F is *not enough*, and the insufficient run shows in the mass (the pan that hasn't lost its 37% is a pan of unconverted bicarb, and the scale catches the lie).
- **NaOH — hydroxide.** Two rungs above the bicarbonate bottom — one above carbonate — and the oven cannot get there: heating sodium carbonate to Na₂O means the E4 rung at **851 °C** (CRC's melting point for soda ash; some tables print it as the decomposition point, and thermodynamic sources say the melt survives and must be driven hotter still — every reading is kiln territory, none is kitchen territory). The route from carbonate to hydroxide is not thermal at all; it is the Lecture-4 trade.
- **The hydrate sub-ladder.** Na₂CO₃ itself is a staircase of waters: **decahydrate → monohydrate → anhydrous** (E2, E3), and the rungs are bench-observable. The washing-soda box's crystals *effloresce* — the PubChem datum prints it plainly: the decahydrate "dries out somewhat in warm, dry air above 50 °C, becomes anhydrous at 100 °C," and on a dry shelf it drifts to the monohydrate, losing 9 of its 10 waters (E2; 286.138 g → 124.003 g per mole — a 56.7% mass loss that the unwary bench mistakes for a failed assay). The drift is not a defect; it is a **free assay clock**: weigh the box when you buy it and again when you use it, and the two weighings tell you which hydrate you now own. Chapter 8's RA-5 lie-ledger generalized: never price a hydrate on an anhydrous recipe's arithmetic. The decahydrate is 37.04% Na₂CO₃; a recipe that says "105.99 g of sodium carbonate" wants 286 g of the crystal — and the 100.35 g of crystal you actually weighed delivers only 37% of the carbonate the label arithmetic promised (Lab 14.1's assay trap line, computed).

### 2.2 The solubility staircase and the separation train

The ladder's members refuse to dissolve to the same degree, and the differences are the separation train's whole menu (pattern **P2**, Chapter 5's train, re-run on the alkali row of the solubility table):

| Species | g per 100 g water, 20 °C | The train's use of it |
|---|---|---|
| NaHCO₃ | 9.6 (16.4 at 60 °C) | The odd one: *cooling* crystallizes it — the reverse of the usual gradient |
| Na₂CO₃ | 21.5 (CRC's series: 6/8.5/17/28 wt% at 0/10/20/30 °C — converts to 6.4/9.3/20.5/38.9 g per 100 g water; the two table families disagree by ~5% at 20 °C, the spread named, not laundered) | Evaporation crystallizes the **decahydrate** from cool solution; boiling-hot solution gives the monohydrate — the rung you get depends on the temperature you filter at |
| K₂CO₃ | 112 | So soluble it never crystallizes easily at bench; deliquescent — it *gains* water on the shelf |
| NaOH / KOH | 111 / 112 | Deliquescent: they will not crystallize; they concentrate and then wet themselves — storage is Lecture 5's subject |
| Ca(OH)₂ | 0.17 (1.73 g/L) | Almost insoluble — which is not a defect but the Lecture-4 driving force itself |
| CaCO₃ | ~0.0012 (measured; the Ksp-derived ideal is ~0.0006 — the measured lift is the limewater seam's own class again) | The floor: effectively zero. The most important zero in this volume |

Two consequences worth the table's ink. **First, the bicarbonate gradient inversion:** nearly every salt climbs in solubility as it warms; sodium bicarbonate's modest ladder (9.6 → 16.4 g between 20 and 60 °C) still lets *cold* water preferentially drop it out of a mixed solution — the one alkali you can crystallize by cooling, which is why the N59 carbonate-hub protocol (below) uses *hot* concentrated NaHCO₃-slurry conditions and accepts the basic salt. **Second, the zero at the bottom:** CaCO₃'s effective insolubility is why Lecture 4 works. Every synthesis in this volume that needs "a carbonate that isn't sodium's" leans on the same trick in reverse — carbonate is a *hub*, and the hub's spokes are exactly Chapter 9's solubility rules.

### 2.3 The carbonate hub, performed twice

Chapter 9 named the design; this chapter is its first Volume-II re-run, and the corpus performed it twice for us. Thompson's *Chemicals on the Cheap* (N30) states the hub's principle: **carbonates are byproduct-clean building blocks** — a carbonate plus any acid gives the salt plus water plus CO₂, and a carbonate plus any soluble metal salt drops the new metal's carbonate in one step, with a byproduct that is nearly always a harmless sodium or potassium salt you can filter off and keep. The performance: the Home Scientist's root-killer-and-baking-soda episode (N59) runs 100.35 g of copper sulfate pentahydrate against 67.99 g of baking soda in water, and collects a green-blue carbonate ore — the idealized metathesis of Lab 14.1's ledger (CuSO₄ + Na₂CO₃ → CuCO₃ + Na₂SO₄), with Chapter 9's honesty note already pinned to it: the real precipitate is the *basic* carbonate, CuCO₃·Cu(OH)₂-class, because carbonate's water is a base too (the anion's own hydrolysis eats some of the bicarbonate into hydroxide before the copper can organize a pure lattice). The follow-up episode (N58) then walks the product up the hub's other spokes — carbonate to acetate with vinegar, to chloride with the purified hydrochloric acid of N46 — and the series' own flagship self-correction lives exactly here: **100.35 g of CuSO₄·5H₂O should give 49.4 g of carbonate on the performer's own arithmetic (0.40 mol at 2 s.f. — the exact engine gives 49.66 g, the Ch 1 RA-3 reconstruction); the performer found 47 g and attributed the miss to mechanical loss — 95% on either basis, honestly counted** (the P12 pattern at full strength, Chapter 1's pin). The filtrate, Chapter 9's ledger already counted: **57.09 g of Na₂SO₄** — one metathesis's "waste" as another chapter's feedstock (Chapter 15 will want exactly that salt for the P14 oxalate face's byproduct). And the episode's own absurdity datum — that dissolving the day's carbonate crop in pure water would want the swimming pool's volume — is the solubility table's bottom row doing its work: the carbonate you made is a solid *because it hates water*, which is precisely why you could make it.

The hub's generality is the volume's real news. Want copper hydroxide instead? Hydroxide is just carbonate's water after one more bite (Ch 9's route). Want zinc carbonate, iron carbonate, magnesium carbonate? Same swap, same filtrate class, same solubility rules. The carbonate hub is the first genuinely *general* synthesis tool the Atlas has taught — P3 (metathesis-precipitation) fired from a feedstock the supermarket sells at food grade — and every remaining alkali in this chapter is upstream of it.

## Lecture 3 — The lime cycle: the oldest chemical technology, run at home

### 3.1 The cycle

If the carbonate ladder is Volume II's staircase, the lime cycle is its foundation stone — and it is *old*. Not "Victorian" old: kilned lime predates pottery glaze, with production evidence at **7000 BCE** [convergent]. The cycle is four reactions and a kiln, and the corpus runs it whole: the Primitive Technology episode (N18) burns shells in a homemade kiln to quicklime, slakes it to putty, and sets it back to stone with the fire's own exhaust — the full cycle, performed with materials gathered in one afternoon. The reactions (E5-E8 of the ledger, all machine-balanced this session):

**Calcination.** CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (E5). The stone gives up its carbon dioxide and loses 43.97% of its mass (56.03% remains as "available CaO" — the lime industry's own assay basis, Lab 14.1's percent battery). The temperature is the cycle's only genuine hardship: the decomposition pressure of CaCO₃ reaches one atmosphere of CO₂ around **840-900 °C**, so the fire must be *able* to hold CO₂ at near-zero partial pressure at that heat — N18's kiln datum is the shells calcining "above ~840 °C," and the home kiln class (charcoal-fired, draft-fed) just reaches it. This is the hardest thermal ask in the book so far — harder than the oven, gentler than Chapter 30's ceramics project, which is why it sits here as a *knowledge-plus episode* rather than a required bench: **watch the corpus do it, and understand the number, before you ever feed a kiln.** The kiln's exhaust, in honest accounting, is the process's own reagent later in the cycle: the lime industry's arithmetic prints **785 kg of CO₂ per tonne of quicklime** (Lab 14.2: 0.7848 t — the industry figure is the stoichiometry to the kilogram), and the carbonation step drinks it back.

**Slaking.** CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ (E6). The most dramatic exotherm on the household bench: ΔH ≈ **−64.8 kJ/mol** [convergent band −63.7 to −65.2 across the tables; printed as a band, honestly]. Lab 14.2's small case: 10.0 g of quicklime into 100 g of water raises it 25.1 K — a third of the way from room temperature to boiling, from ten grams of powder, *with no fuel*. The water demand is only 32.1% of the quicklime's mass, which is the practical warning in disguise: slake into *excess* water (the traditional "putty" protocol — weeks in a covered crock) and the exotherm is tamed; slake with the stoichiometric minimum and the same enthalpy has nowhere to go but temperature — the historical accidents all live there. The reverse rung exists and is the cycle's own brake: Ca(OH)₂ → CaO + H₂O (E7) when the water over it reaches one atmosphere at **512 °C** — slaked lime that wanders into a fire gives its water back as steam, and the cycle's reversibility is complete.

**Limewater.** The slaked solid dissolves to almost nothing and to exactly enough: 1.73 g/L at 20 °C per the shelf tables [convergent] — which, worked as an ideal solubility product (Ksp 5.5×10⁻⁶, Ksp battery of Lab 14.1), should be only 0.824 g/L. **Print the seam honestly, because it is the book's best seam yet.** The concentration product at the tabulated solubility is 5.09×10⁻⁵ — 9.3× the tabulated Ksp; the Davies activity estimate at the resulting ionic strength (I = 0.070 M; γ(Ca²⁺) ≈ 0.41, γ(OH⁻) ≈ 0.80) brings the activity product to 1.35×10⁻⁵, closing 3.8× of the gap; the residual 2.5× is named to ion pairing (CaOH⁺) and the tables' own Ksp spread (5.02-5.5×10⁻⁶) — a **[flagged sketch]**, not laundered into convergence. The bench statement is a band: saturated limewater is ~0.8-1.7 g/L of Ca(OH)₂ and **pH ≈ 12.4** [convergent practice figure]; the ideal engine's 12.35 and the total-solubility figure's 12.67 bracket it. What is *not* in dispute is limewater's role: it is the lab's cheapest strong-ish base, its cleanest Ca²⁺ source, and its most sensitive CO₂ detector — Chapter 10's cloud and Chapter 13's fizz both live here.

**Carbonation.** Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O (E8). The mortar sets by it; the limewater clouds by it; the cycle closes by it. Excess CO₂ bites twice — E9 (CaCO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → Ca(HCO₃)₂) *dissolves* the precipitate back, which is the register's "milkiness disappearing" datum and, at geological scale, the reason caves exist: **hardness is the second bite of a base that met too much acid-anhydride.** Boil the water and E10 runs it back out — Ca(HCO₃)₂ → CaCO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O — which is Chapter 4's kettle-scale datum, now with its full mechanism attached.

### 3.2 The cycle as a mass ledger

Run the cycle at 100.0 g of limestone (Lab 14.2's verbatim ledger): calcine to 56.03 g of quicklime (venting 43.97 g of CO₂); slake with 18.00 g of water to 74.03 g of slaked lime; dissolve it into 42.8 L of limewater at the shelf solubility (or 89.8 L at the ideal-model figure — the seam's two answers, both printed); then carbonation, with the kiln's own exhaust as the feed, returns the full 100.0 g. **The round-trip closes to the milligram** because the calcium atom never leaves: 100.0 → 56.03 → 74.03 → 100.0. And this closure is not bookkeeping trivia — it is the industrial design. The Kraft paper cycle calcines its waste CaCO₃ ("lime mud") back to CaO and slakes it back to Ca(OH)₂ in a closed loop, buying makeup lime only for *mechanical* losses; the atom ledger says the process owes nature nothing but fuel. The mortar in a Roman wall is the same trick at rest: the lime putty took CO₂ back from the air for years, and the wall's carbonate is the stone it came from, minus the labor. When you set a jar of limewater on the windowsill and watch it cloud, you are watching the oldest closed-loop chemical technology on Earth — and the cloud at the bottom is the limestone you would have started with.

### 3.3 What the cycle buys this lab

Three reagents, one episode of pyrotechnics you watched rather than performed: **quicklime** (a drying agent stronger than any desiccant the book has yet catalogued — Chapter 6's drying-agent line named, Chapter 15's desiccant ladder still ahead — and a Lecture-4 feedstock), **slaked lime** (the suspension form — the cheapest base per kilogram in any hardware store), and **limewater** (the clear solution — a base, a calcium source, and a CO₂ sensor). The ladder's economics (P10 again): a 20-kg bag of garden hydrated lime out-alkalis, per dollar, every other base on the shelf — but at Lecture 4's exchange rate, not at hydroxide strength. To make the cheap mild base into the expensive strong one is the next lecture's single move — and the move is a subtraction.

## Lecture 4 — The caustic ladder: trading mild for strong

### 4.1 The trade

Here is the nineteenth century's whole trick, and it is one sentence: **sodium carbonate meets calcium hydroxide, and the calcium leaves.**

Na₂CO₃ + Ca(OH)₂ → 2 NaOH + CaCO₃ (E11)

On the solubility table this looks like an exchange of equals — carbonate for hydroxide, calcium for sodium. But run the solubilities and the trade is not even: CaCO₃ is *effectively insoluble* (Ksp 3.36×10⁻⁹, Chapter 4/9's pin) while both reactants are soluble-ish and both products-but-one are soluble. The precipitate is the engine. Every mole of CO₃²⁻ that drags a Ca²⁺ out of solution forces the Ca(OH)₂ to dissolve to replace it, and every Ca²⁺ that dissolves releases *two hydroxides* — the mild base's water is being strip-mined for its OH⁻, and the strip-miner is a rock that will not stay dissolved. This is pattern **P3** (metathesis-precipitation) at its highest leverage: not a route to a salt, but a route to a *base class change*.

### 4.2 How you could have found the driving force yourself: the subtraction

The chapter's showpiece derivation, and the one Lab 14.1 executes: write the two solubility equilibria side by side, with both solids sitting on the bench together.

Ca(OH)₂(s) ⇌ Ca²⁺ + 2 OH⁻  Ksp₁ = [Ca²⁺][OH⁻]²
CaCO₃(s) ⇌ Ca²⁺ + CO₃²⁻  Ksp₂ = [Ca²⁺][CO₃²⁻]

Subtract the second from the first and the calcium cancels — one dissolution minus another is the *reaction*: CO₃²⁻ + Ca(OH)₂(s) → CaCO₃(s) + 2 OH⁻, and its equilibrium constant is the ratio:

**K = Ksp₁/Ksp₂ = [OH⁻]²/[CO₃²⁻] = 5.5×10⁻⁶ / 3.36×10⁻⁹ = 1637**

(On the Wikipedia Ksp variant 5.02×10⁻⁶ it prints 1494 — the sensitivity is named, the conclusion is not in doubt: three orders of magnitude either way, log₁₀ K = 3.21 on the tabulated pair and 3.17 on the variant.) The constant says the bench will hold hydroxide-squared over carbonate at better than three orders of magnitude of pressure: **the reaction is not "driven" by any mystical causticity of lime — it is two Ksp values divided, and the quotient does the shoving.** Chapter 9's gypsum seam taught the ideal model's honesty; this is the ideal model at its most glorious, and Lab 14.1's ladder climbs it: from 1.00 M sodium carbonate, the equilibrium leaves 99.76% converted, [OH⁻] = 1.995 M with a residual carbonate of only 2.4×10⁻³ M; at 0.100 M it is 99.98%; at 0.010 M the model rounds to complete. **The ideal causticizing ceiling is ~2 M — and the number is not a furnace's boast but an equilibrium's.** Push past it (evaporate the product water, as the industry does) and the residual carbonate grows with the concentration until the quotient bites back: the ladder tops out where [OH⁻]²/[CO₃²⁻] = 1637 says it does.

### 4.3 The honest gap

The Kraft industry measures **80-83% causticizing efficiency** in its agitated tanks [convergent], against the ideal model's 99.7%+. The gap is printed, not waved: the model runs on concentrations; the plant runs on activities in a liquor already carrying ~1 M each of hydroxide, sulfide, and sulfate (the Davies lesson of the limewater seam, at ten times the ionic strength); the CaCO₃ precipitate nucleates as fine mud that carries carbonate down *before* equilibrium finishes; and the residence time in the causticizer is hours, not geological. For the home bench the honest prediction is the band: **expect 90-99% conversion on a quiet overnight stir with excess slaked lime, and *filter well*.** The bench's advantage over the plant is patience; its disadvantage is the filter. (The lab's own batch ledger, Lab 14.2 B1, prices a 100 g batch at 99.77% ideal conversion, [NaOH] 1.883 M with 2.2×10⁻³ M carbonate residual.)

### 4.4 Caustic potash: the fireplace's alkali

Run the same trade with the potassium branch and you have the oldest alkali business of all: **potash.** Leach wood ash with water; the soluble part is mostly K₂CO₃ (plus whatever sodium came along for the ride, plus sulfate and phosphate traces), while the insoluble spent ash — calcium carbonate and charcoal — stays on the filter and is neither product. Evaporate the leachate to dryness and the product is **potash**, the crude potassium carbonate of the entire pre-industrial world; mildly calcine it ("ignite," the old books say — baking in a kiln to burn off organics, the historic definition) and the fine white powder remaining is **pearl ash**, the *refined* grade: the soap of every civilization that had soap, the glass of every civilization that had glass, the object of the **first patent ever issued by the United States** (1790, Samuel Hopkins, "in the making of Pot ash and Pearl ash by a new Apparatus and Process," signed by George Washington [convergent]). Its chemistry at this bench is Lecture 2's carbonate chemistry wearing a K: deliquescent (112 g/100 g — it will not crystallize; it must be *evaporated*, and then kept sealed), strongly alkaline (the carbonate engine with potassium's flat solubility curve), and one E12-trade away from **KOH — caustic potash**: K₂CO₃ + Ca(OH)₂ → 2 KOH + CaCO₃, Lab 14.2's batch B2 at 100.0 g of K₂CO₃ giving 81.19 g of KOH from 53.61 g of slaked lime. (Why want KOH at all, when NaOH is cheaper? The ethanol-cleaning and soft-soap chapters ahead — Chapter 21 and 23 — will show potassium's soaps stay liquid where sodium's go hard; the KOH route rides on.) The historical route to *lye soap* is exactly this ladder: ash to pearlash, pearlash to caustic potash, caustic potash to fat — three steps from a fireplace to a bar of soap, and the register's Soapy Stuff source pinned the practice's own warnings (store ash airtight and dry; leach with hot water re-used until it bites; boil down in an iron or stainless pot — never aluminum, Lecture 5 will say why with an equation).

### 4.5 The two-endpoint problem: assaying an alkali that has met air

A strong base on the shelf is a chemical with a secret, and the secret has a date. Sodium hydroxide eats CO₂ from the air — first bite (E13): 2 NaOH + CO₂ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O; and if the CO₂ keeps coming, the over-bite (E14): NaOH + CO₂ → NaHCO₃. So the "100% lye" box that has been open a season is neither 100% nor lye, and *no single titration can tell you what it is* — because NaOH, Na₂CO₃, and NaHCO₃ all eat HCl, just in different installments. The assay that sorts them is the **two-endpoint titration** (the Warder method's classical shape, run here with the Chapter 8/13 toolkit): titrate to phenolphthalein's endpoint (pH ≈ 8.3) and the acid has neutralized *all* the NaOH and *half* of the carbonate (CO₃²⁻ → HCO₃⁻, the amphiprotic midpoint); titrate on to the methyl-orange endpoint (pH ≈ 4.3) and the rest of the story is consumed (HCO₃⁻ → H₂O + CO₂). Lab 14.2 runs the whole assay as its Warder chain: a 1.500 g sample of aged box-lye dissolves to 250.0 mL, a 25.00 mL aliquot takes 21.40 mL of 0.1000 M HCl to phenolphthalein and 23.85 mL to methyl orange — and the two volumes *are* the analysis: the tail between them (2.45 mL, doubled) is the carbonate, the difference from the tail (the rest) is the hydroxide. The ledger closes to the milligram: the box that said 100% now assays **50.5% NaOH, 34.6% Na₂CO₃, and 14.8% water** — the carbonate's own reaction water (0.0883 g) and the free moisture (0.1344 g) split by E13's own stoichiometry, and the absorbed CO₂ counted at 0.2156 g. This is P9 (instrumentless verification) at its most consequential: the two flasks and two indicators *price the shelf's honesty*, and Ex 14.4 hands you the tool for your own box.

### 4.6 The boundary: what this chapter does not do

State it plainly, because the volume opens here. **No bench chlor-alkali.** The route from salt water to NaOH via electrolysis is industrial knowledge-only in this book (Chapter 12's finding, restated as the volume's boundary): the co-produced Cl₂ is a hazard the home has no business releasing, and the corpus's own chlorine-generator episode (N77) performs the gas with an explicit do-not-attempt disclaimer — the boundary line is drawn by the practitioners themselves. What the home route *does* have is the displacement chemistry of Chapters 11-12 for the metals, and this chapter's trade for the alkalis — and the trade needs no boundary beyond the ones already drawn: slaked lime is caustic but weak; the product solution is dangerous but known; nothing here makes a gas, a pressure, or a flame.

## Lecture 5 — The alkali network and its keepers: CO₂, amphoterism, and the six-node map

### 5.1 Carbon dioxide: the slow enemy

Lecture 4's Warder assay told the story one box at a time; run the arithmetic on the shelf as a system and it is sobering. A liter of 1.00 M NaOH, left open, carbonates by E13 to the tune of half a mole of CO₂ — 22.0 g, **12.2 L of gas at room temperature** — and then, given enough air, doubles it by the over-bite to NaHCO₃: a full mole, 44.0 g, 24.5 L. The air's CO₂ is thin (420 ppm, about 0.017 mol/m³) but it is *free-flowing*: fully carbonating that liter means scrubbing the CO₂ from **58 m³ of air** — a small bedroom's worth, delivered one draft at a time. (This is not a curiosity; it is the air-capture chemistry's first step — the NaOH/calcium cycle of the scrubber literature is literally this chapter's E13 + E11 run in a loop [convergent].) The keepers' rules follow from the mechanism: **store alkalis as solids, tightly sealed; make solutions in the size you will use before the next season; label with a date; and when precision matters, re-assay by the two-endpoint method before trusting an old solution.** The carbonation of the bench is also a *tool*: a deliberate NaOH trap is how CO₂ is cleared from gas streams in half the corpus's apparatus diagrams, and the limewater jar on the windowsill is the household's slow carbonation meter — Lecture 3's cycle, running in reverse at 12.4 pH, one bubble at a time.

### 5.2 Amphoterism: what alkalis do to the two amphoteric metals

Aluminum and zinc are amphoteric (Chapter 11's finding), and a strong alkali proves it on them the violent way — the drain-opener warning with its mechanism:

2 Al + 2 NaOH + 6 H₂O → 2 NaAl(OH)₄ + 3 H₂ (E15)
Zn + 2 NaOH + 2 H₂O → Na₂Zn(OH)₄ + H₂ (E16)

The hydroxide ion is not the attacker; it is the *stabilizer*: Al dissolves because OH⁻ wraps it into the soluble aluminate, and Zn because OH⁻ wraps it into the zincate — Chapter 12's electrolytic zinc bath (N76) was exactly this dissolution run in reverse at a cathode, and the Canadian Chemist's own hazard datum rides with it (the plated zinc is **pyrophoric when dried**). Two rules and one mystery-story, all load-bearing. **Rule one:** never aluminum in a lye pot — the historical soap-boiler's iron kettle rule is E15 with a burn scar attached. **Rule two:** the drain is not a reactor — crystal lye plus water in a clogged, standing drain can localize heat and hydrogen (the product labels' own warnings, honored here as binding). **The mystery-story:** the *smell* of an aluminum-lye reaction is not hydrogen; it is the foam carrying trace amines and the scent of the alloy's own impurities — a reminder that P9's rule of convergence applies even to anecdotes. The forward flag to Chapter 28 (hazard recognition) is written here for the first time as a *mechanism*: the amphoterism that dissolves metal is the same amphoterism that makes drain openers work — the knowledge is one and the danger is one.

### 5.3 The two uses that preview the volume

**Superglue's accelerator.** The forensic bench of Chapter 13 fumed superglue in a sealed chamber for 24 patient hours — *or* accelerated it to minutes with an alkali pad (N66's Crystal drain-opener substitution): cyanoacrylate polymerizes on moisture and on basic initiation, and a wad of paper towel wet with sodium hydroxide solution turns the chamber into a base-catalyzed initiator farm. The alkalis' catalytic hand — base-catalyzed polymerization — will return in Chapter 35. **Soap.** Every alkali in this chapter ends in the same pot: fat + NaOH (hard soap) or fat + KOH (soft soap) — the saponification reaction, Chapter 23's summit, flagged from here because it is the historical *payoff* of the entire ladder: ash to potash to caustic to soap is the oldest synthetic-chemical chain our species runs, and the fireplace-to-bar-of-soap route of §4.4 is the Atlas's own ancestry. Chapter 21's solvents will want the same KOH; Chapter 33's cleaners are carbonate's modern empire; Chapter 30's glass is silica + the soda-ash + lime triangle (which is why Na₂CO₃ + CaCO₃ + SiO₂ sit in the same composition battery of Lab 14.1 — the soda-lime glass recipe is three nodes of this chapter's network).

### 5.4 The six-node map

Close the chapter the way Volume II will open its remaining chapters — with the network, drawn small:

NaHCO₃ --(oven, E1)--> Na₂CO₃ --(E11, with Ca(OH)₂)--> NaOH
   |                         ↑                          |
   |                    (E8 carbonation: CO₂ + Ca(OH)₂) |  (E13: CO₂, slowly — the aging)
   ↓                         ↓                          ↓
CaCO₃ --(kiln, E5)--> CaO --(E6, water)--> Ca(OH)₂ --(E11)--> CaCO₃ (the lime loop closes)

Six white solids, five arrows, four of them reversible, one of them (the kiln) expensive in fuel, one of them (E11) free because two Ksp values disagree by three orders of magnitude. Add the potassium branch (wood ash → K₂CO₃ → KOH) and the network is the spine of this volume — the tech tree that Chapter 36 will draw in full. Every later chapter's first reagent is a node here or one step downstream of one: the acids of Chapter 15 are neutralized by and *made from* these bases (P14's precipitation-driven acid liberation runs on carbonate chemistry); the oxidizers of Chapter 16 need the alkali to tame them; the metals of Chapter 19 fall out of solution as hydroxides at this chapter's pH; the ammonia of Chapter 20 is the gas the alkalis trade in; the glass of Chapter 30 is this network with sand added. **Learn the six nodes and you own the volume.**

## Exercises

Every graded exercise is solved two ways, each way named to its P-index pattern; hints precede the worked answers.

**14.1 ●○○ — The one-pound box.** A 454 g (1 lb) box of the 100%-lye class is dissolved, all at once, in 1.00 L of water. (a) How many moles? What molarity? (b) How much heat does the dissolution release, and what temperature rise would it cause in the water if none escaped? *(Hint: the dissolution exotherm ΔHsoln(NaOH) ≈ −44.5 kJ/mol — this chapter's own [convergent web] constant, printed in the safety table's row 2.)* **Ways.** Way 1 (P12, the mole ledger): 454/39.997 = 11.35 mol → 11.35 M; q = 11.35 × 44.5 = 505.1 kJ → ΔT = 505,100/(1000 × 4.184) = **120.7 K** — the water would boil, violently. Way 2 (the per-gram factor, same pattern different currency): 44.5/39.997 = 1.1126 kJ/g; 454 g × 1.1126 = 505.1 kJ — identical, and the factor form is the one to carry in the head: *lye pays 1.1 kJ per gram, always.* The safety conclusion is the answer's point: the box must go in *in portions* — the Chapter 1 scale-up rule with a number now attached to it.

**14.2 ●○○ — The oven run, priced.** 250 g of baking soda goes into the oven at the hot-and-fast program. (a) What mass of washing soda comes out? (b) What masses of what two gases leave? (c) The pan loses 88 g overnight in a second run; what is wrong? *(Hint: E1's stoichiometry is a fixed fraction — what fraction?)* **Ways.** Way 1 (P12, mole route): 250/84.006 = 2.976 mol → 1.488 mol Na₂CO₃ = **157.71 g**; off-gas: 1.488 mol CO₂ = 65.48 g and 1.488 mol H₂O = 26.81 g. Way 2 (the mass-fraction factor, P7's signature): Na₂CO₃ is 63.08% of the charge and the loss 36.92% splits 26.19% CO₂ + 10.72% H₂O — 250 × 0.6308 = 157.7 g, same answer, no moles needed. (c) 88 g > 92.29 g? No — *less* than the 92.29 g the full conversion demands, so the second run is *incomplete*: the oven never reached the program's temperature, and the scale caught the lie exactly as N19's protocol intends (the reverse case — losing *more* — would mean something else spilled or splattered).

**14.3 ●●○ — The shell kiln, from ash to assay.** A batch of 500 g of shells is 95% CaCO₃ by the calcite's own accounting. (a) What mass of quicklime does a successful burn give? (b) How much water does full slaking demand, and what does the slaked product weigh? **14.4 ●●○ — The aged solution, assayed.** 25.00 mL of a drain-opener solution that has sat open a season takes 16.85 mL of 0.1000 M HCl to reach phenolphthalein's endpoint and 18.30 mL more-minus-nothing — wait, read carefully: 18.30 mL *total* to reach the methyl-orange endpoint (the titration continues, it does not restart). What are [NaOH] and [Na₂CO₃]? What fraction of the original alkali has carbonated? *(Hint: two endpoints, two unknowns; the *tail* between the endpoints is half the carbonate's story.)* **Ways.** Way 1 (P12 algebra): n_p = 1.685 mmol, n_t = 1.830 mmol; n(Na₂CO₃) = 2(n_t − n_p) = 0.290 mmol, n(NaOH) = 2n_p − n_t = 1.540 mmol → **[NaOH] = 0.0616 M, [Na₂CO₃] = 0.0116 M**.
