# Vol II · Chapter 20 — Nitrogen and Ammonia: The Fixed-Nitrogen Bench, One Element, Eight Trades

## Lecture 0 — The fixed-nitrogen shelf: eight trades that sell one element and never say its name

Chapter 14 built the alkali shelf; Chapter 15 climbed the mineral acids; Chapter 17 walked sulfur's ladder; Chapter 18 read the halogens; Chapter 19 read the metals the household owns as objects. This chapter reads a single element — the one the household breathes a liter at a time and never buys as an element — and it opens with the observation that makes nitrogen a chapter rather than a gas: **every label on the fixed-nitrogen shelf hides the word "nitrogen," and yet every item on that shelf is a nitrogen account the household already owns.** The cleaning bottle is an ammonia solution. The garden bag is 21% nitrogen by mass. The sports pack is an ammonium nitrate charge. The pink curing salt is a nitrite. The smelling-salts bottle is an ammonium carbonate. The styptic pencil is an ammonium alum. The baker's jar is ammonium carbonate again. The homebrew shelf's yeast nutrient is diammonium phosphate. Eight trades, eight shelves, one element — and not one of the eight labels says what the bench is holding.

The reason the labels can stay quiet is the reason this chapter exists. **Nitrogen is 78% of the air over the house, and almost none of it is available** — the N≡N triple bond costs 945 kJ/mol to break [convergent], the strongest simple bond in chemistry's everyday register, and so the air's own nitrogen is the most abundant and least reactive substance the household touches. To use it, someone must *fix* it — tear the triple pair apart and bolt each atom to hydrogen — and fixing it is a job the household never does itself. This is the chapter's thesis, and it is the volume's hinge stated a sixth time: **the trades fix the nitrogen for you.** The Haber plant does it at 400–450 °C and 150–200 atm over an iron catalyst; the soil's own bacteria do it at ambient pressure with an enzyme; the lightning bolt does it with a bolt. The household's bench does none of these, and the discipline is the same three-part rule the volume has kept since Chapter 14: let the trades take the dangerous and expensive steps for you (the plant, the soil, the cylinder trade), bench only the faces whose safety architecture exists (the bottle census, the white smoke, the generator at kitchen scale, the cold pack at printed charge), and never once generate the faces the book refuses to make (the nitrogen oxides, the chloramine ladder, the energetic faces of ammonium nitrate).

Sit at the kitchen table with the notebook and pour nothing. The pencil-and-paper census of the fixed-nitrogen shelf, before any apparatus:

- **The cleaning trade: the ammonia bottle.** The label says *ammonia* — the one trade honest enough to name the compound, though it writes the old fiction "NH₄OH" on the MSDS and sells a 5% w/w solution of NH₃ in water (the ≤28% *aqua ammonia* class is the trade's own ceiling [convergent]). Chapter 8 already audited this bottle: 2.88 M at the printed two decimals, pH 11.86, the strongest base on the household shelf. This chapter re-derives the audit from the label up (Lab 20.2 B1) and makes the bottle the census's base face — the one form of fixed nitrogen the household buys *as a reagent*.
- **The garden trade: the fertilizer bag.** The label is three dash-separated numbers — 21-0-0, 34-0-0, 46-0-0 — and the numbers are the mass percentages of N, P₂O₅-equivalent, and K₂O-equivalent. The garden shelf's ammonium sulfate is 21.20% nitrogen (the computed value behind the 21-0-0 label), its urea is 46.65%, and the translation layer — including why phosphorus is reported as an oxide of an element this book's table refuses — is Lecture 1's first work.
- **The sports trade: the instant cold pack.** The pack is a bag of water and a sealed inner sachet of ammonium nitrate; squeeze, the sachet breaks, the salt dissolves endothermically (+25.69 kJ/mol [convergent]), and the bag goes ice-cold. The AN line — the binding line this chapter inherits and states at its strictest — starts here.
- **The charcuterie trade: the pink salt.** *Prague powder #1* is 6.25% sodium nitrite + 93.75% sodium chloride [convergent] — the cure's own chemistry, sold by the ounce, dyed pink so no one ever mistakes it for table salt. The nitrite is nitrogen at +3, and the meter prices it in Lecture 2.
- **The pharmacy trade: the smelling salts.** The little bottle of *sal volatile* is ammonium carbonate — the monohydrate (NH₄)₂CO₃·H₂O of the trade's own article, heritage name *spirit of hartshorn* [convergent] — a salt that is decomposing gently all the time, which is precisely why it wakes the fainter.
- **The barber trade: the styptic pencil.** The alum stick that stops a shaving cut is ammonium alum, NH₄Al(SO₄)₂·12H₂O at 453.313 g/mol — one NH₄⁺ swapped for the potassium of the crystal-potassium alum block at 474.372 g/mol (the 21.059 g/mol difference is exactly K − NH₄, and the engine prints the check).
- **The bakery trade: the baker's ammonia.** *Hartshorn* again — the ammonium carbonate/bicarbonate class [convergent] — the leavening that leaves no residue because its decomposition products are all gas: the cookie that carries it smells faintly of the salt when raw and of nothing at all when baked.
- **The homebrew trade: the DAP jar.** *Diammonium phosphate*, the yeast nutrient, label 18-46-0 — the one fixed-N item whose phosphorus face this book refuses at the element table, and the refusal is printed in the engine's gate log rather than smoothed over.

That is eight trades and one supporting cast — the match trade's saltpeter (KNO₃, 13-0-46, the oxidizer heritage), the stump-remover bottle, the desiccator's magnesium nitrate — and the census's quiet observation is the thesis seen as shelf goods: **every one of these nitrogen atoms was fixed by someone else.** The bottle's ammonia was born in a Haber plant; the fertilizer's ammonium and nitrate were born in the same plant's downstream chemistry; the proteins the soil bacterium fixes end, eventually, as the same shelf's salts. The bench inherits them all pre-paid.

One boundary before any apparatus is unpacked, and it is the book's binding line for this entire chapter — five lines, inherited from Chapters 10, 15, and 16 and stated here together because this is the shelf where they all meet. **First: ammonia and bleach are never combined — no procedure, no proportion, no "safe amount."** The ammonia bottle and the bleach bottle are the two most reactive things on the cleaning shelf, and their meeting runs the chloramine ladder (E12–E14), whose top rung is nitrogen trichloride — *yellow, oily, explosive* [convergent]. The pool's own "chlorine smell" is chloramines [convergent]: the smell the trade warns about is this chapter's never-door, priced at the meter in Lecture 5 and benched in exactly zero procedures. **Second: ammonium nitrate is an energetic material** — the cold pack's endothermic dissolution is its one sanctioned bench face; its thermal face (E23) and detonation face (E24) are recognition-only, printed as bookkeeping, benched nowhere — the salt is never heated, never dried, never contaminated, never confined. The energetics chapter (28) owns the recognition training; this chapter owns the fence at every mention. **Third: hydrazine is refused** — N06's and N07's faces are recognition-only (the meter prices its lazy fall at −21.2 kJ; the element table admitted no formula for it beyond the battery's census row). **Fourth: the nitrogen oxides are never generated** — Chapter 15's line held: nitric acid stays on its own shelf's discipline, and NO and NO₂ are named, priced, and never made; the nitrites are never acidified (the HNO₂ face escapes as the brown gas the book refuses). **Fifth: ammonia gas is a Chapter 10 face** — generated only at the bench's printed scale, delivered upward only (NH₃ at 0.696 g/L is lighter than air), never collected over water (it vanishes — 700 vol/vol [convergent], the number Chapter 10 verified at review), never sealed, never flamed. The prior volume's fences — thermite-class, lead, the alkali metals, chromium and cobalt — still stand; this chapter touches none of them.

## Lecture 1 — The eight trades' labels, translated: NPK numbers, pink percentages, and the fiction on the ammonia bottle

Every fixed-N item arrived through a trade, and every trade writes its labels in its own currency. The translation layer is the chapter's first work, and it runs on the same composition engine as always (Lab 20.1, executed in-drafting below).

**The cleaning trade's fiction: NH₄OH.** The bottle's safety sheet says "ammonium hydroxide" as if a compound NH₄OH were isolated in the jug; the honest chemistry is a 5% w/w solution of NH₃ in water, where most of the ammonia sits as loose NH₃·H₂O pairs and hydrated molecules — which is why the bottle smells so freely, and why the gas rule (never sealed) is the bottle's own rule too. The bench reads "NH₄OH" the way Chapter 2 taught it to read every trade label: as the trade's name for a solution, not for a substance. The audit the fiction hides is worth doing anyway, and Lab 20.2 B1 does it: 500 mL at ρ 0.98 is 490 g of solution, 5% w/w is 24.50 g of NH₃, that is 1.438553 mol, and in half a liter that is 2.8771 M — Chapter 8's printed 2.88 reproduced from the label up, with the pH chain (Kb 1.8×10⁻⁵) landing on 11.857 → the printed 11.86. One label, two chapters, one number.

**The garden trade's arithmetic: the three-number label.** The fertilizer label's numbers are mass percentages — of N itself, then of P as if it were P₂O₅, then of K as if it were K₂O — a convention inherited from the days when fertilizers *were* sold as those oxides. The conversions are fixed multipliers: K → K₂O is ×1.2046, P → P₂O₅ is ×2.2914 [convergent]. The phosphorus multiplier is printed here as trade convention only — this book's element table refuses phosphorus (the gate log says so), and no P-bearing composition is computed anywhere in this chapter. What the bench does compute is the nitrogen behind the first number: ammonium sulfate 21.20% (label 21-0-0), ammonium nitrate 35.00% (label 34-0-0, the trade's own rounding down of the computed assay), urea 46.65% (label 46-0-0), sodium nitrate 16.48%, potassium nitrate 13.85% N with 46.58% K₂O-equivalent (label 13-0-46 — the double-duty salt, an oxidizer *and* a fertilizer), and the calcium ammonium nitrate double salt 15.55% N + 18.54% Ca (label 15.5-0-0 + 18.5 Ca). The price ledger the census builds (B2) is the trade's own economics: at $6 per 500 g bag, AN delivers nitrogen at $34.29 per kilogram; at $8 per kilogram, urea delivers it at $17.15 — **2.00:1, the solid-N census's own verdict, and the reason the garden shelf's biggest bag is always the urea one.**

**The sports trade's ice: the cold pack's honest bookkeeping.** The pack's promise is written in degrees, and the bench reads it in kilojoules: ΔH°soln +25.69 kJ/mol [convergent], which is 0.3210 kJ per gram of salt. Lab 20.2 B4 prices a printed charge (25.0 g into 125 g of water): 8.024 kJ absorbed, a 15.33 K drop, 20.0 °C to 4.7 °C — and the ledger prints the two seams honestly [flagged sketch]: the constant-c idealization, and the 0 °C plateau the label's "ice cold" gestures at. The AN binding line is restated at the pack's own row in the safety table.

**The charcuterie trade's pinch: the pink salt.** Prague powder #1's 6.25/93.75 split [convergent] makes the cure's chemistry safe to *handle* — 6.25 g of NaNO₂ per 100 g is 0.09059 mol, 91 mmol, a spoon-tip of a salt whose pure form the trade rightly refuses to sell plain. The bench prices the nitrite at the meter in Lecture 2 (the +3 rung), notes the pink dye's own purpose (the salt must never look like table salt — the trade's safety architecture doing Chapter 2's work), and honors the line: nitrites are never acidified, and the cure's own nitrosamine chemistry is recognition territory, not bench territory.

**The pharmacy trade's reviver: the salt that is always decomposing.** Sal volatile — (NH₄)₂CO₃·H₂O, the monohydrate, 114.101 g/mol — is a salt the shelf sells *because* it is unstable: it exhales NH₃ and CO₂ continuously (E7), which is why the fainter wakes and why the bottle never smells like nothing. The heritage names — *sal volatile*, *spirit of hartshorn*, the deer's horn ash of the seventeenth-century apothecary [convergent] — are the trade's own memory of the fact. The baker's jar is the same chemistry's carbonate/bicarbonate blend, chosen because its decomposition leaves no solid residue: leavening that vanishes into its own gases.

**The barber trade's pencil: the one-swap alum.** The styptic pencil's ammonium alum (453.313) and the crystal deodorant block's potassium alum (474.372) are the same double-sulfate architecture with one cation swapped — and the swap is the chapter's own lesson in reading an alum: the ammonium face is the one that smells faintly of the fixed-N bench when the pencil is wet (the astringent acid is the same in both, but only one of them carries nitrogen). The engine's census prints both, and the gate arithmetic (474.372 − 453.313 = 21.059 = K − NH₄) closes the pair.

**The homebrew trade's jar: the label this chapter refuses to compute.** DAP — diammonium phosphate, 18-46-0 — is the yeast nutrient that keeps a must fermenting to dryness. Its nitrogen face (the 18) is this chapter's; its phosphorus face (the 46) is refused at the element table, and the refusal is the discipline, not a gap: the engine's gate log records (NH₄)₂HPO₄ refused — TABLE REFUSES P — and the label is read as trade prose exactly as Chapter 18 read the fluoride faces and Chapter 19 read the refusals it inherited.

The census closes as it opened: eight trades, one element, and a translation layer that turns every shelf label into a mole account — **and the observation the chapter now builds on: the household never fixes nitrogen, so the bench's whole fixed-N chemistry is inheritance.** What the bench *does* own is the second half of nitrogen's story: the traffic between the oxidation states, the meter's own ladder — and that is Lecture 2's work.

## Lecture 2 — The meter on nitrogen: the ladder from +5 to −3, and why one salt carries both ends in one crystal

Chapter 11 built the E° meter; Chapter 12 priced the electron; Chapter 19 read a whole metals bench through the ladder. This chapter reads a *single element* through the meter, because nitrogen's one organizing fact is that it is the champion of oxidation states: the household's nitrogen sits at +5 (nitrates), +3 (nitrites), 0 (the air itself), and −3 (ammonium) — four rungs of one ladder, all on the same shelf, and the meter prices every step between them. **The ladder is the trade network's own map:** the +5 rung is the fertilizer bag and the desiccator, the +3 rung is the pink salt, the −3 rung is the ammonia bottle and every ammonium salt, and the 0 rung is the 78% of the air the whole shelf was fixed out of.

Three readings before the bench opens. **First, the fixation row is barely downhill — and that fact is the whole industrial world.** The +0.27 V, −156.3 kJ for six electrons looks eager until it is read against the bond that must die first: the N≡N pair at 945 kJ/mol [convergent] takes most of the thermodynamic budget back, which is why the plant needs 400–450 °C and 150–200 atm over iron to make the row run at a commercial rate (the Haber thermal bookkeeping: −92.28 kJ per 2 NH₃ at 25 °C [convergent] — modest, reversible, and worth a plant's whole architecture in catalyst and pressure), and why the legume does not bother doing it itself: it hires Rhizobium, a bacterium with an enzyme that spends ATP to do at 1 atm what the plant does at 150. **The bench's honest verdict on the Haber row is Lecture 3's BUY** — the one fixed-N account the household could, in principle, make at home is the one account nobody sane makes at home.

**Second — the lecture's one idea, and it deserves its own paragraph — ammonium nitrate is energetic by architecture, not by accident.** The cold pack's salt is the only common substance on the household shelf that carries *both ends of the ladder in one crystal*: the NH₄⁺ cation holds nitrogen at −3 (reduced, electron-rich) and the NO₃⁻ anion holds nitrogen at +5 (oxidized, electron-hungry) — the −671.5 kJ ladder compressed into one dry white solid that needs no external partner to cash it. The dissolution face is the *physical* face (endothermic solution, no redox — the sanctioned bench face, B4's ledger), and the two redox faces are the bookkeeping the chapter prints and never runs: the thermal face E23 (NH₄NO₃ → N₂O + 2 H₂O, ≈ −36 kJ/mol on the ΔH°f bookkeeping: AN −365.6, N₂O +82.05, H₂O(g) −241.8 [convergent]) and the detonation face E24 (2 NH₄NO₃ → 2 N₂ + O₂ + 4 H₂O — the face that makes the same salt a mining trade's product), both recognition-only, both priced here only so the fence is honest, both owned by Chapter 28's recognition training. The AN binding line — never heated, never dried, never contaminated, never confined — is the meter's own warning written as a shelf rule.

**Third, the tame rows explain the shelf's quietness.** Nitrate is everywhere on the household shelf precisely because its first step is so lazy: NO₃⁻ → NO₂⁻ in base is +0.01 V, −1.9 kJ — nothing. Sodium nitrate, potassium nitrate, the calcium and magnesium nitrates of the fertilizer and desiccator shelves all sit for years as polite white solids (the drift E20 — 2 NaNO₂ + O₂ → 2 NaNO₃ — runs the *other* way, aging nitrites into nitrates in air). It is the acid rows that are eager — NO₃⁻ at +0.94 in acid is the mineral-acid shelf's own oxidizing power, and Chapter 15 already fenced it there — and the escape of the waystation (HNO₂ → NO at +1.00) is why the book's never-generate line covers the whole NOx family: the waystation does not sit still once it exists. The nitrite half of the ladder (−521.0 kJ to ammonium) is why nitrites do not linger in reducing company either; the pink salt is the +3 rung held politely in a salt.

And the two recognition rows close the ladder's ends: **hydrazine's lazy fall at −21.2 kJ** (N₂H₄ → 2 NH₄⁺, the −2 rung — priced here because the meter can price it safely, benched nowhere, N06/N07 held at register level), and **the nitrification cell at +0.359 V** — the row Lecture 5 builds the whole cycle map on: the −3 face of ammonia meeting the oxygen cathode, E°cell = 1.229 − 0.87, −277.1 kJ per mole of nitrogen — the cell the soil runs for free, every day, in every compost pile and every filter bed, and the row the meter prices while the bench generates nothing at all.

One tabulation seam is printed here rather than smoothed over [flagged sketch]: the nitrate-to-nitrous-acid couple is tabulated across the references from +0.835 to +0.94 V; the chapter prints +0.94 (the CRC-class value the −181.4 kJ row is built on) and names the spread, exactly as Chapter 19 named the CuO ΔH°f spread at its review.

## Lecture 3 — The ammonia bench: the bottle, the smoke, the generator, and the one account the bench buys

The −3 rung is where the household's bench actually works, and it works through four doors: the bottle as bought, the smoke as test, the generator as the bench's one make-at-home gas, and the delivery discipline that makes the whole bench legal under Chapter 10's rules.

**The bottle, audited.** Lab 20.2 B1 walks the label to the mole account and back: 500 mL at ρ 0.98 g/mL is 490 g of solution; the 5% w/w label is 24.50 g of NH₃; that is 1.438553 mol; in half a liter, 2.8771 M — and the two Chapter 8 pins reproduce exactly (2.88 at two decimals, pH 11.86 through the Kb 1.8×10⁻⁵ chain: [OH⁻] = √(Kb·C) = 7.196×10⁻³, pOH 2.143, pH 11.857). The working dilution (1:20) is 0.1439 M — Chapter 8's printed 0.144 — and its pH is 11.21, the strength of most of this book's ammonia benches. The audit's quiet lesson is the trade's: *the label is the assay*, and the assay drifts — the bottle is dated like the muriatic jug (Chapter 15's discipline carried), because a loosened cap vents the assay one molecule at a time. The absorption refrigerator's own use of ammonia (R-717, the trade's own refrigerant designation [convergent]) and the classroom's ammonia fountain are both named as recognition: the first as the fixed-N bench's proudest industrial employment, the second as the 700-vol/vol solubility's own demonstration — a sealed-flask demonstration this bench reads about and does not schedule, because this bench's rule for NH₃ is upward delivery and never a sealed system.

**The smoke, as test.** The bench's own ammonia test is the oldest one: concentrated muriatic acid held near ammonia fumes throws the white smoke of NH₄Cl (E1) — the solid meeting in the air at 2.141:1 by mass (HCl:NH₃, the pair arithmetic of B5: the smoke demands twice its weight in acid). The white smoke is the *positive* test; the nose at the door is the warning; damp red litmus turning blue at the mouth of a generator is the bench's written record. Chapter 13's qualitative scheme already had the chloride half of this door; this chapter adds the ammonia half, and Exercise 20.6's trap — the two white bottles — is built on exactly this meeting.

**The generator, priced three ways.** The bench's one at-home ammonia route is the ammonium-salt-plus-base displacement, and Lab 20.2 B3 prices all three household versions of it on the same 10.00 g of ammonium salt: the sal-ammoniac route (E2: NH₄Cl + NaOH → NH₃ + NaCl + H₂O — 0.186944 mol, NaOH 7.477 g, NH₃ 3.184 g = 4.574 L dry at RTP), the sulfate route (E3, the garden bag's own salt — 0.075681 mol, NaOH 6.054 g, NH₃ 2.578 g = 3.703 L, plus the Glauber-salt byproduct 10.749 g), and the Victorian route (E17: 2 NH₄Cl + Ca(OH)₂ — the gasworks-era generator, 6.926 g of slaked lime, the CaCl₂ byproduct 10.374 g). The B5 ranking tells the bench which salt carries the most ammonia per gram — urea 0.567, sal volatile 0.354, sal ammoniac 0.318, the baker's bicarbonate 0.215 — but the bench's honest route choice is the sal-ammoniac one: cheapest, cleanest, and its byproduct is table salt.

**The delivery, under Chapter 10's discipline.** Every gram of generated NH₃ moves upward only — the gas is lighter than air (0.696 g/L, the Chapter 10 pin the engine re-derives) — is never collected over water (700 vol/vol: over water, the collection jar *empties itself into the bath*, which is the solubility's own joke), is never sealed, and never meets a flame. The rule is Chapter 10's, the gas is this chapter's, and the safety table's first row is the two of them restated.

**The Haber verdict: BUY.** The one account the household could make from air is the one account this book never schedules: 400–450 °C, 150–200 atm, an iron catalyst, and a reversible −92.28 kJ per 2 NH₃ that the plant runs in loop after loop of compression and condensation. The cylinder trade and the bottle trade both stand between the bench and the plant, exactly as Chapter 15's acid verdicts stood between the bench and the smelter. The bench buys the bottle; the plant keeps the pressure; the legume keeps its bacterium; and the household's fixed nitrogen stays, as the thesis said, an inheritance.

## Lecture 4 — The ammonium-salt bench: the salts that smell, decompose, sublime, and diagnose — plus the two faces the table refuses

The −3 rung's salts have their own bench discipline, and it begins with the observation that **ammonium is the only common cation whose whole chemistry is a slow leak.** Ammonium salts decompose — gently at room temperature (the smelling-salts bottle), smokily on a hot shelf (sal ammoniac's own sublimation), and completely under the gas rule (every generator route) — and the bench reads the leak as a feature, not a defect.

**The thermal faces, as a ladder of their own.** The smelling salt's monohydrate exhales its whole anion (E7: (NH₄)₂CO₃·H₂O → 2 NH₃ + CO₂ + 2 H₂O); the parent carbonate does the same with one less water (E6); the baker's bicarbonate is the gentlest of the three (E5); and the ammonium carbamate behind them (NH₂COONH₄, E8 — the salt of ammonia and carbon dioxide that the trade's old *volcano bottles* were made of) is the sealed-bottle lesson of the whole class: an equilibrium salt that decomposes into two gases and re-forms when they meet, which is why the smelling-salts bottle is never filled to the brim and never sealed hot. The urea of the garden shelf joins the ladder by enzymatic door (E10: CO(NH₂)₂ + H₂O → 2 NH₃ + CO₂, the urease reaction — a nickel metalloenzyme [convergent], the stable floor's own chemistry, and Lecture 5's cycle face). The hot face is sal ammoniac's own: E9's dissociation (NH₄Cl ⇌ NH₃ + HCl) is what makes the salt *sublime* at ~340 °C [convergent] — the smoky dance that named the salt for the volcano it performs on a hot shelf — and the recombination in the cooler air above is the white fur the trade calls flowers of sal ammoniac. A kitchen-scale sublimation on a watch glass is the one thermal face small enough to bench; the same rule as the gas discipline holds: never in a closed vessel.

**The qualitative door, and the trap that guards it.** The bench's one diagnostic for "is this white crystal an ammonium salt?" is E4: dampen with the lye, warm gently, and the nose (or the damp red litmus) answers. The door is Chapter 13's own wet-scheme discipline extended to the cation side. The trap — and it is the exercise engine's ◆ summit — is that the *chloride* half of the scheme cannot distinguish the two white bottles: silver nitrate throws white curds with NaCl *and* with NH₄Cl (Chapter 18's own AgCl wall, invoked by both). The lye door tells them apart in ten seconds; the AgNO₃ bottle never will; and the bench that reaches for the silver bottle first has reached for the wrong instrument — P9's instrument-first discipline priced as a diagnosis.

**N70's metathesis, at register level.** The Home Scientist's metavanadate episode (*015 - Synthesize Ammonium Metavanadate*, the register's own wording) is this lecture's one corpus salt-synthesis face, and it is read at register level exactly as the register pins it: NH₄Cl + NaVO₃ → NH₄VO₃↓ + NaCl — "just about any ammonium salt will do," the episode's own lesson, an ice-bath crystallization driven by the product's the-lesser-solubility. The vanadium is refused at this book's element table (the gate log prints NH₄VO₃ refused — TABLE REFUSES V), the episode is benched nowhere, and the lesson the bench keeps is the metathesis itself: ammonium salts trade partners like any salt pair, and the ammonium ends up in the *less* soluble product — Chapter 9's solubility discipline doing the fixed-N bench's own work.
