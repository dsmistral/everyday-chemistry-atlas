# Vol II · Chapter 23 — Fats, Soaps, and Esters: The Ester Shelf, One Linkage, Both Directions

## Lecture 0 — The ester shelf: eight trades that sell the same linkage and never say "ester"

Volume II has read the metals, the one element the household breathes, the flammable shelf, and the color shelf. This chapter reads the functional group the household buys by the kilogram and never once sees named: **the ester** — the linkage R–COO–R that a carboxylic acid and an alcohol make when they lose a water, and that water takes back when it gets the chance. The observation that makes this a chapter rather than a pantry inventory: **every fat is an ester, every soap is a salt, and both directions of the same linkage run at the kitchen scale — the fat is built by an esterification and unmade by a saponification, and the household owns both arrows.** The fryer's oil is a triglyceride: three fatty-acid chains esterified onto one glycerol. The bar at the sink is the same molecule after lye has done its work: three sodium-or-potassium salts where the ester bonds were, and the glycerol set free. One linkage, both directions, and the trades never print the word.

The reason they never print it is the reason this chapter exists. The household owns the ester shelf as *products* — a cooking oil, a bar of soap, a bottle of glycerin, a tin of paint, a candle, a candy that smells of wintergreen — and each product is the linkage running one direction or the other, right now, unprompted: the oil in a hot pan is shedding free fatty acids that lower its smoke point, the soap in hard water is trading its sodium for calcium and becoming scum, the linseed oil on a painter's rag is grabbing oxygen at the double bonds and heating itself toward combustion. **The trades package the linkage; the direction is the chemistry.** This is the volume's hinge stated a ninth time, and the census shows how wide it runs:

- **The kitchen trade: the fat shelf proper.** Cooking oils (olive, canola, coconut), butter, lard, vegetable shortening — all triglycerides, all esters. The label the trade prints instead of "ester" is the fatty-acid profile: *saturated, monounsaturated, polyunsaturated* — which is to say, how many C=C double bonds the chains carry, a count this chapter will price in iodine (Lecture 2) and blame for the rancidity (Lecture 3). Olive oil runs ~59–74% monounsaturated (the oleic class [convergent]); coconut runs ~94% saturated, near half of it lauric [convergent] — the two poles of the pantry shelf, and the two anchors of the saponification arithmetic.
- **The cleaning trade: the soap shelf.** The bar of soap — the label's ingredient list printed as *sodium tallowate, sodium palmate, sodium cocoate* — which is the most transparent nomenclature on any shelf in the household: the INCI name of a soap is simply the salt named after its own acid, and the whole label is a salts list (Lecture 1). Castile — the olive-oil bar and its history; Marseille — the 72% olive rule of the 1688 Edict of Colbert [convergent]; the liquid soap on the pump, which is the same chemistry wearing potassium instead of sodium.
- **The cosmetics trade: the glycerin bottle and the wax jar.** Glycerin — the humectant, the soap-maker's byproduct sold back as a moisturizer, the same triol Chapter 21 priced as the shelf's safest fuel (17.96 MJ/kg behind a 160 °C flash point) and the corpus's luminol solvent (N06's high-boiler, honored again here). Beeswax — mostly esters (myricyl palmitate, 677.240 g/mol [computed], the major component [convergent]) — beside the paraffin block, which is *not an ester at all* (a hydrocarbon blend; the "wax" on the label hides the split, Lecture 5).
- **The paint trade: the drying oils and the refused driers.** Linseed oil — the paint binder that cures by oxidizing its double bonds into a polymer network (an IV above ~130 is the trade's own drying-class line [convergent]); boiled linseed — historically drier-metal soaps in the oil, the litharge-and-lead class (E21, museum-only). The modern driers — cobalt and manganese linoleates — are the metals this chapter's own engine table refuses at the gate (Lab 23.1): recognition-only faces, benched nowhere.
- **The candle trade: stearin and paraffin.** The classical candle is stearin (the stearic-acid triglyceride class) burning at E18's oxygen bill; the cheap candle is paraffin (E20, the hydrocarbon). The two burn alike and are chemically unrelated — the wax trade's own quiet isomer lesson, and this chapter's museum row.
- **The pharmacy trade: the liniment and the laxative.** Wintergreen liniment — methyl salicylate, 152.149 g/mol [computed], the ester of salicylic acid and methanol, the aspirin family's own ester (Lecture 5); mineral oil — the label that is *not* an ester (a hydrocarbon; saponification value ≈ 0, the one number that sorts the whole bathroom shelf, Lecture 1); the glycerin suppository — the byproduct again, on its third trade.
- **The garage trade: the motor oil and the biodiesel pump.** Motor oil — a mineral hydrocarbon blend, recognition-only, not an ester (the SV ≈ 0 class again); biodiesel — the fatty-acid *methyl* esters, the ester shelf's one modern fuel, priced at the desk (E15) and never scheduled (the methanol fence, below).
- **The flavor trade: the ester shelf's perfume counter.** Isoamyl acetate — banana (E13); ethyl butyrate — pineapple (E14); methyl salicylate — root beer and wintergreen candy (E12). The flavor trade is the ester family's storefront: small esters are volatile and fruity, and the trade's entire flavor arsenal is this chapter's linkage wearing small chains. (The tasting rule applies — Lecture 0's binding lines — these are smelled, never sipped.)

One linkage, eight trades, and the chapter's thesis is the two arrows: **the household eats the forward arrow's products and cleans with the reverse arrow's.** The bench that follows honors both.

**Six binding lines open the chapter, inherited from the volume's prior fences and stated together because this is the shelf where they all meet.** *First: the flame line — no open flame anywhere in the room, water bath or hot plate only* [Chapter 6, verbatim]. The fat shelf is a fuel shelf — every triglyceride on it burns at 39.6 kJ/g (Lecture 2), the smoke point table (Lecture 3) is the fat's own fire-safety specification, and a grease fire is the flame line's worst-case meeting: **never water on a grease fire** — the water flashes to steam under the oil and throws burning fat across the room [convergent]; the lid goes on the pan, the heat goes off, baking soda (not flour, not baking powder [convergent]) smothers the small ones, and the fire extinguisher owns the rest. *Second: the lye discipline* [Chapter 14, verbatim in force] — sodium and potassium hydroxide are caustic; the eye is the absolute hazard; the "it only feels soapy" moment on the fingers is the alkali saponifying the skin's own fats, and the gloves and goggles are worn for the whole batch, not the pour. *Third: the tasting rule* [Chapter 8] — nothing from this shelf is tasted, and the flavor esters are smelled at a distance, never sipped: methyl salicylate is the reason the rule has teeth on this shelf, at ~1860 mg of aspirin-equivalent per milliliter of wintergreen oil [convergent], and the candy flavoring is diluted by orders of magnitude the lab bottle is not. *Fourth: the methanol fence* [Chapter 21] — biodiesel's chemistry is priced at the desk (E15) and its procedure is never scheduled: the route wants methanol (the 10 mL blindness datum, Chapter 21's B5 ledger) *and* lye (the burn above) in the same pot, and the chapter reads the route's chemistry while keeping both fences. *Fifth: the pressure line* — fat hydrogenation (E9/E10) is the industrial margarine door, and it stays industrial: pressurized hydrogen over a nickel catalyst [convergent] is not a kitchen reaction; the equations are balanced in the meter, the apparatus is never built, and the recognition is the whole face. *Sixth: the drier metals are refused* — cobalt, manganese, and the historical lead are museum faces (E21 composes the lead soap for the museum's label only); the rags that carried the drying oil are the disposal hazard the trade itself warns about: linseed-soaked rags oxidize exothermically and have burned buildings [convergent] — they dry flat, spread out, never balled in the trash. The prior volume's fences — thermite-class, lead, the alkali metals, chromium and cobalt — stand unbroken; the one new element this chapter's table admits is none (Lab 23.1 holds Chapter 22's nineteen and refuses three).

## Lecture 1 — The trade's labels, translated: SAP values, INCI salts, and the number that is zero

The fat shelf's labels are the volume's most transparent dialect, because the trade's own assay numbers are printed or implied on nearly every package. The bench translation runs six entries:

**The INCI salts list.** A soap label's ingredients — *sodium tallowate, sodium palmate, sodium cocoate, sodium olivate* — are simply the sodium salts of the fat's own fatty acids, named acid-by-acid: tallow's, palm's, coconut's, olive's. The label is the saponification equation (E1–E3) written as a shopping list. The potassium versions (*potassium olivate*) mark the liquid soaps — the same salts wearing potassium, and potassium's soaps stay soft and liquid where sodium's go hard, the trade fact Chapter 14 flagged forward and the bench prices at E4.

**The SAP value.** The soap trade's central number: the lye demand of an oil, printed in the trade's units — grams of NaOH per gram of oil (the household-soap convention, ~0.134 for olive, 0.183–0.190 for coconut [convergent]) — or in the assay convention, milligrams of KOH per gram of fat (the *saponification value*, olive ~185–200, coconut ~250–265 [convergent]). The two conventions are the same number times one factor: SV × (M NaOH / M KOH) / 1000 = 190.09 × (39.997/56.105)/1000 = 0.1355 for the olive anchor (Lab 23.2's B1) — sitting just at the top of the trade's own range, which is the mixture honesty line: the single-species anchor is the arithmetic's peg, the label's range is the real oil, and the gap between them is the chemistry the bench learns to expect (Lecture 2).

**The fatty-acid profile.** The food label's *saturated/monounsaturated/polyunsaturated* is a double-bond census: zero, one, or several C=C per chain. The chemistry consequence is two-faced — the double bond is the oil's *liquid* (oleic's 13.4 °C melt versus stearic's 69 °C-class solidity) and the oil's *liability* (the oxygen attack that rancidity is, Lecture 3). The assay convention that prices the census is the **iodine value** (Lecture 2's second meter).

**The smoke point.** The frying specification, in °C, and the honest variable on the shelf: the smoke point *falls as free fatty acids rise* [convergent] — a fresh refined oil and the same oil after a season of use are different chemicals at the pan, because the smoke is the glycerol backbone's own decomposition product (acrolein, Lecture 3's recognition test). The trade's printed numbers (butter 150, lard 190, coconut ~177–204, refined olive ~199–243, EVOO ~190 with the honest contested spread [convergent]) are the *fresh-oil* end of each range.

**"Mineral oil" and the number that is zero.** Baby oil, the pharmacy's colorless laxative, the garage's motor oil — all hydrocarbon blends, **not esters**, and the assay that sorts the whole shelf is the saponification value: a mineral oil's SV is ≈ 0 (the trade's own assay standards carry a separate mineral-oil SV method for exactly this reason [convergent]). One number, run at the desk (Exercise 23.6's diagnostic door), and the ester shelf sorts itself from the petroleum shelf without a single reagent beyond lye and patience.

**The wax label's hidden split.** "Wax" names two chemically unrelated shelf-mates: beeswax, mostly *esters* (the myricyl palmitate class — a fatty acid esterified onto a long-chain *alcohol* rather than glycerol; the ester linkage is the same, the backbone is different), and paraffin, a *hydrocarbon* blend with no linkage to hydrolyze. The beeswax/paraffin blend on the craft-store shelf is the ester/non-ester split sold as one product — and the candle trade burns both without caring (E18 vs E20), which is the fire chapter's problem, not the ester chapter's.

## Lecture 2 — The meter on the ester: three assay meters, one linkage, and the equilibrium that prices the direction

The meter this chapter reads is arithmetic-first: the engine prices the family, and the assays follow from the formulas. Lab 23.1's battery holds the masses; this lecture is the meter that uses them.

**The family, composed.** A saturated fatty acid is CnH2nO2 — acetic C2H4O2 (60.052 [computed], Chapter 4's own vinegar acid), lauric C12H24O2 (200.322), palmitic C16H32O2 (256.430), stearic C18H36O2 (284.484); unsaturation subtracts an H2 per double bond — oleic C18H34O2 (282.468), linoleic C18H32O2 (280.452), linolenic C18H30O2 (278.436) [all computed]. The triglyceride is three acids minus three waters on one glycerol: triolein C57H104O6 (885.453), tristearin (891.501), trilaurin (639.015) [computed]. And the soap is the salt: sodium oleate C18H33NaO2 (304.450), sodium stearate (306.466), sodium laurate (222.304) [computed]. Every number in the benches that follow is these four formulas and their siblings, priced by the engine and closed by the ledger (E1–E3, all mass-exact).

**Meter one: the saponification value, or the chain-length meter.** SV = 3 × M(KOH) × 1000 / M(triglyceride) — and because the numerator is fixed (three ester bonds per molecule, one KOH each), **the SV is the inverse of the molecule**: short chains, high SV. The anchors (Lab 23.2, B1): triolein 190.09, tristearin 188.80, tripalmitin 208.48, trimyristin 232.74, trilaurin 263.40 mg KOH/g [computed]. The label joins [convergent]: olive 185–200 (the triolein anchor inside it), tallow-and-lard ~192–203 (the tristearin anchor at its floor), coconut 250–265 (the trilaurin anchor inside it). The meter's lesson is the trade's whole craft in one division: coconut's short chains demand the most lye per gram and give the quick-lather hard bar; olive's long chains demand the least and give the slow mild one — and the soap calculator's SAP column is this meter printed to three decimals.

**Meter two: the iodine value, or the unsaturation meter.** IV = (double bonds) × M(I2) × 100 / M(triglyceride) — iodine adds across each C=C, and the grams absorbed per 100 g of fat count the bonds. The anchors [computed]: triolein, three bonds, IV 86.0; trilinolein, six bonds, IV 173.2; the saturated triglycerides, IV 0. The label joins [convergent]: coconut 7.7–10.5 (nearly all-saturated chains plus a small oleic fraction), olive ~80 (the triolein anchor), soybean ~130 (the semidrying line), linseed 155–205 (the drying oils). And the meter's trade reading is the paint chapter's own gate: **an oil whose IV runs past ~130 dries to a film** [convergent] — the double bonds that iodine counts are the same double bonds that oxygen attacks and cross-links, which is Lecture 3's rancidity at the pantry and Lecture 5's drying oil at the paint store — one mechanism, two trades, and the IV is its price tag.

**Meter three: the energy meter, or the near-hydrocarbon face.** The fat's combustion (E17, mass-exact: C57H104O6 + 80 O2 → 57 CO2 + 52 H2O) bills **80 moles of oxygen per mole of oil — 2.891 g of O2 per gram of fat [computed]** — and the striking datum is how *little* oxygen the fat carries of its own: six O in 885.453 g, 1.08% by mass. The triglyceride is nearly a hydrocarbon wearing an ester tax, and it burns like one: the tabulated heat of combustion of triolein is **−35,100 kJ/mol — 39.64 kJ/g, and 13.71 kJ per gram of oxygen [tabulated; computed per O2]**, which sits in Chapter 21's fire-basis ladder at hexane's own floor (hexane 13.69, acetone 13.99 [Ch 21 pins]) — the fat is the household's densest shelf-fuel, denser than every solvent on Chapter 21's meter, and its flash point is the pan, not the bottle. The physiologist's version of the same number is the Atwater factor — fat at 9 kcal/g = 37.7 kJ/g — which is the bomb value discounted 4.9% for the body's own yield (37.7/39.64 = 95.1% [computed]); and the byproduct joins the same meter from Chapter 21: glycerol, 17.96 MJ/kg, oxygen bill 1.2161 g/g — the engine's E19 closure reproducing Chapter 21's printed O2 column to the fourth decimal, the cross-meter join done at both ends.

**The equilibrium that prices the direction.** The forward arrow — acid plus alcohol, minus water, the Fischer esterification — is the shelf's *weakest* reaction, and the honesty is the assay: the equilibrium constant for acetic acid + ethanol ⇌ ethyl acetate + water is **K ≈ 4, tabulated in the 3.4–4.0 spread [tabulated spread named]**, which prices the door at a ceiling: from a 1:1 charge, the conversion tops at **x = √K/(1+√K) = 2/3 — 66.67% at K = 4, 64.84% at K = 3.4 [computed]**, the two-thirds ceiling the organic-lab literature prints as "about 65% [convergent]." The two levers that move it are the two the shelf already owns: *excess alcohol* (a 4:1 ethanol charge lifts the ceiling to 92.96%, a 9:1 charge to 97.07% [computed]) and *water removal* — the Dean-Stark azeotropic lever this book has already priced at the oxalic-acid episode (N87), and the reason the door's bench form (Lecture 5) is a still, not a pot. The equilibrium's own chapter is Chapter 24, and this chapter flags it forward as promised: the K's derivation is Chapter 24's homework; here it is priced, used, and honestly labeled [flagged sketch at this chapter's level].

## Lecture 3 — The fat bench: assays without instruments, the smoke, and the two rancidities

The fat bench's doors are assay doors — the shelf's own numbers reproduced at the bench — plus the one recognition test that needs nothing but heat and a pinch of an old salt.

**The saponification-value assay, priced.** The classical SV determination is a reflux and a back-titration [convergent]: a weighed oil sample, excess *alcoholic* KOH under reflux (the ethanol dissolves the oil; the alkali hydrolyzes every ester bond, E1–E3 run to completion), then a back-titration of the surviving KOH with standard HCl to phenolphthalein — the blank-minus-sample difference *is* the KOH the fat consumed, and the formula prints the SV. The bench owns every piece already: the reflux (Chapter 6's still-head discipline), the titration (Chapter 8's drop counter, Chapter 14's Warder chain), the endpoint (phenolphthalein — Chapter 22's own indicator). The assay is priced here as arithmetic (B1's anchors) and run as a bench description; the household's honest version of the datum is the label's own printed range.

**The acrolein test, or the burnt-fat recognition.** The oldest fat-recognition instrument is a smell: glycerol, heated with a dehydrating salt (the classical reagent is potassium bisulfate [convergent]), sheds two waters and becomes **acrolein, C3H4O, 56.064 g/mol [computed]** (E8, mass-exact) — the acrid smell of burnt fat, the molecule Berzelius named from *acrid* + *oleum* while working with soap-boiler's glycerol in 1839 [convergent]. The test sorts the ester shelf at a stroke: a fat or a glycerin gives the smell (the glycerol backbone is there to dehydrate); a mineral oil does not (there is no backbone). It is also the safety datum wearing a recognition face: **the smoke above an overheated pan is the fat's free glycerol turning to acrolein** — never breathe the smoke, and the smoke point table below is the fence drawn in °C.

**The smoke point, and why it falls.** The trade's frying numbers, fresh-oil class [convergent]: butter 150 °C (the milk solids, not the fat, smoke first), lard 190, virgin coconut ~177 (refined 204), the refined-olive class 199–243, EVOO ~190 with the honest contested spread, ghee/clarified ~250 (the solids removed), tallow ~250. The mechanism the table hides: **the smoke point falls as free fatty acids rise** [convergent] — hydrolysis (the reverse arrow again) clips the triglycerides and the freed glycerol dehydrates sooner; an old fryer's oil smokes hundreds of degrees below its label because it is no longer the chemical on the label. The kitchen's own assay: the first wisp of smoke over a fresh pan is a thermometer with one reading, and the reading is *replace the oil*.

**Rancidity, both kinds.** *Hydrolytic rancidity*: water and lipase clip the ester bonds and free the fatty acids — the soap-taste class, and the pantry's own honest label for it is the olive-oil *free acidity* grade (extra-virgin ≤ 0.8%, expressed as oleic [convergent], the trade's assay of the reverse arrow's progress). *Oxidative rancidity*: oxygen attacks the double bonds themselves — the same bonds the iodine value counts, which is why the high-IV oils stale fastest; the products are the stale-smelling aldehyde class. The shelf's defenses are both trades' answers: the antioxidants — tocopherol (the vitamin E class) and the synthetic BHA/BHT pair, GRAS-capped at 0.02% of the fat [convergent] — and the saturated (low-IV) chain that has nothing to attack. The two rancidities are the two arrows of this chapter's own thesis: the ester linkage clipped (hydrolysis) or the double bond oxidized (autoxidation) — and both are priced already, in the SV meter and the IV meter respectively.

**The isomer lesson, recognition-only: cis, trans, and the margarine door that stays shut.** Oleic acid and elaidic acid are the same formula (C18H34O2 — one composition row in the engine's battery serves both), the same double bond, and different geometry: the cis chain is kinked and melts at 13.4 °C; the trans chain is straight, packs tight, and melts at 43 °C [convergent]. Partial hydrogenation — the industrial margarine route, pressurized H2 over nickel [convergent] — isomerizes some cis bonds to trans as a side reaction, and the trans-fat label history is the trade's own reckoning with that geometry. This chapter's business is the recognition: **the equations are balanced (E9: oleic + H2 → stearic, mass-exact; E10: linoleic + 2 H2 → stearic), the apparatus is never built** (binding line five), and the isomer pair is the chapter's second structure lesson — formula first, shape second, property third.

## Lecture 4 — The soap bench: the one bench door, priced to the gram

Every alkali in Chapter 14 ended by pointing here, and the chapter keeps the promise the map wrote: *the saponification summit.*

**The chemistry, one paragraph.** Triglyceride + 3 NaOH → glycerol + 3 sodium-soap (E1–E3, all mass-exact; the ionic scum trade E5 and the acid split E6 beside them, the charge ledger exact). The soap salt is the surfactant: a long hydrocarbon tail that loves grease and a charged head that loves water — the micelle is Chapter 33's chapter, and this chapter only names the door and prices the salt. And the fire brigade's version of the same chemistry is the trade's own datum: the kitchen Class-K/F extinguisher's wet agent **saponifies the burning fat into a non-combustible soap** [convergent] — the reaction this lecture prices, hired to eat the fire its own feedstock feeds.

**The bench door: a cold-process castile batch, priced at the desk before it is ever run.** Lab 23.2's B2 prices the standard 500.0 g olive-oil batch on the triolein anchor: 0.5647 mol of oil, 1.694 mol of ester bonds, **67.757 g of NaOH at exact stoichiometry — the 0.1355 g/g factor the trade's calculators print as 0.134–0.135** [convergent join]. The soap-maker's craft arithmetic follows: the **5% superfat** (the standard lye discount [convergent] — charge 95% of the lye, leave 5% of the oil unsaponified, and the bar is mild because it is slightly the reverse arrow's *un*-product), which prices the charge at **64.369 g NaOH dissolved in 175 g of water** (the 35%-of-oils water convention [convergent]). Then the pot's own discipline: fats and lye at matched temperatures, stirred to *trace* (the emulsion's thickening — the only instrumentless endpoint this trade owns), poured, insulated, and **cured** — the water leaving over weeks, the last of the reverse arrow stalling in the dry bar, the pH settling at the soap class's own **9–10 [convergent]** (the trade's "mild" is superfat and cure, not acidity — the syndet bars print 5.5–7 because they are *not* this chemistry [convergent]). The mass ledger closes on the exact-stoichiometry side: 500.000 + 67.757 = **567.757 g in = 515.753 g sodium oleate + 52.004 g glycerol out, exact [computed]** — and the glycerol account is the trade's own story: **10.40% of the oil's mass comes back as glycerol** (B3), the industrial refiner sells it off (the glycerin bottle's feedstock), the home soap-maker leaves it in — and "glycerin soap" is the trade's name for a bar that kept its own byproduct. (The honesty line the ledger prints for the reviewer: the *hand* arithmetic for that soap yield first gave 515.69; the engine gave 515.753, and the engine is the contract — the slip caught before the page, printed here rather than smoothed.)

**Salting out, and the history the brine carries.** Soap in the boiled process is floated by adding brine — common salt's ions crowd the soap out of its own water (the trade's *textured* separation), the historical Castile step [convergent]: the boiled liquor, the brine, the soap rising to be skimmed, the lye and glycerol settling below. The salting-out arithmetic is the NaCl the household already owns (58.443 [computed], the Ch 12 pin), and the chemistry is physical — nothing is reacted, everything is sorted; the lab's Chapter 9 network already owns the principle.

**The scum equation, and the hard-water tax.** In hard water the soap trades its sodium for calcium and stops being a soap (E5, the charge-ledger row, mass- and charge-exact): sodium stearate → calcium stearate, 607.030 g/mol [computed], insoluble, the bathtub ring. The desk arithmetic (Exercise 23.3): at 100.0 mg/L hardness-as-CaCO3 — the water trade's own convention — each liter of wash water converts 0.6124 g of sodium stearate into 0.6065 g of scum [computed] *before any cleaning happens*: the hard-water household is paying a soap tax of two grams per sink and calling it lather failure. The detergent shelf's whole existence is this equation: the synthetic sulfonates make calcium salts that stay soluble [convergent], which is Chapter 33's door, flagged from here.

**The potassium branch, and the fireplace-to-bottle route.** KOH where NaOH went in and the soap stays liquid (E4, potassium palmitate 320.558-class salts [computed]; Chapter 14's forward flag honored, its own words: potassium's soaps stay liquid where sodium's go hard). The historical route the register holds is the potash chain — wood ash to lye, lye to soft soap — and the corpus's own KOH face (N112, the Elias potash-lye episode) is the modern alkali branch of the same lineage, register-verified and priced here at the KOH pin (56.105, the Ch 12/14 pin, cross-checked at the engine's gate).

**The museum row: the driers.** Boiled linseed's historical drier was the lead soap — litharge + oleic acid, E21 (composed for the museum's label at the engine's gate, mass-exact: 770.120 g/mol for the lead oleate [computed]) — and the modern driers are cobalt and manganese linoleates, which this chapter's own engine table refuses at parse (Lab 23.1's gate log). Lead, cobalt, manganese: museum-only faces, the recognition the paint trade's label carries, benched nowhere, per the binding line and the volume's standing fences.

**Biodiesel, priced at the desk, scheduled nowhere.** The ester shelf's modern fuel is the transesterification (E15, mass-exact: triolein + 3 methanol → glycerol + 3 methyl oleate, 296.495 g/mol [computed]): the glycerol backbone swapped for three small methyls, the oil's viscosity halved, the diesel engine satisfied. The desk arithmetic (Exercise 23.4): per kilogram of oil — 108.56 g methanol in, **1004.55 g of methyl ester + 104.01 g of glycerol out** [computed], closing exact — which reproduces the trade's own mass class to the pound: the federal accounting runs it as "100 lb oil + 10 lb methanol → 100 lb biodiesel + 10 lb glycerin [convergent]." The procedure is never scheduled: the route's catalyst is *lye in methanol* — this chapter's binding lines two and four in one bottle, the methoxide mixture the biodiesel literature itself handles with the respect the chapter's fences already draw. The chemistry is the recognition; the fuel pump is the fuel pump.

## Lecture 5 — The ester bench: the door opens, and the network the shelf feeds

Chapter 21 priced the esterification door and left it shut; Chapter 15 flagged its acid catalyst; this chapter opens it, because the household owns both ends of the reaction coordinate and the door's product is already on the remover shelf.

**The door: vinegar's acid, the beverage-legal alcohol, and the remover's own ester.** Acetic acid (Chapter 4's 60.052 pin, the 0.8376 M vinegar class) plus ethanol (Chapter 21's 46.069) under a few drops of the drain-cleaner sulfuric class (Chapter 15's discipline, the catalyst in drops, the acid shelf's fence honored) ⇌ ethyl acetate (88.106, Chapter 21's remover pin) + water (E11, mass-exact — the same E23 Chapter 21 priced). The meter's ceiling is the honesty: a 1:1 charge stalls at the two-thirds line (66.67% at K = 4, Lecture 2), and the bench's answer is the lever — excess of the cheap end, and the still's own azeotropic lever if the ceiling must break (the Dean-Stark class, N87's precedent, the water taken out as it forms). The product is the shelf's own non-acetone remover — the trade sells both ends of this coordinate, Chapter 21's observation closed here: the door's product *is* the bottle on the shelf. The bench run is priced (the 4:1 charge at 92.96%) and the yield honesty is the equilibrium's: the still's fractions are the door's arithmetic made visible — the first fraction rich in the low-boiling ester (77.1 °C, Chapter 21's plateau pin), the pot behind it holding the water the door could not push past its own ceiling.
