# Vol II · Chapter 15 — The Mineral Acids: The Strong-Acid Ladder

Chapter 14 built the base end of the reagent network: six white powders, one trade, and the subtraction that made mild into caustic. This chapter builds the other end — the acids — and the symmetry is exact enough to state as a law: **the alkalis were stepped up to along a ladder of carbonates, and the acids are stepped up to along a ladder of their own, rung by rung, from the diluted jugs the world sells to the concentrated reagents the bench needs.** The shelf sells two of the three mineral acids already dissolved (muriatic at 31.45%, sulfuric at the drain-opener's 98% class), sells the third nowhere at all (nitric, the restricted one, the acid the corpus builds entire routes to avoid buying), and sells every one of them at *somebody else's* concentration. The chapter's five questions, in bench order: **what counts as a strong acid at the shelf, where are the walls that no still can cross, which doors lead past them (the gas door, the still), how does a precipitate make an acid out of a salt (P14, the volume's founding pattern), and how do you keep what you have made?** The corpus has been holding this chapter's raw material since the survey's earliest batches: the drain-cleaner sulfuric distilled to reagent grade on a DIY mantle (N02, rebuilt at 540 °C with PTFE tape in N82); the hardware hydrochloric distilled against its azeotrope cap (N83, N46); the titration-and-gravimetric cross-assay of the Rooto jar (N57); the oxalate routes that precipitate sulfuric and nitric from salts (N28, N16, N84); the nitre still with its honest 60–80% band (N27's still, N21's own stated yield); and the drying agents that close the last percent (N86, N87, N97). **The thesis of this chapter, and the hinge of the volume: acid strength is not found, it is climbed to — and every climb is a boundary understood: the azeotrope walls price what the still can do, the gas door and the precipitation engine price what the still cannot, and the keeper's discipline decides whether what you made is still what the label said a season later.** One boundary is drawn before any apparatus is unpacked, and it is the book's binding line for this entire chapter: **nitric acid is benched in this Atlas as a reagent — and nothing else. No nitration procedure appears anywhere in this book; the corpus's one energetic-material face (N75) is cited at metadata level only, through the hazard-recognition lens of Chapter 28, and the lens itself is the lesson.**

Open the cabinet under the sink. Walk the rest of the house and you will not find the third mineral acid anywhere: no shelf in the hardware store, the pharmacy, or the grocery sells nitric acid, and the corpus's own restricted-acid faces record the fact from two continents (N83's Australian 32% muriatic jug, distilled precisely because the stronger acids are gated; N84's European chemist building sulfuric *from copper sulfate* because the bottle is not for sale).

And the absence itself is the third observation: no bench note you can write will make nitric appear on a household shelf, so the question the chapter answers for it is not *which bottle* but *which route*.

That is the whole chapter in one cabinet, and the reading of it needs no mole, no formula, and no apparatus beyond a strip of paper and an honest eye. What the chapter adds to the observation is the machinery the rest of the volume was built to supply: the assays of Chapters 4 and 8 to price what the labels claim, the walls of Chapter 6 to name what no still can cross, the precipitation network of Chapter 9 to build the acids the shelf withholds, the gas discipline of Chapter 10 to handle what fumes, and the redox engine of Chapter 11 to follow the nitrogen cycle that the brown bottle rides. ## Lecture 1 — The acid census: what "strong" means, and what the shelf actually sells

### 1.1 The strong-acid ladder, defined at this bench

The word "strong" in *strong acid* has a precise meaning and a folklore meaning, and the chapter needs the precise one. A strong acid is an acid whose dissociation is, for bench purposes, complete: hydrochloric, nitric, and the first proton of sulfuric all let go of their proton essentially fully in water, and the working model this book has used since Chapter 8 — the model that lets 1 M HCl *be* 1 M in H⁺ — stands on that completeness. The honest fine print, printed because this chapter prints it for every constant it uses: completeness is a very large equilibrium constant, not an infinity. Nitric's own Ka is measurable at about 25 (pKa −1.4 to −1.7 across the tables [convergent band, named]); hydrochloric's is far larger still; sulfuric's second proton is *not* strong at all (Ka₂ ≈ 1.0–1.2×10⁻², pKa 1.92–1.99 [convergent]) — the reason Lab 15.1's bisulfate cross-pin matters, and the reason sodium bisulfate, the "dry acid in a jar" of the pH-lowering aisle, dissolves to a pH of only 0.98–1.02 at one molar (Run 6's band, computed). **The strong-acid ladder of this chapter is a ladder of *concentration*, not of kind**: every acid on it is already "strong" at any dilution; what the bench climbs is the amount of it per liter — from the assayed jug, toward the azeotrope walls, and past them by the doors.

And the rungs below zero are on the ladder too, because the pH arithmetic does not stop at the card's edge. A 10 M hydrochloric solution — roughly the muriatic jug — has pH −1.00 (Lab 15.1's battery, both label bases); the wall acid at 20.2% sits at about −0.78 by the same arithmetic (Run 4's 6.09 M at the wall's own density); a 12 M hardware "concentrated HCl" approaches −1.08; and the sulfuric wall at 98% is past the aqueous pH language entirely (its acidity is measured on a different scale, the chapter's honest flag: past ~18 M sulfuric, the *water* is the solute). The pH card's zero was never a floor; it was a fence, and the fence has a gate the notebook may walk through: **pH = −log[H⁺], and [H⁺] is a concentration, not a rating.** The census's first lesson is that the instrument reads honestly even off its own card, if the arithmetic behind the card is understood.

## Lecture 2 — The walls: azeotropes, and the arithmetic of climbing toward them

### 2.1 The three walls, stated as physics

Distillation fails at a wall, and this chapter has three of them. An **azeotrope** is a mixture that boils at a composition its own vapor preserves: the distillate comes over at the same weight percent as the pot's azeotropic mixture, so no amount of patient boiling moves the residue past that composition. The walls, all three [convergent, web-verified this session]: **hydrochloric and water pinch at 20.2% HCl, boiling at 108.6 °C** — a *maximum-boiling* (negative) azeotrope, hotter than either pure component would boil alone; **sulfuric and water pinch at 98.3% H₂SO₄, boiling at 337–338 °C** (one table family prints 98.54% at 317 °C — the disagreement is named, not laundered, and the Atlas carries the 98.3/337 pair as its working wall); and **nitric and water pinch at 68% HNO₃, boiling at 120.5–121.8 °C** (again maximum-boiling). The sign matters as much as the number: for a *maximum*-boiling azeotrope, the residue of a distillation converges toward the wall from **both** sides — boil acid weaker than the wall and the pot climbs toward 20.2% as the water-rich vapor leaves; boil acid *stronger* than the wall (the laboratory's fuming grades, or the 37% hardware acid) and the early vapor runs HCl-*richer* than the pot, dragging the residue *down* toward the same 20.2%. The wall is an attractor, not a cliff on one side only — this is the convergence rule of the negative azeotrope, and it prices every still run this chapter discusses.

The honest application to this Atlas's own prior chapter: Chapter 6 printed a cut ladder for 100 g of the 32% class under its own stated simplification — the distillate comes over *pinned exactly at the cap* — and the ladder's four rungs (35.0/39.9/43.8/49.7% after 20/40/50/60 g of distillate) are **re-pinned to the digit here as 34.95/39.87/43.80/49.70%** (Lab 15.2's B1 ledger, machine-derived under Chapter 6's own stated model). The direction seam is named before the adversarial review can find it first: below the wall the climb is the true direction (the dilute side's vapor is water-richer than the pot, unambiguously), but the pinned-cap simplification is a *mass-balance* model, not a vapor–liquid-equilibrium datum — the real early vapor over any HCl-rich pot runs HCl-richer than the pinned 20.2%, and the honest chapter prints the model's answer *and* the seam together. The rule stands as Chapter 6 wrote it and as this chapter re-derives it: **on the dilute side, the still climbs toward the wall and never past it; the wall itself is the cleanest fraction a still can ever deliver from the weak side.**

### 2.2 The concentration ledgers, run as bookkeeping

The climb is arithmetic before it is apparatus, and the ledger discipline of Chapters 4/6/14 runs it here. and the ledger discipline of Chapters 4/6/14 runs it here — starting with the question the ledger itself asks first: *which side of the wall is the pot on?* Below the wall the climb is real: from 100 g of a 10% class (the thin end of the hardware world), boiling to the wall, the pot's HCl is 10 g; at the wall the pot holds 10/0.202 = 49.5 g — meaning 50.5 g of water must leave as distillate, and the honest fuel bill for that water (latent heat 2.256 kJ/g, the Ch 6/7 pin) is ~114 kJ of burner time for a jug-sized batch. But the muriatic jug itself starts *above* the wall — 31.45%, and N83's Australian 32% with it — so its still-run is not a climb at all but the purification trade (N83's 20% cap, N46's jug run): under the pinned-cap model the residue converges down toward the wall, and Lab 15.2's B1 prints that model's own rungs from the 32% class with the direction seam named where the model and the negative azeotrope disagree. From battery acid the sulfuric climb is the same ledger one wall over: 100 g of 35% electrolyte holds 35 g of acid, which is 35.61 g of wall-stock at 98.3% — so 64.39 g of water must leave (the pot keeps 0.61 g of it as the wall's own composition), and the fuel bill is 145.3 kJ of latent heat alone (B3). **The lesson both ledgers share: the walls price the still, and the still prices the fuel.**

Past the walls, the honest line is drawn where Chapter 6 drew it and this chapter holds: concentrating sulfuric *past* 98.3% requires driving off the azeotrope's own water at 337 °C — a temperature past the kitchen ceiling by 200 degrees, and the corpus's own practitioners' hazard (N02's DIY mantle run, N82's 540 °C hotplate with PTFE tape and a containment bin, the seized-joint hazard). The Atlas cites these as **corpus knowledge only — priced, not scheduled**: the arithmetic is printed because a complete book prices the door, and the door stays shut at this bench because the Charter prices the burn. The last percent to the wall has a cheaper road: the **drying-agent handoff** honored from Chapter 6 — set a desiccant rung (Lecture 5.3) to pull the final water from 98%-class acid toward the wall's own composition, the same subtraction the desiccant ladder prices for the chemist's drying pistol.

### 2.3 The assay discipline: the assay is the bottle's

Every acid this chapter uses is bought or made at *somebody else's* concentration, so the assay is the first instrument, not the last. Three assays, in ascending order of labor. **The titration**: the Ch 8 carbonate-standard method carried to this bench — N57's own two-part discipline (titration *and* gravimetric, both faces, never one witness alone). **The gravimetric BaSO₄ cross**: precipitate the sulfate as barium sulfate, weigh it, and back-solve — the assay Ex 15.2 works two ways, with the honest fence the Ch 13 bench drew: *barium stays behind Chapter 13's fence at this bench* (solubility is not safety; the reagent is priced in the exercise's arithmetic and not taken out of the jar). **The ladder**: what the assay *found*, priced against what the label *claimed* — Lab 15.2's B7 prints the Rooto class's own honest spread: density-derived readings of 18.75/18.40/18.00 M back-solve to 99.94/98.07/95.94% w/w, against the SDS's own printed 93.19% [convergent]. The spread *is* the lesson: a hygroscopic acid's assay drifts between the factory and the bench (Lecture 5.1 prices the drift), so **the assay is the bottle's, never the class's** — titrate YOUR jug, weigh YOUR jar, and date the number. The Chapter 1 rule arrives at the acid bench wearing its most expensive coat: a label is a claim, and the notebook is the only instrument that can cash it.

### 2.4 Dilution: the exotherm is the rule's arithmetic

Making an acid *weaker* is the most dangerous operation in this chapter, and the reason is a number. Dissolving sulfuric in water releases about 95 kJ/mol on the full hydration path [convergent; the first-molar-step band runs 86–90 kJ/mol], and the bench case prices it exactly (Lab 15.2's B8): taking 1.000 mol of sulfuric from the 98% wall-stock (100.07 g = 54.39 mL of the 18.386 M acid) into 945.6 g of water gives a 1045.7 g batch of ~1 M acid whose temperature, if nothing is lost, rises **19.7–20.6 K** on the all-water heat capacity — and 22.8–23.9 K on the ~3.6 J/g/K the hot strong solution itself may present [flagged sketch, the second basis named]. Twenty degrees is the difference between a quiet bench and a spitting one: **acid into water, always, slowly, stirring, outdoors** — never water into acid, because the first touch of water lands on concentrated acid and flashes to steam at the interface, and the spit-back carries bulk acid with it. The quench at disposal is priced with the same arithmetic (B8's tail): 10 mL of the wall-stock splashed into bicarbonate demands 30.89 g of NaHCO₃ and delivers **9.01 L of CO₂** in a hurry — the surge is why the rule *is* a rule, why the quench is an open-vessel, small-portions, sink-rinse discipline, and why the Chapter 8 never-combinations are re-pinned at the acid bench: acid never meets bleach, never meets the drain-opener's other families, and never meets a closed vessel.

## Lecture 3 — The doors past the wall: the gas door, and the nitre still

### 3.1 The gas door: the wall is only a wall for liquids

The still stops at 20.2%, but the *gas* never promised to obey it. Hydrogen chloride is a gas at room temperature that water drinks greedily — about 720 g of HCl per liter of cold water at the ceiling [convergent] — and the 37% "fuming" hardware acid is made in the world's plants by exactly the door this section prices: **dry HCl gas, dissolved into wall-stock acid**. The ledger (Lab 15.2's B2): into 100 g of 20.2% wall acid, dissolving 26.667 g of dry gas reaches exactly 37.00% — the wall beaten by 17 points of concentration, no still required. The gas is made the Mannheim way, the salt-spirit pair the ledger gates as E1 (gentle: NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl) and E2 (driven: 2 NaCl + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2 HCl), and a 30 g salt charge runs the arithmetic of a real generator: 0.513321 mol of salt → 18.716 g of HCl = **12.58 L of gas at the 24.5 L/mol bench molar volume** (the Ch 10 pin), which dissolved into that same 100 g of wall acid would land it at 32.78% — one generator charge, one honest door.

The apparatus rules are the chemistry of the gas itself. HCl fumes: the generator, the drying train, and the receiver live **outdoors or in a real draft, never sealed** — pressure is not a friend of a fuming gas generator, and the corpus's chlorine-train discipline (N77's gas-handling face, cited at its register level as the do-not-attempt boundary it is) prices the whole class of apparatus: gases are handled *flowing*, with the exit into water or fume, never with a closed lid. The drying train runs one-way (gas in, water out of reach); the receiver is guarded by the **suck-back trap** the Ch 10 gas chapters made standing apparatus — a low loop or empty trap between the drying train and the receiver, so that when the generator cools and its pressure drops, the receiver's water climbs into the trap instead of into the hot acid generator, where it would arrive as steam and spit the charge back through every joint. The door is cheap, and it is also the chapter's first reminder that **the binding rules are not decoration: every one of them is somebody's remembered incident** priced as a sentence.

### 3.2 The nitre still: the route the shelf gated

Nitric is the acid the shelf withholds, and the corpus's honest route to it is a distillation the chapter's ledger prices whole. The equation is E3 (the nitre still proper: NaNO₃ + NaHSO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + HNO₃), the potassium still E17 its twin (KNO₃ + H₂SO₄ → KHSO₄ + HNO₃) — and the confession printed before any apparatus is unpacked: **the in-pot equilibrium is hopeless**. Lab 15.1's byproduct attack prices E3's own constant at K = Ka₂(H₂SO₄)/Ka(HNO₃) ≈ **4.8×10⁻⁴ (2.4×10⁻⁴ at the band's other end)** — a reaction that, left to its own pot, barely starts. The still is not an option bolted onto the route; the still *is* the route: HNO₃ is the most volatile thing in the pot (its azeotrope boils at 120.5–121.8 °C, far under the pot's salt-melt temperatures), so **the distillation does the work — Le Chatelier by steam**: every molecule of acid that leaves the pot as vapor drags the equilibrium forward, and the receiver, chilled in an ice bath, keeps what arrives. The apparatus line is absolute and the corpus stands behind it (N27's bisulfate still, N26's waste-recovery still — the register's own NO₂-avoidance framing, *much better than dealing with toxic nitrogen dioxide*, transcript-level): **all glass, no cork, no rubber** — nitric at strength attacks every organic joint and gasket; the joints are ground glass, the receiver is chilled, and the whole rig stands outdoors, because the acid's own brown gas is the failure mode the design exists to avoid.

The ledger (B5): 50 g of sodium nitrate is 0.588277 mol; the bisulfate demand is 70.62 g; the theoretical acid is 37.07 g HNO₃ = **54.51 g of the 68% wall solution**; the residue is 83.56 g of Na₂SO₄ — a byproduct Chapter 14's thrift culture prices as a feedstock (the N57 filtrate-reuse precedent), not a waste. And the honest band, carried from the corpus rather than laundered: the practitioners' own yield is **60–80% of theoretical** (the corpus's honest route-class band — N21's stated yield for its own route, transcript-level, carried to the still as the honest expectation), so the bench expects 33–44 g of wall acid from the 50 g charge, and the notebook books the band, not the ceiling. Ex 15.5 walks the potassium twin at summit scale, and the residue (KHSO₄, the acid salt) is kept: it is the *feed* of the E3 route and the desiccant shelf's neighbor, the keeper's loop the chapter's last lecture maps.

### 3.3 The NOx cycle, and the brown bottle

The acid that comes over clean does not stay clean, and the chapter's redox spine (Chapter 11's engine, re-applied) explains the yellow. Nitric decomposes in light and warmth by E10 (4 HNO₃ → 4 NO₂ + O₂ + 2 H₂O), and the nitrogen dioxide *is* the yellow-brown the old bottle wears: dissolved NO₂ colors the acid, and a 1% decomposition in a 100 g bottle of the 68% wall acid puts 0.4965 g of NO₂ into it — 0.496% by weight, and 0.264 L of the brown gas poised to leave if the cap fails (Ex 15.6 prices the drift). The cycle's other faces are the metallurgy of the acid bench: on copper, the concentrated acid attacks with brown fumes by E11 (Cu + 4 HNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2 NO₂ + 2 H₂O) while the dilute acid attacks with the quiet colorless gas by E12 (3 Cu + 8 HNO₃ → 3 Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2 NO + 4 H₂O) — the *same metal, different products, concentration decides*, which is Chapter 11's redox engine wearing its most bench-visible costume. Zinc follows the dilute route (E13), and silver yields to nitric alone (E15, the 3 Ag + 4 HNO₃ → 3 AgNO₃ + NO + 2 H₂O of the classic silver work) — the faces of Chapters 11 and 12 re-pinned at the acid bench. And the reclaim closes the loop: E14 (4 NO₂ + O₂ + 2 H₂O → 4 HNO₃) is E10 run backward — the brown bottle's own chemistry is reversible in principle, which is why the industrial acid plant *is* this cycle at scale, and why the bench that must reclaim yellowed acid (Ex 15.6's E14 question) is running the plant's own absorption step in a jam jar.

One boundary is restated here, in the section where nitric is most at home, because the chapter's honesty demands the line live where the temptation would: **nitric acid is benched in this Atlas as a reagent — a solvent, an oxidizer, a copper-etchant, an assay tool — and nothing else. No nitration procedure appears anywhere in this book.** The corpus's one energetic-material face (N75, the guncotton video) is cited at metadata level only, through Chapter 28's hazard-recognition lens, and the lens *is* the lesson: the gate on the shelf and the line in this book exist because the same bottle that etches copper cleanly nitrates organics catastrophically, and the Atlas's readers are taught to recognize which chemistry they are holding *before* the energetic class is ever reached. The binding line is not prudery; it is the assay discipline applied to procedures themselves.

## Lecture 4 — The liberation engine: how a precipitate makes an acid

### 4.1 The subtraction, and the corpus that fired it first

Chapter 14's showpiece subtracted two solubility equilibria and made an alkali from a carbonate; this chapter's showpiece is the same subtraction pointed the other way, and it is the volume's founding pattern (P14) arriving at its own bench. The question sounds impossible when first stated: **can a precipitate pull an acid out of a salt?** The copper face of it: copper sulfate solution meets oxalic acid, and copper oxalate — a solid with no taste for water — crashes out, leaving sulfuric acid behind in the liquor (E4: CuSO₄ + H₂C₂O₄ → CuC₂O₄ + H₂SO₄). The driving-force arithmetic is Chapter 14's subtraction verbatim, two equilibrium constants divided (Lab 15.1's Run 5): the oxalic acid's full deprotonation product Kox = Ka₁·Ka₂ = 2.8837×10⁻⁶, and each precipitate's Ksp — so for copper, **K(Cu) = Kox/Ksp(CuC₂O₄) = 2.8837×10⁻⁶/2.87×10⁻⁸ = 100.477**, and for calcium **K(Ca) = Kox/Ksp(CaC₂O₄) = 2.8837×10⁻⁶/2.7×10⁻⁹ = 1068.033**.
