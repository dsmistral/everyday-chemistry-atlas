# Vol II · Chapter 21 — The Alcohol Family and Organic Solvents: The Flammable Shelf, Two Groups, One Flame Line

## Lecture 0 — The flammable shelf: eight trades that sell two functional groups and never say "flammable"

Chapter 14 built the alkali shelf; Chapter 15 climbed the mineral acids; Chapter 17 walked sulfur's ladder; Chapter 18 read the halogens; Chapter 19 read the metals; Chapter 20 read the one element the household breathes and never buys. This chapter reads the shelf the household owns as *liquids* — and it opens with the observation that makes the organic shelf a chapter rather than a list: **every active liquid the trades sell is built on one of two functional groups — the hydroxyl (–OH) or the carbonyl (C=O) — and every one of them is more flammable than the household that owns it behaves.** The rubbing-alcohol bottle is a hydroxyl on three carbons. The sanitizer is a hydroxyl on two. The tincture, the vanilla, the mouthwash, the lamp's charge — all the same two-carbon hydroxyl, at six different strengths. The remover bottle is a carbonyl on three carbons. The paint thinner is a carbonyl on four. The garage's washer fluid is a hydroxyl on one carbon — the one that blinds. The antifreeze is a hydroxyl on two, twice. Eight trades, one shelf, two groups — and the property that outranks every other property the shelf owns is printed on none of the labels in plain words: **the flash point**, the temperature above which the liquid's vapor is ready to burn.

The reason the labels can stay quiet is the reason this chapter exists. **It is the vapor that burns, not the liquid** — Chapter 6's rule, and this is the shelf where that rule is the whole safety architecture. A cup of acetone at room temperature is not a liquid sitting there; it is a liquid *manufacturing an ignitable atmosphere over its own surface*, because its flash point (−20 °C [convergent]) is below every room it will ever stand in. The shelf's vapors are heavier than air — ethanol's is 1.590× air, acetone's 2.005×, isopropanol's 2.074× [computed from Chapter 10's air molar mass, 28.969] — so they *pour off the bottle, run along the bench, and pool at floor level*, hunting for a flame nobody has lit yet. This is the chapter's thesis, and it is the volume's hinge stated a seventh time: **the trades package the flammability for you, and the bench's only job is to never give the vapor an appointment.** The never-potable line, the never-mix line, the peroxide-former line, and the toxic-alcohol line are the four fences around that one rule, and the rule itself is Chapter 6's, inherited verbatim: **no open flame anywhere in the room — water bath or hot plate only** [Chapter 6, its own safety row].

Sit at the kitchen table with the notebook and pour nothing. The pencil-and-paper census of the flammable shelf, before any apparatus:

- **The pharmacy trade: the rubbing-alcohol bottle.** *Isopropyl alcohol 70% v/v* — a three-carbon hydroxyl sold as a skin antiseptic, with the trade's own honest label convention (v/v, not w/w — Lecture 1's first translation). The same shelf holds the hand sanitizers, ethanol at 60–95% [convergent — the CDC's own floor and ceiling], and the mouthwash, 21.6% v/v in the Cool Mint bottle [convergent]. Three pharmacy actives, one functional group.
- **The kitchen trade: the vanilla bottle.** The FDA's own standard of identity: vanilla extract is **at least 35% v/v ethanol** [convergent] — the spice trade's solvent, at a strength the pharmacist would call a spirit. The flavoring shelf is a hydroxyl shelf wearing a cookbook's vocabulary.
- **The hardware trade: the thinner cans.** Acetone (the "100% acetone" can and the lacquer thinner behind it) and MEK — methyl ethyl ketone, the four-carbon carbonyl of the paint trade — and the rubber-cement solvent's hexane. One trade, all three of the shelf's carbonyl-plus-alkane faces.
- **The garage trade: the two jugs.** The washer-fluid jug is 30–50% methanol [convergent; the concentrate class runs 90–100%] — a one-carbon hydroxyl whose reputation Lecture 3 prices honestly. The antifreeze jug is ethylene glycol, the two-hydroxyl antifreeze at the trade's own 50/50 mix. The garage is the shelf's *toxic* wing, and it is recognition-only from the first page.
- **The cosmetics trade: the remover bottles.** Nail polish remover is sold in two denominations — *acetone* and *non-acetone* — and the non-acetone bottle is ethyl acetate, a carbonyl-and-ether that the trade sells to people whose nails acetone dries out. The same trade's styrofoam-packing peanut dissolves in the acetone bottle in seconds [convergent] — the datum Lecture 4 reads as polymer chemistry, not as a party trick.
- **The cleaning trade: the denatured bottle.** The hardware store's "denatured alcohol" is ethanol poisoned on purpose: SDA 40-B, the formula printed in the Code of Federal Regulations itself — *to every 100 gallons of alcohol add 1/16 avoirdupois ounce of denatonium benzoate and 1/8 gallon of tert-butyl alcohol* [verbatim, 27 CFR 21.76] — the bitterest substance the trade knows, at a concentration Lecture 3 prices in the never-potable ledger (B5).
- **The fuel trade: the lamp.** The bench's own fuel — Chapter 1's spirit lamp, repriced by Chapter 7 at 29.67 MJ/kg — is this shelf's one sanctioned face: a two-carbon hydroxyl whose flame is cold enough for glassware and clean enough to leave nothing. The lamp is where the whole shelf's flammability is *allowed* to happen, under a discipline, away from every stock bottle.
- **The auto-parts trade: the starting-fluid can.** Diethyl ether, the four-carbon oxygen bridge, in an aerosol can sold for cold mornings — and it is the shelf's one **peroxide-former**: the liquid that reacts with its own oxygen over months to crystals that detonate on distillation [convergent — the peroxide-former classes, the six-month test rule and the 0.5–100 ppm test strips, are the institutional safety literature's own]. The can is in the census; the can is never on the bench; the can's rule is the chapter's fifth fence.

That is eight trades and one supporting cast — the camp-stove fuels, the perfume bases (denatured ethanol again), the marker inks — and the census's quiet observation is the thesis seen as shelf goods: **two functional groups cover every active liquid the household owns, and the two groups share one carbon ladder.** The alcohol's carbon sits at −2 (methanol, ethanol, isopropanol all) or lower fractional states; the ketone's sits at −4/3 (acetone) and −1 (MEK, ethyl acetate); the alkane's at −7/3 (hexane); combustion's carbon exits at +4, in CO₂ — and the whole ladder is one meter, priced in Lecture 2.

One boundary before any apparatus is unpacked, and it is the book's binding line for this entire chapter — six lines, inherited from Chapters 6, 10, 15, and 16 and stated here together because this is the shelf where they all meet. **First: the flame line — no open flame anywhere in the room, water bath or hot plate only** [Chapter 6, verbatim]. Every bench procedure in this chapter is a water-bath procedure; the lamp is lit only at the lamp station, a meter from every stock bottle, after the day's solvents are capped and racked. **Second: never-potable.** Nothing on this shelf is drunk, nothing is made into a beverage, no beverage is fortified, and the denaturants are never removed — Chapter 6's beverage-legality line inherited whole, because this is the shelf the line was written for. **Third: the toxic alcohols are recognition-only.** Methanol's 10 mL is the blindness datum [convergent]; the washer jug holds 151× that much (B5); the EG jug holds ~19 lethal classes at the trade's own 50/50. They are recognized at the label, priced in the ledger, and *never distilled out of their products* — the still is the instrument that would concentrate the poison with the product. **Fourth: never-mix.** Acetone × bleach runs the haloform reaction to chloroform (E17, the NEVER row priced at the meter) — no procedure, no proportion, no "safe amount"; and the wider rule is Chapter 16's: a solvent and an oxidizer are never in the same working area. **Fifth: the peroxide-formers are never benched.** Ether is dated the day it is opened, tested at the institutional classes' own six-month interval, never distilled, and in this book, never bought for the bench at all. **Sixth: the standing prior fences still hold.** The acids stay on Chapter 15's shelf (the esterification door opens in Chapter 23, under the acid discipline, not here); ammonia is a Chapter 10 face; sodium metal is refused (Chapter 16/19) — which is why the ethoxide door of the corpus is recognition-only (E22). The prior volume's fences — thermite-class, lead, the alkali metals, chromium and cobalt — stand unbroken; this chapter touches none of them.

## Lecture 1 — The trade's labels, translated: two "alcohols," the percent conventions, proof, and the poison on purpose

Every flammable-shelf item arrived through a trade, and every trade writes its labels in its own currency. The translation layer is the chapter's first work, and it runs on the same composition engine as always (Lab 21.1, executed in-drafting below).

**The word "alcohol" — the trade's and the chemist's.** The trade's *alcohol* means one compound: ethanol, C₂H₅OH, 46.069 g/mol. The chemist's *alcohol* means a whole family: any carbon chain wearing a hydroxyl group — methanol CH₄O (32.042), ethanol (46.069), isopropanol C₃H₈O (60.096), ethylene glycol C₂H₆O₂ (62.068, a *diol*, two hydroxyls), glycerol C₃H₈O₃ (92.094, a *triol*, three). The label "rubbing alcohol" is the collision of the two meanings in one bottle: it means the *family* (isopropanol, not ethanol), at the *trade's* strength convention (70% v/v), sold where the *chemist's* alcohol (ethanol) would need a liquor license or a poison. The bench reads the label the way Chapter 2 taught: the word is the trade's, the formula is the bench's, and the two only meet in the composition engine.

**The percent conventions: v/v, w/w, and why the trade picks one.** *70% v/v* means 70 mL of the active made up to 100 mL of product — a volume convention, honest to the only thing the retail shelf can do (pour). The w/w convention (Chapter 8's muriatic bottle, Chapter 20's ammonia bottle) is the industrial convention, honest to the only thing a tank car can do (weigh). The two are not interchangeable, and the conversion needs the densities — which is why the pharmacy's v/v and the cleaning trade's w/w have kept their own books all book long, and why B1 converts the pharmacy's v/v to the bench's molarity through ethanol's density pin (0.789 g/mL, Chapter 6's own): a 70% v/v sanitizer is 552.3 g of ethanol per liter, and that is 11.99 M. The same chain, run down the shelf: the tincture's 47% (Chapter 18's label pin) is 8.05 M; the vanilla's 35% is 5.99 M; the mouthwash's 21.6% is 3.70 M; the wine bottle's 12% is 2.06 M — and the whole ladder sits below the azeotrope wall's 16.68 M, which sits below pure ethanol's own 17.13 M. One chain, six bottles, the shelf's whole ethanol account in one column.

**Proof.** The liquor trade's proof is twice the percentage by volume: 80 proof is 40% v/v [trade convention]. The convention's history (the gunpowder test of the British navy — a "proof" spirit igniting damped powder) is the right kind of datum for this shelf: the trade's own flammability test, retired into an arithmetic. The bench's version is B1's row: 80 proof is 6.85 M, run through the same density pin. The Atlas prints the multiplier as a trade convention and does not chase the historical number further.

**SDA 40-B: the poison on purpose, in the CFR's own words.** The denatured bottle is ethanol the tax law has *ruined for drinking* and thereby freed for the workbench, and the ruining is a recipe in the Code of Federal Regulations: *to every 100 gallons of alcohol add 1/16 avoirdupois ounce of denatonium benzoate and 1/8 gallon of tert-butyl alcohol* [verbatim, 27 CFR 21.76]. Denatonium benzoate is the bitterest substance the trade knows — the bitterant of the same family the antifreeze trade uses — and B5 prices the recipe: 1/16 of an ounce is 1.772 g per 100 gallons, which is **4.68 mg per liter**, a concentration at which one sip is unforgettable and a glassful is not survivable as a beverage experience. The tert-butyl alcohol (1/8 gal per 100 gal, 0.125% v/v) is the denaturant's own solvent-partner. The trade's honesty is the reverse of the usual: the label *tells you* it is poisoned, because the poison is the point — the never-potable line, written into federal law before the bench ever inherits the bottle.

**The garage jugs, translated.** The washer-fluid label says *methanol* on the safety sheet and *30–50%* in the trade's own honest range (the freeze-protection ladder: more methanol, lower freeze point). The antifreeze label says *ethylene glycol* and the trade's own mix instruction — 50/50 with water — which is the datum B5 prices as ~19 lethal classes per jug at the commonly cited ~100 mL adult class [tabulated]. The two jugs are the shelf's reason for the third fence: the household owns these liquids *as products*, the bench audits them *as labels*, and the difference between the two is the entire distinction this chapter's discipline draws. Nothing in the garage wing is ever poured, opened, or smelled for confirmation; the label is the instrument.

**The two carbonyl conventions.** The remover shelf's two denominations are both honest labels: *acetone* (the ketone proper, C₃H₆O — the "100% acetone" can is the trade's own purity boast) and *non-acetone* (ethyl acetate, C₄H₈O₂ — an *ester*, a carbonyl with an ether arm, gentler to nails and to many plastics, at 0.902 g/mL against acetone's 0.791). The paint trade's MEK is the second ketone (C₄H₈O, 72.107 g/mol), the slower-evaporating one, and the trade's own choice of which carbonyl goes in which can is evaporation-rate economics — the same property the flash point measures from the safety side. The ketone family's smell is its own label: acetone's sharp sweet, MEK's heavier sweet, both unmistakable, both the nose's one sanctioned use on this shelf (a recognition face at the cap, never a deep inhale).

**The peroxide-former's label, translated.** The starting-fluid can says *ether* and says *extremely flammable* and does not say the one thing the institutional safety literature says for it: that diethyl ether forms peroxides in storage, that the peroxides concentrate exactly where distillation would put them, and that the institutional rule is a date written on the can the day it is opened and a test strip every six months [convergent]. The Atlas's translation is shorter: the can stays in the census, the ether never comes to the bench, and the peroxide-former fence is the chapter's quiet fifth line — the one fence whose *reason* is invisible until it is catastrophic, which is the reason it is a fence and not a suggestion.
## Lecture 2 — The meter on carbon: the ladder from −7/3 to +4, and why the shelf's vapors are its whole danger

Chapter 20's meter priced one element through its oxidation states. This chapter's meter does the same for carbon, and the ladder it climbs is the shortest in the book: from the alkane's −7/3 to the alcohol's −2 to the ketone's −4/3 and the ester's −1, one step at a time, with combustion's +4 waiting at the top of every rung. The arithmetic is the engine's (Lab 21.1's meter block), and the oxidation states are the bookkeeping you can do at the kitchen table: assign hydrogen +1 and oxygen −2, and make the molecule sum to zero. Hexane C₆H₁₄: 6C + 14 = 0, so C = −7/3 = −2.333. Methanol, ethanol, isopropanol: the single oxygen and the OH's hydrogen leave every carbon at −2. Acetone C₃H₆O: 3C + 6 − 2 = 0, so C = −4/3 = −1.333. MEK and ethyl acetate: C = −1. Glycerol's three carbons: −2/3. And the exit: CO₂, where C = +4, fully paid out.

**The combustion meter, nine rows.** Every fuel the shelf owns, priced the Chapter 7 way (ΔHcomb [convergent tabulations; MEK and glycerol computed from their ΔHf° values; ethylene glycol printed at −1198 with its cross-source band −1198 to −1203 named]) — converted to kJ per gram and MJ per kilogram by the engine, with the oxygen bill and the carbon's starting state beside them:

| fuel | M (g/mol) | ΔHc (kJ/mol) | kJ/g | O₂ (g/g fuel) | C oxidation state |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| hexane C₆H₁₄ | 86.178 | −4163 | 48.31 | 3.5274 | −2.333 |
| isopropanol C₃H₈O | 60.096 | −2051 | 34.13 | 2.3960 | −2 |
| MEK C₄H₈O | 72.107 | −2444 | 33.89 | 2.4407 | −1 |
| acetone C₃H₆O | 58.080 | −1790 | 30.82 | 2.2037 | −1.333 |
| ethanol C₂H₅OH | 46.069 | −1367 | 29.67 | 2.0837 | −2 |
| ethyl acetate C₄H₈O₂ | 88.106 | −2231 | 25.32 | 1.8159 | −1 |
| methanol CH₄O | 32.042 | −726 | 22.66 | 1.4979 | −2 |
| ethylene glycol C₂H₆O₂ | 62.068 | −1198 | 19.30 | 1.2888 | −1 |
| glycerol C₃H₈O₃ | 92.094 | −1654 | 17.96 | 1.2161 | −0.667 |

The table's first lesson is Chapter 7's, seen from the organic side: **the oxygenates discount themselves.** Hexane, the one fuel on the shelf with no oxygen of its own, is also the densest at 48.31 MJ/kg; every oxygenate carries part of its own oxygen bill as dead weight — ethanol is "a third of its mass is oxygen it brought along" (34.73% re-derived, Chapter 7's own RA-6 clarification), glycerol is half oxygen-adjacent (52% by its O₃), and the MJ/kg column falls as the oxygen fraction rises. The ladder's oxygen content is the energy column's whole explanation.

**The meter's second lesson: invert the basis, and the ranking inverts at its edges.** Price the same nine fuels *per gram of oxygen consumed* instead of per gram of fuel (kJ per g O₂: methanol 15.13, ethylene glycol 14.98, glycerol 14.77, isopropanol 14.25, ethanol 14.24, acetone 13.99, ethyl acetate 13.94, MEK 13.89, hexane 13.69), and the mass-ranking's two ends trade places: hexane, the mass winner, is the *weakest* fuel per gram of air it must breathe; methanol, nearly the mass loser, is the *strongest* per gram of oxygen. The mechanism is the H/C factor, and it is pure elemental bookkeeping: hydrogen's combustion pays 2 × 241.8 kJ per 32.0 g of O₂ = 15.1 kJ per gram of oxygen (to steam), carbon's pays 393.5/32.0 = 12.3 — so *per oxygen*, a hydrogen-rich fuel outranks a carbon-rich one, and methanol (effectively H/C = 4) sits near hydrogen's own rate while hexane (H/C = 2.33) sits near carbon's. The two bases answer two different questions — *what to carry* (mass basis) and *what a fire must breathe* (oxygen basis) — and the honest meter prints both, because the shelf's safety story is written in the second one: a solvent fire in a closed room is an *air* account, and the oxygen column says the room empties faster than the fuel column suggests.

**The flammability table: the property the shelf never prints.** The flash point is the temperature at which the liquid's vapor over its surface reaches the concentration that will ignite from a flame — the closed-cup datum of the safety literature, the single number that says *when this liquid becomes its own hazard*:

| liquid | flash point (°C) | bp (°C) | vapor density (× air) |
|---|---|---|---|
| diethyl ether | −45 [tabulated] | 34.6 | 2.56 |
| hexane | −26 | 68.7 | 2.98 |
| acetone | −20 | 56.1 | 2.005 |
| MEK | −9 | 79.6 | 2.49 |
| ethyl acetate | −4 | 77.1 | 3.04 |
| methanol | 11 | 64.7 | 1.11 |
| isopropanol | 12 | 82.6 | 2.074 |
| ethanol | 13 | 78.37 | 1.590 |
| ethylene glycol | 111 | 197.5 | 2.14 |
| glycerol | 160 | 290 | 3.17 |
| water (the control) | none | 100.00 | 0.62 |

Read the column the way a firefighter reads it. **Every flash-liquid on the shelf has a flash point below room temperature** — the eight from ether's −45 to ethanol's 13 all stand in a 20 °C room manufacturing ignitable vapor *right now* — and the two exceptions are the two the household thinks of as "not very flammable": ethylene glycol (111 °C) and glycerol (160 °C), whose flash points sit above the boiling point of water, the datum that makes the antifreeze jug a *toxic* problem rather than a *fire* problem, and makes glycerol the shelf's safest fuel (17.96 MJ/kg of potential, parked behind a 160 °C door). The vapor-density column is the other half of the danger: every one of the shelf's vapors is heavier than air (1.11× for methanol up past 3× for ethyl acetate — all computed at M/28.969, Chapter 10's air molar mass), so they do not rise and disperse; they *descend and pool*, running along benches and floors to floor level, meters from the bottle that released them. Flash point says *when*; vapor density says *where*; together they are Chapter 6's rule with its mechanism showing: the vapor travels, so the flame rule is about rooms, not about bottles.

**The gate refusals, and the recognition face that remains.** The meter would love to price the two classical oxidations of the shelf: the dichromate breathalyzer (ethanol → acetic acid over Cr(VI), orange to green, the roadside instrument of a generation) and the permanganate oxidation (the purple stain that consumes alcohol as it fades). Both are refused at the gate, and the refusal is Chapter 15/16's line standing on this shelf: chromium(VI) and permanganate are oxidizers, this is the solvent shelf, and **a solvent and an oxidizer are never in the same working area** — the never-mix line's widest form. What remains is the recognition face, and the bench keeps it at exactly that level: the breathalyzer's chemistry is read (ethanol's −2 carbon climbing to acetic acid's 0 under the dichromate's +6 falling to +3, the color change the instrument counted, at a blood-alcohol threshold of 0.08% the trade prints and this book prices nowhere) and benched nowhere. The old instrument is the meter's one refused row: named, explained, refused.

## Lecture 3 — The ethanol bench: the bottle audit, the wall, the classical breakers, and the lamp

The ethanol account is the shelf's biggest, and the bench audits it the way Chapter 8 audited the ammonia bottle: from the label up, through the density pin, to the molarity the label hides. Lab 21.2 B1 runs the whole shelf in one column — sanitizer 11.99 M, tincture 8.05 M, vanilla 5.99 M, mouthwash 3.70 M, wine 2.06 M, 80-proof 6.85 M — and the audit's quiet result is that **the household's whole ethanol inventory, every trade of Lecture 0 included, is one compound at six strengths**, and the strongest thing on the shelf that money and the law allow is not the strongest thing the shelf *could* be: 16.68 M, the azeotrope wall, against pure ethanol's own 17.13 M.

**The wall, repriced.** Chapter 6's wall is the ethanol-water azeotrope at 95.6 wt% (x = 0.89470, ten places, the Ch 6 RA-2-corrected value), the composition where water and ethanol evaporate *together* and no amount of patient distillation crosses. This chapter converts the wall into the bench's currency, and the chain is the engine's own (Lab 21.1's pin block): x = 0.89470 → 95.60 wt% → at the azeotrope's specific gravity 0.804 [Chapter 6's own handbook pin], one liter of wall-strength ethanol is 804 g of solution = **768.6 g of ethanol + 35.4 g of water = 16.68 M** — the wall, priced as a bottle. The back-conversion round-trips (95.60/46.069 over the same over 4.40/18.015 gives x = 0.89470 again — gate G5), and the round trip is the audit's proof that the two chapters are describing the same liquid with the same arithmetic.

**The freeze ladder and the classical breaker.** How does the trade get *above* the wall — the "absolute" ethanol the ethoxide video's 99% label gestures at [N43, description-level]? Chapter 6 named the wall's breakers and refused them at household scale (the benzene azeotrope, the drying trains); this chapter prices the one classical breaker that is honestly within the Atlas's premise: **quicklime.** Calcium oxide drinks water to calcium hydroxide (E19, CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂), irreversibly — the water is *consumed*, not just held — and the bench's price is B4's: 0.32125 g of water consumed per gram of CaO. A liter of wall-strength ethanol carries 35.4 g of water; drying it with CaO wants ~110 g of lime (35.4/0.32125), a reflux over the fresh oxide, a decant, and the trade's own classical route to near-absolute [convergent — the classical procedure; the Atlas prices it and leaves the running of it to the still chapter's discipline and to the "never needed" verdict: the bench's extractions and the lamp do not care about the wall, and the one door that does (the ethoxide) is refused anyway]. The lime cycle is Chapter 14's own door walking over here: the calcination that made the oxide (43.97% mass loss, the Ch 14 pin) is the same atom ledger the drying now closes, and the Ca(OH)₂ byproduct is the mortar chapter's own slaked lime.

**The trap in the drying drawer: why CaCl₂ cannot dry an alcohol.** The classical drying-agent list says calcium chloride; the alcohol shelf says no, and the reason is one of the book's cleanest structure-property lessons: **calcium chloride does not just absorb water — it *dissolves into ethanol* and forms a crystalline addition compound, CaCl₂·4C₂H₅OH** (295.260 g/mol, the engine's own row) [convergent — the classical qualitative-analysis datum; the 2CaCl₂·7C₂H₅OH variant also reported]. The compound is real enough to have a melting point and a stoichiometry, and its meaning is written in B4's ledger: per gram of CaCl₂, the salt can *drink* 1.6604 g of ethanol — it is a sponge for the very thing you are trying to dry. This is why the bench's drying agent for anything alcoholic is magnesium sulfate: **Epsom salt, oven-dried** (N86's own episode — the store-bought Epsom salt baked at the dehydration ladder 110 → ~250 °C [register-verified]), whose anhydrous MgSO₄ takes 1.0477 g of water per gram (the heptahydrate, E21) and *cannot* drink an alcohol at all. The drying-pistol class (N97's desiccant train, the CaCl₂ in the barrel where its water-appetite is the whole point and no alcohol is present) is the recognition face of the same datum [register-verified]. One drawer, two salts, and the choice between them is the shelf's own chemistry: water-drinker or alcohol-drinker.

**The esterification door.** Ethanol's second bench face is the one that opens on Chapter 23: acetic acid + ethanol ⇌ ethyl acetate + water (E23), the trade's own non-acetone remover written forward as a reaction. The door is priced here (the equilibrium is Chapter 24's homework — [flagged sketch] at this chapter's level) and *opened* nowhere: the acid catalyst belongs to Chapter 15's discipline and the water removal the equilibrium demands is the Dean-Stark class this book has already priced at the oxalic-acid episode. The chapter's honest gesture at the door is B3's ledger: the remover shelf's two denominations are the *product* of this reaction (ethyl acetate) and the *feedstock's sister* (acetone) — the shelf sells both ends of the same reaction coordinate.

**The lamp.** The chapter closes the ethanol bench where it opened the book: at the spirit lamp, Chapter 1's own instrument, lit under Chapter 6's flame line at the lamp station a meter from every stock bottle. B2 prices the charge honestly: 5 mL of ethanol is 3.945 g, which is **117.1 kJ** at 29.673 kJ/g (Chapter 7's own row, re-derived at full precision), which boils away **51.89 g** of water already at 100 °C at water's own latent heat (2256 J/g, Chapter 6/7's pin). One charge, one tea-cup's worth of boil-away, a clean cold blue flame a lid extinguishes — the shelf's whole flammable energy budget, spent in the one place the six fences allow it to burn.
## Lecture 4 — The ketone bench: the one honest bench door, the synthesis face, and the NEVER row

The carbonyl shelf's bench story is shorter than the ethanol bench's, and its honesty is the point: this chapter schedules **exactly one** door onto the bench — a purification, not a synthesis — and reads every other face of the ketone family at recognition level, because each one is either behind a fence (the haloform), someone else's premise (the ketazine), or the tech tree's business (the dry distillation).

**The one honest bench door: N125's acetone from nail polish remover.** The Canadian Chemist's episode is this chapter's bench door almost verbatim as the corpus filmed it: the "100% acetone" remover bottle is *already* the pure reagent wearing a cosmetics label — and the episode's own premise (distill the remover to bench-grade acetone) is the one distillation on this shelf that is honest, legal, safe to run, and worth the glassware [N125, register-verified at description level — the method unpinned, the episode the premise's face]. The Atlas's version runs under the flame line in its entirety: **water bath or hot plate only, never a flame in the room** (Chapter 6's rule verbatim — acetone's −20 °C flash point is the rule's own reason), a simple still, small charge, the receiver capped, the room ventilated. B3 prices the run: 100 mL of remover at ρ 0.791 is 79.1 g = 1.3619 mol of acetone, and the plateau is acetone's own 56.1 °C — the pot thermometer reads the product's identity as it comes over (the bp cut is the recognition instrument, P9's own face). The "non-acetone" bottle run in the same ledger is the control: 100 mL at 0.902 g/mL is 90.2 g = 1.0238 mol of ethyl acetate, plateau 77.1 °C. Two denominations, two plateaus, one still, and the shelf's whole remover account audited in two runs.

**The synthesis face, read not run: N123's dry distillation.** The corpus's other acetone episode is the *making* face, and it belongs to the tech tree rather than the bench: NileRed's calcium-acetate pyrolysis, **Ca(C₂H₃O₂)₂ → CaCO₃ + acetone** (E18, balanced, the ketone-by-pyrolysis class) [N123, register-verified — the Tums prior-episode chain, the honest-quality stance in the description's own words].
