## Lecture 0 — The Soil Census (a jar of dirt, read like a label)

The garden's first question is the book's first question: **what do you actually have?** The premise answers it with the premise's own instrument — a jar.

Fill a straight-sided jar one-third with soil, top it with water, shake hard, and let it settle overnight. In the morning the soil has introduced itself in layers: the grit that fell first (sand), the flour that took an hour (silt), the clay that stained the water and took the night, and the dark flecks still floating on top — the organic matter, the only fraction light enough to refuse the water entirely. Read the layers as percentages by height and you have the mineral skeleton's census; what settles where is size, and size is chemistry by another name: the skeleton is overwhelmingly the silica family — SiO₂, 60.083 g/mol at this book's table (Ch 30's mineral pin) — wearing grain sizes.

The jar test is **P9, instrumentless verification**, in its purest household form: no instrument, one evening, and a measurement that repeats. The garden's second instrument is older still: the plant itself, yellowing low (nitrogen hungry), purple-leafed (the trade's own mnemonic, recognition-only here), or simply silent. This chapter builds the arithmetic behind both readings.

**What the fertilizer label is.** The sack with three numbers (N-P-K) is an *elemental* census, not a compound list — the trap Chapter 2 taught at the muriatic shelf and Chapter 20 taught at the fertilizer aisle: the label declares elements; the chemist reads compounds. A bag whose first number is 46 is urea — CO(NH₂)₂, 60.056 g/mol, carrying nitrogen at 46.65% by this book's own table (Ch 20's census, battery row L1); the label's 46 is the compound's true nitrogen rounded to whole numbers by trade law. The elemental style is not a lie; it is a convention — and conventions are exactly what Chapter 14's potash arithmetic already taught you to convert in your head.

And one law before any arithmetic: **phosphorus is refused at this book's element table.** The middle number on the sack — the P of N-P-K — is read as trade prose and nothing more: bone meal, superphosphate, the DAP jar's 46 face, all recognition-only, their labels copied into the notebook verbatim and never computed (Lecture 3 prints the refusal as taught content, and the engine's gate log executes it three times). A chemist who cannot verify a number is honest enough to say so, and this book has said so since Chapter 20.

**The idea of this lecture, alone:** a soil is a ledger of what is present and what is missing — and the garden's entire chemistry is the bookkeeping of deficits.

## Lecture 1 — The Nitrogen Web

Nitrogen is the garden's currency, and it circulates the way currency does: minted somewhere far away, taxed at every exchange, and hoarded by the organisms that can make it.

**Urea: the one bag the ruined world still understands.** Industrial urea is the Haber world's product — ammonia and carbon dioxide reacted under heat and pressure — and this book's verdict on that feedstock was rendered in Chapter 20 and stands inherited: **BUY.** The kitchen cannot make the feeds (Chapter 36 drew exactly this edge as the tree's one industrial corridor), so the urea bag is read as a terminal import, priced per mole of nitrogen at its own label face: 46.65% N (L1; the label rounds to 46).

In the soil the bag does one thing before the plant sees anything: it hydrolyzes. **CO(NH₂)₂ + H₂O → 2 NH₃ + CO₂** — mass identity 780,710 myu EXACT at this book's table (B1; the same corridor Chapter 36 certified in both directions, its E3/E7 class). The ammonia is not lost; it is the web's next node.

**Nitrification: the plant's preferred face.** The ammonia oxidizes in two microbial steps to nitrate — the form most roots actually drink. The bookkeeping leg is **NH₃ + 2 O₂ → HNO₃ + H₂O**, 810,270 myu EXACT (B3), and the formal nitrate→nitrite reduction leg is carried as **2 HNO₃ = 2 HNO₂ + O₂**, 1,260,240 myu EXACT — a **[flagged model]**: bookkeeping-exact, with the biology named in prose (the Nitrosomonas/Nitrobacter relay) and the mechanism expressly disclaimed. The web's shape so far: urea → ammonia → nitrate, each arrow a balance row, each row owned by the battery.

**The franchisees.** The atmosphere is four-fifths N₂ (28.014 g/mol, Ch 10's pin), and the triple bond that makes it inert is priced at 945 kJ·mol⁻¹ — Chapter 20's own fixation row (+0.27 V, −156.3 kJ per mole of hydrogen equivalent) — which is why the kitchen never fixes nitrogen and the legume's root nodules do: the franchise is held by bacteria that pay the bond's price in sugar. The ruined-world gardener does not compete with the franchise; the gardener plants it (beans, clover, alder) and lets the ledger credit itself.

**Biuret: the bag's own shadow.** Heat urea past its decomposition edge — above roughly 132 °C [convergent] — and two molecules condense: **2 CO(NH₂)₂ → C₂H₅N₃O₂ + NH₃**, 1,201,120 myu EXACT (B2). The product, biuret — NH₂CONHCONH₂, 103.081 g/mol at this book's table (N1, this chapter's one new composition) — is the fertilizer trade's own seedling-safety quality limit: below half a percent the trade stamps the bag fit; above one percent it is refused for seedlings, seed beds, and foliar spray [convergent]. The garden-chemist's lesson is the one the trade learned expensively: **the urea bag must never sit by the kiln.** And the engine catches the mis-typed version of the same lesson — the condensation written one hydrogen short — at a gap of 10,080 myu (B8, CAUGHT): the table is the contract in both directions.

**The idea of this lecture, alone:** garden nitrogen is a web with three sources — bought (Haber's bag), planted (the legume franchise), and recycled (the compost pile, next lecture) — and every arrow on it is a balance row.

## Lecture 2 — The C:N Ledger (the pile that heats itself)

The compost pile is the ruined world's fertilizer factory, and it runs on one ratio. The microorganisms that eat the pile want carbon for energy and nitrogen for biomass at roughly **30 parts carbon to one part nitrogen** [convergent: the Cornell composting canon, registered session 110] — which as a fraction of the whole is nitrogen at 1/31 = 3.226% of C+N (C1). The gardener's entire blend arithmetic is: price every ingredient's C:N, then steer the pile to 30:1.

**The blend.** Grass clippings run about 45% carbon and 2.4% nitrogen (18.75:1) and sawdust about 480 and 1.2 (400:1) [convergent: Cornell's own band inputs]. Per kilogram of grass (450 g C, 24 g N) the pile needs x kilograms of sawdust (480 g C, 1.2 g N each) such that the total hits 30:1:

- 450 + 480x = 30 × (24 + 1.2x)
- 444x = 270
- **x = 270/444 = 0.608 kg sawdust per kilogram of grass** (C2).

That is the whole method: one linear equation per blend, solvable in the notebook, no instruments. Autumn leaves (about 50% C and 0.75% N — 66.7:1, Cornell's own figures) are too carbon-heavy to fix by blending alone at garden scale, so the ledger's other lever applies instead: **the urea credit.** A 100 kg leaf pile (50,000 g C, 750 g N) at 30:1 wants one-thirtieth as much nitrogen as its carbon; the deficit is 2750/3 g of N, and urea at its own 46.65% (L1) closes it with **1965 g of urea** (C3). Buy-N enters exactly where plant-N cannot — the web's three sources, working one ledger.

**The pile as calorimeter.** A compost pile that is fed and watered heats itself — the microbes' oxidation is the fire — and the heat budget prices the difference between a live pile and a dead one. Take a 50 kg pile and ask what it costs in glucose-equivalent to raise it 20 K: at a specific heat of 4.184 J/(g·K) — Ch 7's own anchor, inherited here as a **[flagged model: water-dominated]**, because the wet pile is mostly water and the model says so openly — the pile needs 4,184,000 J (H2). Glucose oxidation delivers −2.803 MJ per mole [convergent] on the balance C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O, 3,721,440 myu EXACT (H1; glucose 180.156, Ch 21's pin). Divide: **1.493 mol → 268.9 g of glucose-equivalent** to warm the pile through one 20-degree step (H2).

**The thermophilic window.** The pile's working band is **55–65 °C** [convergent: Cornell] — the floor where plant pathogens and weed seeds die, the ceiling where even the thermophiles slow. On the same 50-kg ledger, the band's two edges cost **470.6 g and 605.1 g of glucose-equivalent** (H3; 7,322,000 J and 9,414,000 J). The practical readings fall straight out: a pile that stalls below the band is a blend problem (C2) or a moisture problem; a pile that sits in the band for a season has pasteurized its own seed bank. The instrument is a compost thermometer or a steel rod left in the center — **P9** again, convergent evidence over instruments; and the turn is **P17**, release-rate control: turning re-feeds oxygen to the oxidation the same way a fire lay re-feeds air.

**The idea of this lecture, alone:** the compost pile is a ledger — one ratio to steer, one heat budget to respect — and every number in both is a battery row.

## Lecture 3 — The K Face and the P Refusal

**Ash: the burn barrel's fertilizer.** Wood ash is the household's potassium bottle: sweep the stove, keep the ash dry (the potassium rides out with the first rain — leached ash is limestone-hearted trivia), and read it with the potash convention Chapter 14 taught. The trade quotes ash as K₂O-equivalent; the compound actually present is mostly K₂CO₃ — 138.204 g/mol, of which the K₂O face is 68.16% by Ch 14's own convention (L2). Extension-service analyses put garden wood ash in a 1–10% K₂O band, roughly 6% typical [convergent: Oklahoma State, Alabama Extension]; so one kilogram of ash at the typical band carries 60 g K₂O-equivalent, which at the convention is **88.0 g of K₂CO₃-equivalent** (L3). The ash can is thus a real — if modest — potassium ledger, priced the way every label in this book is priced: convention named, band named, arithmetic shown.

**The refusal, taught.** Now the middle number. Bone meal is calcium phosphate rock ground for the garden shelf; superphosphate is that rock treated with acid; the DAP jar reads 18-46-0. Every one of them is a *label read* — the numbers copied into the notebook as trade prose — and every one of them is **refused at this book's element table**: phosphorus, P, is not an admitted element here (Ch 20's standing law, Ch 24's buffer bench), so no P-bearing composition is ever computed, and the engine's gate log executes the refusal three times in this chapter's battery: bone meal's mineral (R1: REFUSED admission: P), the DAP face (R2/L4: REFUSED admission: P). This is not squeamishness; it is the book's own honesty law stated in its sharpest form: **a table you have not verified is a table you do not compute from.** The garden trade's N-P-K survives intact as a *reading* — you can identify a bag, estimate its middle number, and act on the trade's own claims — and dies as *arithmetic*. Chapter 20 printed the law at the DAP jar; this chapter inherits it wholesale and prints the gate log as the fence in action.

**The idea of this lecture, alone:** potassium is a bottle you can price honestly; phosphorus is a bottle you can only read — and the honest chemist knows which is which.

## Lecture 4 — The Lime Loop (Ch 14's inheritance, closed by hand)

The garden's pH instrument is a loop, and Chapter 14 walked most of it already; the garden closes it.

**Burn.** Limestone — CaCO₃, 100.086 g/mol, Ch 14's pin — gives up its carbon dioxide to a hot fire and leaves quicklime, CaO (56.077, Ch 14). The dolomite class is the same burn with magnesium riding along: **CaMg(CO₃)₂ → CaO + MgO + 2 CO₂**, 1,843,990 myu EXACT (B6; Ch 30's E12 class — the burn the cement chapter certified at the kiln face). The garden's version is the shell kiln or the heap of limestone in the fire ring.

**Slake.** Quicklime plus water is the most dramatic reaction on the garden bench: the lump steams, crumbles, and boils — slaking, the exotherm priced at Ch 14's own band (−63.7 to −65.2 kJ/mol; the battery carries the band's −64.8 center). Slake the day's shell-kiln output of 4.746 mol of quicklime and the ledger reads **4.746 × 64.8 = 307.5 kJ** released (H4) — enough heat, once, to warm the slaking tub's water perceptibly, which is itself the thermometer (P9: the reaction reports its own progress). The product is slaked lime, Ca(OH)₂ (74.092, Ch 24's pin).

**Carbonate.** Left in the air, slaked lime slowly drinks CO₂ back and returns to the beginning: **Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O**, 1,181,010 myu EXACT (B5) — the white crust on limewater, the same test Ch 14 used for the gas, closing the loop. The gardener drives the loop deliberately: lime the acid soil in autumn, let the winter's rain and carbon dioxide mellow the caustic edge, and plant in spring into a bed that has come back around to the mild carbonate.

The lime loop is the whole chapter in miniature: a label (the soil's sourness), a ledger (how much lime sweetens it — priced by the mole at Ch 14's own table), and a fence (the caustic middle of the loop, handled by the Charter's rules — Safety row 3).

**The idea of this lecture, alone:** pH is not a number you own; it is a loop you steer — burn, slake, carbonate, repeat — and every stage of the loop is a pinned compound.

## Lecture 5 — Biochar, Honestly

The garden wing's most fashionable bottle deserves the book's plainest treatment.

**The char model.** Take the cellulosic half of the biomass ledger — cellulose, C₆H₁₀O₅ as the book pins it at Ch 25: 162.141 g/mol — and model pyrolysis as the extreme dehydration: **C₆H₁₀O₅ → 6 C + 5 H₂O**, 1,621,410 myu EXACT (B7, a **[flagged model]**: bookkeeping-exact, mechanism expressly disclaimed — real pyrolysis does not stop at clean carbon and clean water; it makes tars, acids, methane, and a yield that depends on temperature and dwell). What the model *is* good for is the ceiling it prices: the char fraction of the model is 44.45% by mass (C4) — a number to steer a kiln against, not to promise from one.

**The honest faces.** The battery's own C:N face for the char is *undefined* (C4) — there is no nitrogen in 6 C, and a material with no nitrogen has no C:N ratio, which the row prints rather than dodges. That undefined face is the truest sentence in the biochar literature: **char is not a fertilizer.** It is a carbon skeleton — a habitat claim for the soil's biology, a water-holding claim for sandy beds, a long-game claim for the carbon itself — and every one of those claims is biology the battery does not price. What the battery prices is the burn: the kiln is run exactly as the fire chapter taught (FC1's lay, P17's release-rate), outdoors, never sealed, and the char is quenched with the ash can's own contents — Lecture 3's bottle and Lecture 5's kiln sharing one bench, which is the chapter's architecture in one image.

**The idea of this lecture, alone:** biochar is a model, a yield, and an undefined ratio — and the honest chapter prints all three, labels on.
