## Lecture 0 — The Label on the Packet (the pencil case, before any chemistry)

Read the ingredient list of any processed food and three working faces of the Manufactured Shelf appear again and again: an acidulant — citric acid, E330, the sourness that is also the preservation; a thickener or texturizer — starch, the E1400 family, the granule that swells; a flavor — and here, under the single word *flavouring* or *artificial flavor*, the vanillin the packet almost never names. Three molecules, three jobs: keep, thicken, taste. The chapter's pencil case is the make-or-buy question each of them answers in a different register. Citric acid is the world's most-produced food additive by a wide margin, and almost none of it has ever seen a lemon — the industrial answer to make-or-buy was won by a mold. Starch is the one the home cook *can* make from a potato, and the store shelf's own answer (corn starch at commodity price) makes the homemade version a purity statement, not an economy. Vanillin is the starkest: the natural extract priced like a luxury and the synthetic crystal priced like a commodity, the same molecule by every measure but provenance, at a price ratio of hundreds to one.

The pencil-case skill this lecture hands the reader is the book's standing one, sharpened: the label is a chemistry document. The E-number and the *flavouring* word are not fine print; they are the shelf telling you exactly which chapter of the pantry you are eating. And the question the rest of the chapter answers is not *what* these molecules are — one napkin covers that — but why the industry makes them the way it does, and what the home extraction actually buys when it is done for real.
