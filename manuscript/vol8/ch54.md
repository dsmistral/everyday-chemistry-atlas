## Lecture 0 — Four Products, One Question (the pencil case, before any chemistry)

Take four things off the shelf that almost no one thinks of as chemistry: a tub of margarine, a bottle of sunscreen, a bar of soap, a roll of toilet paper. Each is, in fact, a chemistry document — the margarine an emulsion engineered around a hydrogenation reaction; the sunscreen a conjugated molecule designed to absorb a specific band of light; the soap a salt of a fatty acid, self-assembling in water into the structure that actually lifts the grease; the paper a polymer of sugar, the one carbohydrate the human body cannot digest. And each carries the question this chapter asks of it, the deconstruction's only question: *could you make it yourself?*

The question is not rhetorical, and the chapter's five faces have all actually tried. The register's product-deconstruction axis runs through four NileRed episodes — margarine from olive oil, sunscreen from scratch, soap from grocery fats, ethanol from toilet paper — and one generalist maker's project that tried to make a glass bottle from raw sand and is the chapter's fifth face and its frame. The generalist's project is Andy George's *How To Make Everything* (S88, the register's 88th source, the first new source since S87 at batch 33cr), whose premise is the deconstruction premise in its purest form: take an everyday object, make every component of it from raw materials, and learn what the object actually is. The premise is honest, and the register records its honest defeat — the river sand "really difficult to blow," the glassblowers' prepared sand substituted in-source — which makes it the perfect frame for a chapter whose four chemist's attempts all succeeded at the chemistry and still answer the make-or-buy question the same way: no.

The pencil-case skill this lecture hands the reader is the deconstruction reading itself: the ingredient list, the E-number, the "fragrance" and "preservative" words, the SPF number, the "hydrated fat" phrase — each a chemistry claim the shelf is making about itself. The rest of the chapter reads four of those claims from the inside, one product per lecture, and closes on the generalist's bottle.

## Lecture 1 — The Margarine Axis: The Emperor's Contest and the Hydrogen Ladder (N398, read at 4,380,403 views)

The register's margarine head is NileRed's "Making margarine from olive oil" (drIm8_1CvjQ, 08 Aug 2017; the watch at 4,380,403 against the 1.6M crawl, the 2.7378× ratio the register holds exact; the TheTVDB production-code route's debut, the 20-minute runtime its pin; the dual-track Spanish-full and English-auto page — the full ES track the register's second language-variant face at gather). The episode's premise is the deconstruction premise exactly: margarine is a manufactured food, the manufacture is hydrogenation, so hydrogenate some olive oil and see what you get. What you get — the video's own verdict, stated up front and never softened — is "terrible and inedible," and the episode is the better for the honesty: the chemistry works, the product fails, and the failure is itself the lesson [register].

The history lecture is the industry's own, and the web battery confirms it convergently. Margarine begins as a state project: France in 1869, Napoleon III's contest for a butter substitute that would feed the army and the poor, won by Hippolyte Mège-Mouriès with a beef-tallow-and-milk emulsion he patented on 15 July 1869 — the name from the Greek *margaron*, pearl-white [convergent]. The product he could not make was today's: his fat was as soft as the tallow it came from. The missing reaction arrived three decades later — Wilhelm Normann's hydrogenation of liquid oils, developed 1901, patented in Germany as 141029 in 1902 [convergent] — the process of bubbling hydrogen through an unsaturated oil over a metal catalyst (industrially nickel; the video's palladium) to saturate its double bonds, hardening a liquid into a fat at room temperature. The hardened oils entered food by 1910 and the American market by Crisco in 1911 [convergent], and the 1950s made hydrogenated vegetable oil the margarine era's own medium [register]. The video's theory lecture walks the same ladder in its own words: full hydrogenation saturates everything (the candle-wax extreme); partial hydrogenation stops partway — and in stopping, isomerizes some of the remaining cis double bonds to trans, the trans fats that behave like saturation in the body without being saturated. The epilogue belongs to the regulators: the FDA's 2015 determination that partially hydrogenated oils are not generally recognized as safe [convergent] — the ladder's last rung, and the reason today's margarine is made by interesterification and blending instead.

The bench run is honest to its bones. The feedstock is grocery olive oil — the high-oleic triglyceride, its 18:1 chains the substrate. The hydrogen is self-made, the drain-cleaner-and-aluminum generation route the register's P8 face; the catalyst palladium on carbon, charged and handled; the reaction run and run again. And the register's own cap-cut discipline closes the episode exactly where the transcript cuts: the final yield's sentence — "my final yield of the saturated fat was f..." — is the register's fourth explicit-marker truncation, and the yield, verdict close, and economics stay unrecovered even after the 33er sidecar campaign re-read the Spanish full track at its own 32.7K cap [register, the honest zero standing]. The chapter refuses to paper over it: the margarine axis's mass balance ends at the cut, and no fabricated row fills it. The episode's whisk-revisit promise — a comment-informed plan to re-emulsify — is the register's P9 face; the lost-footage honesty is the same face; and the two honest zeros (the P12 yield, the P16 close) are the register's own ledger, printed here as zeros.

The make-or-buy verdict the episode hands the home reader is stark without being stated: a Pd/C catalyst, a hydrogen source, and a pressure-capable setup to make a spread the store sells for the price of the oil — the deconstruction buys the emulsion-theory understanding, and that is all it buys. The lecture's one-sentence takeaway: the margarine tub is a hydrogenation reactor's product, and the reactor is the part the kitchen does not have.
