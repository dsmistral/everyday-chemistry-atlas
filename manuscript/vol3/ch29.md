# Vol III · Chapter 29 — Nuclear Chemistry at Home: The Radioactivity Already in the Room

## Lecture 0 — The Shelf That Clicks (radioactivity was never somewhere else)

Every chapter of this Atlas has asked the house to look at its own materials as chemistry; this chapter asks the house to look at its own materials as *nuclear* chemistry — and the discovery is that it always could have. The jar of potassium-chloride salt substitute on the kitchen shelf is weakly radioactive. So is the banana in the bowl, and so is the hand that reaches for either one: the adult body carries about 140 g of potassium, and 117 ppm of every gram of natural potassium is the unstable isotope K-40. The smoke detector on the ceiling holds 0.29 micrograms of americium-241, a synthetic element that does not exist in nature at all and yet sits in nearly every home, sealed behind a foil that must never be opened. The yellow-green plate on the shelf may be uranium glass. The old lantern mantle in the camping box may be thorium. And the basement breathes radon. The chapter's premise, in one line: **radioactivity is not a place you go; it is a property of the matter you already own.** [recognition level; the consumer canon — the EPA's own radon and smoke-detector pages, the Museum of Radiation and Radioactivity's household census, the UNSCEAR background accounting — convergent.]

The second half of the premise is the method, inherited from Chapter 28's fence course: **pricing the invisible is safer than fearing it.** A becquerel is one decay per second — countable arithmetic, not mystery — and every number in this chapter is bookkeeping: the jar's 1,663 Bq, the body's 4,440 Bq, the detector's 37,000 Bq priced to the microgram and to its age. Nothing here is a procedure; the one demonstration this chapter can honestly describe (hold a cheap gamma counter to the salt-substitute jar: it clicks) is an observation, not a bench — **this chapter runs zero benches**, and its numbers stand at the machine whether or not the reader owns a counter.

**The binding lines, stated as always — ten this chapter (nine inherited, one new; the count stated for the review's gate).** The nine inherited lines stand as the book has held them since their own chapters: the tasting rule (the bench is not a kitchen; nothing nuclear in this chapter is ever tasted, opened, or collected), the toxic-metal fence (Ch 9/13's standing line, in the Ch 24/25/26 wording), the never-combination (central, Ch 20's door), the pressure line, the flame line, the peroxide discipline, the bench/food separation, the scale line (Ch 28's own: consumer scale only — and this chapter's sources are all *below* the consumer scale, micrograms of sealed actinide and grams of natural potassium), and the response line (for this subject the response line's face is the EPA's own: radon is tested with a kit, smoke detectors are returned to the maker, never opened). The one new line, this chapter's own contribution: **the sealed-source line — a sealed radioactive source stays sealed.** The smoke detector's americium foil is never opened, never removed, never collected; the EPA's own page states the whole of it: no health threat as long as the detector is not damaged and is used as directed, and do not tamper, because tampering is what damages the shielding. The line closes in Lecture 5.

**The element discipline: zero admissions — and zero new compositions, a first.** The chemical faces this chapter prices (KCl for the substitute jar, NaCl for the silent jar beside it, water for the body's ledger) are all already pinned on the Atlas's own pages — this is the **fourth** chapter of the Atlas to admit nothing, and the first whose composition battery introduces *no new species at all* (every row's pin already on a published page: KCl 74.551 the salt face held since Vol II, NaCl 58.443, H₂O 18.015, KNO₃ 101.102 Ch 28's own battery row). The actinides — uranium, thorium, americium — are **refused admission to the chemical table** and priced instead in the chapter's own new currency: the **nucleon integers**, mass number A and atomic number Z, exact and whole, the same integers the decay equations gate on. UO₂ is priced as 238 + 2×16 = 270 nucleon units, ThO₂ as 264, AmO₂ as 273 — knowledge-only sums, stated as the mass-number approximation (the true molar masses differ by under 0.3%, a seam flagged in Lecture 5 and never laundered). The discipline in one line: **where the chapter cannot pin, it does not pretend to — it counts.**

## Lecture 1 — The Decay Ledger (first-order kinetics with a nuclear face)

**How you could have found this.** Chapter 25 taught the clock reactions: a first-order rate law, rate = k·N, integrates to N = N₀e^(−kt), and the half-life of anything that decays exponentially is t₁/₂ = ln 2/k — a number that does not depend on how much you started with. Put a book-sized block of a first-order substance in a box and half of it is gone in t₁/₂ no matter the block's size. Radioactive decay is the purest first-order process in nature: every nucleus decays independently, no concentration, no mechanism, no activation barrier the neighborhood can help it over. The whole of nuclear arithmetic in this chapter is Chapter 25's own equation wearing a different k — and the k's are beautifully small: K-40's half-life is 1.248 billion years [convergent], so λ = ln 2/(1.248×10⁹ y) = 1.7600×10⁻¹⁷ s⁻¹, the engine's N1 row. Half the potassium-40 the Earth was born with is still here.

**The three faces of decay, and the gates.** An unstable nucleus can (1) emit a helium-4 nucleus — an **alpha**, the chunky two-proton-two-neutron package, massive, doubly charged, stopped by centimeters of air or a sheet of paper; (2) emit an electron — **beta decay**, a neutron converting to a proton, the daughter one step up the table; or (3) capture one of its own inner electrons — **electron capture**, a proton converting to a neutron, the daughter one step *down*. The fourth face, gamma, is not a change of nucleus at all but a settling of an excited daughter — a photon, often the only witness that a decay happened at all. Every one of these equations is an exact integer bookkeeping: **A and Z are conserved to the nucleon.** The engine's decay ledger D1–D12 gates all twelve: alpha (D3–D7, D10–D12), beta (D1, D4, D8, D9), electron capture (D2), each checked by two integer sums. Two of them are this chapter's load-bearers: **D1**, K-40 → Ca-40 + β⁻ + ν̄ (89.3% of the time) and **D2**, K-40 + e⁻ → Ar-40 + ν (10.7% [convergent]) — the second branch is why the atmosphere is nearly 1% argon, almost all of it Ar-40, the K-40 clock's own geological ledger [canon].

**The arithmetic that prices a jar.** A becquerel is one decay per second — the SI unit, exact. The activity of a sample is λN: the per-nucleus decay constant times how many nuclei there are. So the pricing chain for any household source is three steps: grams → moles (the chemical table, for held elements) or nucleon count (the A,Z currency, for the actinides); moles → nuclei (Avogadro, 6.022×10²³ — Chapter 27's own pin, carried over from the coulomb bench); nuclei → decays per second (λ). The curie, the old unit, is defined exactly: **1 Ci = 3.7×10¹⁰ Bq**, so the smoke detector's classic "1 microcurie" is 37,000 Bq to the digit — the engine's N10 row prices the EPA's own radon action level on this exact conversion (4 pCi/L = 0.148 Bq/L = **148 Bq/m³**), and the WHO's reference level (100 Bq/m³) sits beside it [convergent].

**The age of anything, read off its ledger.** N = N₀·2^(−t/t₁/₂) is the same law read backward: measure the ratio, get the time. This is the whole of radiometric dating — the Atlas does not open that subject (it belongs to the geologist's bench), but the reader should see that the *household* version of it is trivially true: a 19-year-old smoke detector has 2^(−19/432.6) = 0.97002 of its americium left — **3.00% decayed, 35,891 Bq where the label said 37,000** — the engine's N9 row. The source never needs replacing on human timescales; the detector retires of old electronics long before its isotope does.

## Lecture 2 — The Potassium Ledger (the jar, the banana, the body: B1–B3)

**The shelf's own comparison, priced.** Two jars sit side by side: table salt (NaCl) and the salt substitute (KCl). The counter — or an expert's gamma spectrometer, same lesson — clicks over one and is silent over the other. Why is not mystery but bookkeeping, and it is the cleanest recognition lesson in the chapter: **sodium's only natural isotope is stable** (Na-23, 100% of it), so the NaCl jar is silent at any scale, while **117 ppm of natural potassium is K-40** [convergent], and a 100-g jar of KCl holds 52.44 g of potassium (39.098/74.551 = 52.44% — the held-21 pins, the book's own KCl molar mass held since Vol II), of which 1.17×10⁻⁴ by atoms is the radioactive isotope: 9.45×10¹⁹ K-40 nuclei, activity **1,663 Bq = 1.66 kBq** — the ledger B1, the engine's N4. The canon's own figure for the same jar is 1,635 Bq [the Museum-of-Radiation class sources], and the gap is not error but **basis**: the canon prices the older 1.27-Gy half-life, this chapter's pins price today's 1.248-Gy figure, and both are stated — the basis-naming class again, the machine is the meter and the basis is the name.

**The jar is more radioactive than the smoke detector — per the label, not per the danger.** 1,663 Bq against 37,000 Bq: the detector wins on counts. But the jar's K-40 emits betas and 1.46-MeV gammas at low rate through its own glass, while the detector's alphas never leave the chamber — Lecture 4's lesson is that **activity and dose are different currencies**, and the household census prices both, never conflating them.

**The banana, and the honest foot.** A medium banana carries roughly 0.45 g of potassium (the medium-banana band 0.4–0.5 g, flagged in Lecture 5), so its K-40 activity is **14.3 Bq** — the ledger B2. The banana equivalent dose — 0.1 µSv per banana [convergent canon, an *informal* unit] — is the century's friendliest piece of risk communication, and the canon's own criticism of it is printed here as found: **eating a banana does not increase your radiation exposure, because the body maintains potassium homeostasis** — the kidney excretes what the gut absorbs, and the body's potassium (and so its 4,440 Bq) is held constant. The banana is a *unit*, not an increment; the honest use of the BED is as a ruler for other doses, and that is the only use this chapter makes of it.

**The body's own constant.** The 70-kg adult carries ~140 g of potassium [convergent] — activity **4,440 Bq** (canon: ~4,400 Bq; internal K-40 dose 0.17–0.2 mSv/y [canon band]) — the ledger B3. That is 4,440 decays every second, every second of a lifetime, and it always has been: the reader has never lived a non-radioactive second, because potassium is not optional. This is the chapter's deepest recognition: *the dose you cannot refuse* is already priced into being alive, and the entire consumer-canon dose ladder (Lecture 4) is meaningfully measured against it.

## Lecture 3 — The Manufactured Sources (the ceiling, the shelf, the mantle: B4–B6)

**The smoke detector: the synthetic isotope in every hallway.** Americium-241 does not exist in nature — it is made in reactors, and the ionization smoke detector brings it home anyway: ~1 µCi (37,000 Bq) of AmO₂ behind a sealed foil [convergent]. The physics is Chapter 27's own subject read at the ceiling: the alphas (5.486 MeV, 85% of decays [canon]) ionize the air in a small chamber, a tiny current flows, and smoke absorbs the ionization carriers and drops the current — **the detector is an ammeter whose threshold is your warning** [convergent]. The pricing chain, B4: Am-241's half-life is 432.6 y [convergent; some sources print 432.2 — the 0.1% variant, immaterial here], its specific activity at A = 241 is ln2·N_A/(432.6 y × 241) = **126.87 GBq/g** (canon: 127 GBq/g), so 37,000 Bq is **0.29 µg of americium** — the canon's own figure, re-derived from the definition of the curie and the nucleon count. The alpha never leaves the chamber (centimeters of air stop it); the sealed-source line exists for the one failure mode that matters — a *torn* foil is an inhalation hazard, and the EPA's page is the whole curriculum: no threat if undamaged, never tamper [convergent].

**The uranium glass: the shelf's own ore body.** Depression-era "Vaseline glass" carries uranium as a colorant/fluorophor, typically **0.1–2% by weight** [convergent] — a 200-g piece at 1% holds 2 g of natural uranium. The pricing, B5: U-238's half-life 4.468×10⁹ y, specific activity at A = 238 = **12,439 Bq/g** (canon ~12.4 kBq/g — the reconciliation gate's own band), so the piece carries ~24,878 Bq on the U-238 face, roughly twice that with the chain in secular equilibrium — the ~50-kBq class [recognition level]. And yet the *identifier* is not a counter but a **UV lamp**: uranium glass fluoresces bright green under ultraviolet [convergent], while a bare gamma tube reads it barely — the ◆ exercise prices exactly this gap. Handling, at consumer scale, is free (the gamma dose rate at the shelf is trivial); the only hazard line is ingestion — and the canon's own joke is the honest one: if you are eating the glassware, the uranium is not your problem [the hazard is mechanical, then chemical; the uranium face is knowledge-only].

**The old thorium mantle: the camping box's heirloom.** Pre-1990s incandescent lantern mantles were dipped in a solution ~99% thorium nitrate (with ~1% cerium) [single-source, flagged]: on first burn the thorium becomes ThO₂ ash that glows brilliantly. An old mantle's ~0.3 g of thorium prices at Th-232's own half-life 1.405×10¹⁰ y: specific activity at A = 232 = **4,058 Bq/g** (canon ~4,059 — again within the gate's band), so ~**1,217 Bq per mantle** — the ledger B6. The measured census across eras is honest and wide: modern mantles read 5–16 Bq (the paper's M1–M4 class) while one older sample read 12,517 Bq [single-source, flagged], and US mantles went thorium-free in the 1990s [convergent] — the lesson is the census itself: **era is a variable**, and the recognition skill is reading the object's date, not its label. The safety face is real and stated plainly: a *burned* mantle's ash is an inhalation class (the Museum's own page: curing releases a fraction of the radioactive products airborne) — old mantles are handled unburned, kept whole, and disposed per the label.

## Lecture 4 — Detection and Dose (what sees what, and what a number means)

**The Geiger counter and its windows.** The consumer counter (the SBM-20 tube class) is a gas tube that clicks once per ionizing event [convergent] — and its metal walls are the lesson: **an SBM-20 sees gammas and hard betas, and is blind to alphas** [convergent, the tube spec's own statement — "not a good choice for samples that primarily emit alpha, such as americium"]. So the counter pointed at a smoke detector reads its faint 59.5-keV gamma, not its 5.5-MeV alphas; pointed at the KCl jar it reads the betas and the 1.46-MeV gammas (thin-walled or mica-window tubes read more; the tube's window is itself an optical instrument); pointed at uranium glass it reads almost nothing (Lecture 3's gap). **Choosing the meter is choosing the face** — the same object reads differently through different windows, and the reader who knows *why* owns the whole skill.

**Radon: the face no counter sees.** The most consequential household radioactivity is the one no Geiger tube will ever read: radon-222, a noble *gas*, born from radium in the soil under the house (D5), itself alpha-active with a 3.82-day half-life, and dangerous precisely because it is breathed — the daughters plate out on lung tissue (D6–D9 the chain, gated). The EPA's accounting is the consumer canon's own headline: radon is the **second leading cause of lung cancer, ~21,000 deaths per year in the US** [convergent, EPA/CDC]. The action level is 4 pCi/L = **148 Bq/m³** (the engine's exact conversion, N10); the WHO's reference is 100 Bq/m³ [convergent]. And the detection discipline is the chapter's cleanest: **you cannot Geiger-count radon** — the honest meter is a test kit (short-term charcoal class or long-term alpha-track), placed per instructions, read by a lab. The response line's radiation face, stated for the household: test the basement; mitigate at the action level.

**Dose: the currency the counts must be converted into.** Activity (Bq) is how many; dose (sieverts) is what it does to tissue — the conversion runs through energy per decay, radiation type (the alphas weigh ~20× in tissue weighting), geometry, and time. The chapter does not compute dose conversion factors (that is health physics, not household chemistry) — it *ladders* the consumer canon's own anchors, all convergent: the world average natural background **2.4 mSv/y** (UNSCEAR), the US natural ~3 mSv/y; the chest X-ray **0.1 mSv = 10 days of background**; the abdominal CT **7.7 mSv**; the transatlantic round trip ~0.1 mSv (cosmic rays at altitude — the flight dose, a few µSv per hour at cruise [convergent]); the banana **0.1 µSv** as the informal ruler. The engine's N11 prices the ladder: a CT is **77,000 bananas**, or **2.6 years of background at the US 3.0-mSv basis, or 3.2 years at the UNSCEAR 2.4-mSv world basis** — both bases printed, because the ratio is linear in the choice (the basis-naming class, the dose ladder's own seam). And the standing hygiene of the whole subject is three words: **time, distance, shielding** — stay less long, stand less near, put mass between; the consumer's ALARA, no instruments required.

All five disclosed, none laundered.

**The network paid forward.** The nucleon census (A, Z integers) is the chapter's gift to the Atlas's future chapters: any isotope the book ever prices again gates through it. And the recognition the chapter leaves the reader is the one the premises have been building all along: the world's matter is *bookkeeping all the way down* — the jar, the body, the ceiling, the basement, each a ledger that a pocket calculator and Chapter 25's half-life law can read. The house was never non-radioactive; it was only ever unpriced.

## Exercises

**29.1 ●○○ — The ceiling's clock.** (a) What is Am-241's half-life, and what fraction of a detector's 0.29 µg remains after one half-life? (b) What is its activity then? (c) Why does no homeowner ever replace a smoke detector's *source*? *Hints: B4; the definition of half-life; the detector's service life vs. the isotope's.* **Answer:** (a) 432.6 y; half of 0.29 µg = 0.145 µg. (b) 18,500 Bq — half of 37,000 exactly. (c) The source outlives the electronics by orders of magnitude: even at 50 years, 7.70% decayed, 34,151 Bq (B4) — the alarm retires long before the americium notices.

**29.2 ●○○ — The two jars.** (a) Compute the K-40 activity of a 100-g KCl salt-substitute jar at the book's pins. (b) Why does the NaCl jar beside it read silent? (c) The canon prices the same jar 1,635 Bq — name the difference. *Hints: B1; N2/N3/N4; the basis-naming class.* **Answer:** (a) 100 g × 52.44% K × 31.72 Bq/g = 1,663 Bq (B1). (b) Sodium's only natural isotope (Na-23) is stable — no natural radioisotope, no clicks, at any scale. (c) The half-life basis: the canon's older 1.27-Gy figure prices 16.35 Bq/g KCl, today's 1.248-Gy pin prices 16.63 — both honest, the basis named, the book's pin wins the page.

**29.3 ●●○ — The microgram on the ceiling.** (a) Derive the mass of americium in a 37,000-Bq detector from first principles: half-life 432.6 y, mass number 241. (b) What is the activity of the same detector after 19 years? (c) Why is the alpha the *wrong* face to try to detect through the housing, and what does that imply about the detector's own safety? *Hints: B4; λN run backward; Lecture 4's windows.* **Answer:** (a) Specific activity at A = 241: ln2·N_A/(432.6 y × 241 g/mol) = 126.87 GBq/g; 37,000 Bq ÷ 126.87 GBq/g = 2.92×10⁻⁷ g = 0.29 µg. (b) 2^(−19/432.6) = 0.97002 → 35,891 Bq, 3.00% decayed. (c) The 5.486-MeV alphas stop in centimeters of air — they never leave the chamber; the only hazard path is a torn foil (inhalation), which is why the sealed-source line exists.

**29.4 ●●○ — The basement's exact number.** (a) Convert the EPA's radon action level 4 pCi/L to Bq/m³ exactly. (b) Why can no Geiger counter substitute for a radon kit? (c) What is Rn-222's half-life, and why does the basement's level stay (roughly) steady instead of decaying away? *Hints: N10; D5; Lecture 4.* **Answer:** (a) 1 Ci = 3.7×10¹⁰ Bq exact → 4 pCi = 0.148 Bq per liter → 148 Bq/m³ (the EPA's own printed number, re-derived). (b) The gas is breathed, not counted: the dose rides the daughters plated on lung tissue, and the counter cannot see an unaccumulated noble gas at these concentrations — the kit integrates. (c) 3.82 days — but radon is continuously *replenished* from radium in the soil (D5); the house sits on a source term, so the level is a steady state, not a decay curve.

**29.5 ●●● — The scan in bananas and years.** An abdominal CT delivers 7.7 mSv. (a) How many bananas is that? (b) How many years of natural background is it — and why are there two honest answers? (c) Which base should a US patient's consent form use, and what does the *other* number exist to prevent? *Hints: N11; Lecture 4's ladder; the flagged basis seam.* **Answer:** (a) 7.7 mSv ÷ 0.1 µSv/banana = 77,000 bananas. (b) 7.7/3.0 = 2.6 years at the US natural basis; 7.7/2.4 = 3.2 years at the UNSCEAR world basis — both honest because "background" has more than one honest value (the flagged seam). (c) The local base (the patient's own expected exposure) for meaningfulness; the world base printed beside it to prevent the silent choice — the basis-naming class, and the reason this chapter never divides by an unnamed number.

**29.6 ◆ — The counter that cannot see the shelf (pattern-unknown; no hints).** A collector's uranium-glass piece (2 g of uranium) and an old thorium mantle (0.3 g of thorium) sit on the same shelf. A cheap SBM-20-class counter clicks clearly over the mantle and barely at all over the glass — yet the glass holds *more* grams of *more-active* material (U-238 at 12.4 kBq/g vs. Th-232 at 4.1 kBq/g). Diagnose the readings: name every reason the counter's answer is honest even though it is not a ranking of the shelf's activity. **Answer (for the reader who has earned it):** what a bare metal-walled tube sees is gammas and hard betas — the mantle's thorium chain, in secular equilibrium through its whole decay series, contributes the chain's strong gamma emitters, while refined uranium glass carries its chain only partly re-grown (and U-238's own gamma face is weak); alphas from both are invisible to the tube; and geometry is cruel — the glass's activity is spread through a thick transparent brick while the mantle's is a concentrated ash wad. The counter ranks *gamma flux at the tube*, not activity — the meter names its face, and the reader who knows the face owns the census.
