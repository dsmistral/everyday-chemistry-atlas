# Vol III · Chapter 24 — Equilibrium at the Kitchen Bench: The Meter That Asks How Far, Not How Fast

## Lecture 0 — The standing waves: a house where nothing ever finishes

Volume III begins where Volumes I and II kept arriving. The pH engine of Chapter 8 computed a *position*; the Ksp bench of Chapter 9 priced a *cloud*; the ester shelf of Chapter 23 hit a *ceiling* and flagged its derivation forward to this chapter; the metals bench of Chapter 19 ran a coupled solve and honestly named its residual "ionic strength — Chapter 24's debt." Every one of those numbers was an equilibrium constant wearing work clothes, and the volume's founding observation is that **the household is not a collection of finished reactions but a collection of standing waves — processes running both directions at once, whose "done" is not a stop but a tie.** The fizz in the fridge runs both ways the moment the cap comes off. The scale in the kettle is a tie between limestone and carbon dioxide that moves with the weather of the soil. The bar of soap at the sink is a salt sitting at pH 9–10 because its own acid is too weak to push further. The bottle of milk of magnesia is a solid that refuses to dissolve more than a ten-thousandth of a mole per liter, and that refusal is the whole medicine. **Every reaction has a rate and a position; the previous twenty-three chapters measured positions everywhere and derived them nowhere. This chapter is the derivation's own home — how far, not how fast.**

The census the chapter reads is the house itself, re-walked as a list of ties:

- **The fridge: the soda bottle.** A sealed bottle is a fixed position — CO₂ over carbonic over bicarbonate, held at roughly 0.1 M dissolved CO₂ (Ledger B6) by the headspace pressure, a standing wave capped with a cap. Opening it drops the CO₂ pressure tenfold and the wave moves — the fizz is the position readjusting, visible, audible, and (Chapter 10's pressure line) never to be sealed behind an uncontrolled fermentation.
- **The kettle: the scale.** Limestone dissolves in soil-water (Chapter 9's carbonate hub, priced there; derived here) and un-dissolves on the heating element — the same coupled equilibrium, run backwards by boiling the CO₂ out of it. Ledger B4's cubic prices the whole story in three lines of arithmetic, and Exercise 24.5's summit prices the deposit per liter.
- **The medicine shelf: the bottle that will not dissolve.** Milk of magnesia — Mg(OH)₂, Ksp 5.61×10⁻¹² [convergent] — a suspension *because* the position sits at 1.12×10⁻⁴ M, and an antacid *because* the consumed hydroxide pulls more solid into solution exactly as fast as the stomach spends it: a buffer made of rock (Lecture 4).
- **The aquarium and the pool: two trades that sell pH meters because their chemistry is a position.** The pool shelf's active chlorine is a species pair, HOCl/OCl⁻, whose ratio rides the pH at pKa 7.53 (Chapter 16's pin) — 68% active at pH 7.2, 35% at 7.8 (Ledger B1's speciation join); the aquarium trade's whole pH discipline exists because ammonia's toxic face, free NH₃, climbs from 0.5% to 10% of the total between pH 7 and 8.3 (the same join, one shelf over).
- **The laundry shelf: the compound that was a national standard.** Borax — sodium tetraborate decahydrate, 381.363 g/mol [computed] — whose solution *self-buffers at its own pKa at any concentration* (Lecture 3), and whose 0.01 M solution, pH 9.18 at 25 °C, was for decades the metrology institutes' own pH standard [convergent]: the laundry shelf once calibrated the nation's pH meters, and Lecture 3 derives why.
- **The sink: the bar at pH 9–10.** Chapter 23's cured soap — a salt of an acid too weak (pKa ≈ 4.8 vs the water it dissolves into) to push its own solution past the mid-alkaline band; the bar's mildness *is* an equilibrium constant.
- **The pantry: the bottle that will not finish.** Chapter 23's esterification — vinegar and ethanol at a two-thirds ceiling — the shelf's weakest reaction, flagged here by name: **this chapter pays Chapter 23's debt and derives the K** (Lecture 1).

One thesis, eight shelves, and the chapter's four questions, asked once at the door and answered lecture by lecture: **(1) How far does a reaction go before it ties? (2) Why does it stop there? (3) What moves it? (4) How do we read the position with no instrument beyond a balance, a bottle, and arithmetic?** The answers, in order: the constant K (Lecture 1); free energy's near-flat bill (Lecture 1); the levers — concentration, removal, pressure, temperature (every lecture); and the equilibrium math the whole book has been borrowing — Ka, Kb, Kw, Ksp, Kf, one letter, six benches.

**Seven binding lines open the volume, inherited whole from the fences that raised them.** *First: the tasting rule* [Chapter 8, verbatim in force] — nothing on these benches is tasted, and the bottle this chapter prices most often (milk of magnesia) is the rule's own reason: a medicine-cabinet product is a bench reagent only at arm's length, recognized, never sipped. *Second: the flame line* [Chapter 6] — the esterification bench runs under a reflux water bath, never an open flame; ethanol and ethyl acetate are Chapter 21's flammables and Chapter 23's fire class. *Third: the ammonia fence* [Chapters 8/16/20] — the deep-blue ammine door (Lecture 5) opens only in a ventilated space, and the ammonia bottle never meets bleach: chloramine is a *thermodynamically eager* product and the never-combination stands exactly as Chapter 20 pinned it (Chapter 28 takes the recognition course). *Fourth: the lye discipline* [Chapter 14] — sodium hydroxide appears here only at recognition level, as the arithmetic behind the soap bar's pH; no caustic bench is scheduled. *Fifth: the CO₂ pressure line* [Chapter 10] — the fizz bottle's standing wave is priced at the desk; the sealed-vessel rule stands: fermentation locks vent, seltzer bottles are engineered, and nothing on this bench is ever latched with gas still coming. *Sixth: the toxic-metal fence* [Chapters 9/13] — copper(II) appears in the ammine door at demonstration scale, spot-plate scale, and its waste leaves by Chapter 9's precipitation route, never down the drain untreated. *Seventh: the borax hazard line* [this session's web battery] — borax was reclassified in the EU as a substance of very high concern for reproductive toxicity (the 2010 borates reclassification) [convergent]; the buffer bench prices it as chemistry and handles it with gloves, never on food surfaces, and the pool trade's own buffer literature says the same. The prior volumes' fences — thermite-class, lead, the alkali metals, chromium and cobalt, the methanol fence, the drier metals — stand unbroken; the one new element this chapter's table admits is **boron** (Lab 24.1), the borate buffer's own element, admitted for the first time in four chapters.

## Lecture 1 — The K meter, and the debt paid: deriving Chapter 23's equilibrium

**The meter this volume reads is the law of mass action itself.** For a reaction at a fixed temperature, the ratio of products to reactants — each raised to its stoichiometric power — converges to a number: the equilibrium constant K. The household's constants are one letter wearing six uniforms:

| Bench | Constant | The household face | Order |
|---|---|---|---|
| The acid–base bench | Ka, Kb, Kw | vinegar's 2.41; ammonia's 11.86; water's 1.0×10⁻¹⁴ | 10⁻¹⁴–10⁻⁵ |
| The ester bench | K (the Fischer constant) | Chapter 23's two-thirds ceiling | ~4 |
| The buffer bench | Ka re-read (Henderson) | the borax standard's 9.18 | — |
| The solubility bench | Ksp | the kettle's scale; MoM's refusal | 10⁻¹²–10⁻⁵ |
| The complexation bench | Kf (β₄) | Chapter 13's deep blue | 10¹³ |
| The speciation bench | K (species ratios) | the pool's HOCl; the aquarium's NH₃ | 10⁰–10³ |

Six benches, one law — and the first lesson is how *wide* the orders run: the same arithmetic that prices a tied esterification at 4 prices a copper ammine at 10¹³ and a magnesium hydroxide at 10⁻¹². **The constant is the reaction's personality.**

**Q before K: the direction-of-the-moment.** The reaction quotient Q is the mass-action ratio computed at *any* moment, and the comparison is the household's most practical oracle: **Q < K, the reaction runs forward; Q = K, it is at rest; Q > K, it runs backward.** Every fizz, cloud, and scum in the book is one of these three verdicts. The opened soda: Q (for CO₂ outgassing) sits below the headspace's position — the dissolved acid decomposes, and the bottle hisses until Q = K at the new pressure. The kettle: cold soil-water holds its calcium bicarbonate (Q = K); boiling expels CO₂, Q jumps above K for the precipitation direction, and calcite plates the element. The scum at Chapter 23's sink: hard water's calcium meets the soap's stearate, Q exceeds Ksp instantly, and the curd is the verdict. **The house never stops reacting; it just keeps re-tying at every new Q.**

**THE DEBT PAID: deriving Chapter 23's esterification constant.** Chapter 23 priced the forward arrow and wrote, honestly: *the K's derivation is Chapter 24's homework; here it is priced, used, and honestly labeled.* Here is the derivation, from the bench up, on the balanced bookkeeping of E24: **CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ C₄H₈O₂ + H₂O** (BALANCED, Lab 24.1 — mass closure 106.121 = 106.121 exact).

*Step one — the constant is the composition.* Charge a 1:1 pot and let it tie. The organic-lab corpus's own datum, verified against the encyclopedic and textbook traditions this session [convergent]: the mixture converts to ester at **about 65% at room temperature** — the classic figure the lab manuals print and the practical-lab titration course measures (the bench assay is simply: titrate the acid before and after). Write the position as *x*, the fraction converted. Then the pot holds (1−x) acid, (1−x) alcohol, x ester, x water, and

**K = x² / (1−x)².**

At the classic tie x = 2/3: **K = (2/3)²/(1/3)² = 4.000 [computed]** — the tabulated constant's headline value; at the manuals' measured 65%: **K = (0.65/0.35)² = 3.45 [computed]** — inside the tabulated spread **3.4–4.0 [tabulated, the spread named]**, and the derivation's first honest print is that *the constant is the composition*: measure where it ties and you have measured K. No instrument beyond a titration flask.

*Step two — why it ties there: the free-energy bill.* ΔG° = −RT ln K at 298.15 K (R = 8.314 J/mol·K), so at K = 4: **ΔG° = −8.314 × 298.15 × ln 4 = −3.44 kJ/mol [computed]**; at K = 3.4: **−3.03 kJ/mol**. This is the derivation's physical content: **the Fischer reaction ties two-thirds of the way because its free-energy bill is nearly flat** — three kilojoules is a rounding error against the hundreds that drove Chapter 19's displacement ladder or Chapter 11's redox cells. Products and reactants are near-equals in free energy, and a near-equal contest ties near the middle. (The formation-table route — K back-computed from tabulated ΔfG° values — is the same arithmetic run in reverse; its honest seam, named and not laundered: the standard compilations' ΔfG°(ethyl acetate, l) values sit close enough to the reaction's own 3-kJ scale that compilation differences move K across the whole tabulated spread, so the Atlas prints the bench-composition derivation as the owning route and the tables as the round trip. [flagged sketch: the ΔfG° route named, not printed as a number.])

*Step three — the ceilings, derived.* From the same K, the maximum conversion of a 1:1 charge follows by taking the square root of the mass-action expression: x/(1−x) = √K, so

**x = √K / (1 + √K)** — at K = 4: **66.67%; at K = 3.4: 64.84% [computed]** (Chapter 23's printed ceiling pair, reproduced by the engine to the digit, Lab 24.2's B2).

And the lever arithmetic Chapter 23 flagged: charge the alcohol at r:1 and the mass-action expression becomes a quadratic in x,

**(K − 1)x² − K(1 + r)x + Kr = 0 [computed],**

whose physical root at K = 4 gives **r = 4 → x = 92.96%; r = 9 → x = 97.07%** — Chapter 23's two levers, derived here rather than quoted. The quadratic also reproduces the 1:1 case exactly (r = 1 → x = 66.67%, the closed form's own value — the cross-check Lab 24.2 prints).

*Step four — what moves a tie, and what never does.* The three levers the shelf already owns, each one a Q-re-set: **excess alcohol** (r:1 — the quadratic above, the winemaker's and the distiller's arithmetic), **water removal** (the Dean–Stark azeotropic lever, priced at the oxalic-acid episode as N87's own bench trick [register-verified, reused from Chapter 23's citation] — pull a product, Q drops below K, the tie moves forward), and **product distillation** (the still's fractions are the ester's own arithmetic made visible, Chapter 23's door). And the one lever that is *not*: **the catalyst** — Chapter 23's acid catalyst moves the *rate*, never the position; Chapter 25 (kinetics) takes that distinction as its own founding flag, and this chapter states it as the volume's thesis: **K is how far; rate is how fast; no bottle on the shelf trades one for the other.**

## Lecture 2 — The Ka/Kb/Kw bench: water's own tie, and the acids that are mostly gas

**Water ties with itself.** The founding equation of the whole pH world is the smallest one in the ledger: **2 H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻** (E1, BALANCED — mass closure 36.030 = 36.030 exact, charge ledger 0 = 0). At 25 °C the tie sits at **Kw = [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴ [convergent]** — one in ten million water molecules is busy being an ion pair at any instant — and the pH scale of Chapter 8 is this one equilibrium constant read on a base-14 slide rule. Every acid–base number the house owns is a partition of the same Kw.

**The conjugate-pair conservation law.** For a conjugate pair, Ka × Kb = Kw — the pair's two rungs multiply to water's own tie. The engine's round-trip (Lab 24.2, B1): acetic Ka 1.8×10⁻⁵ × acetate Kb 5.556×10⁻¹⁰ = **1.00008×10⁻¹⁴** [computed] — Kw to four decimals, the +0.008% the rounding of the 5.556 pin carries; the ammonia pair lands on the identical product (Kb 1.8×10⁻⁵, Ka(NH₄⁺) 5.556×10⁻¹⁰ — Chapter 8's and Chapter 20's pins). The bicarbonate pair splits both ways at once — Kb(HCO₃⁻) = Kw/Ka1 = **2.33×10⁻⁸** and Kb(CO₃²⁻) = Kw/Ka2 = **2.128×10⁻⁴** [computed] — which is the arithmetic identity of bicarbonate's amphiprotic character, and the next paragraph's engine.

**The two-tier pH engine, re-derived as position arithmetic.** Chapter 8 built the engine; this chapter reads it as K. For a weak acid at concentration C: the 5% gate first (is the ionization small enough that √(KaC) self-consistently undercuts 5% of C?), the quadratic when it is not. The book's own pinned faces, reproduced by the fresh engine to the digit (Lab 24.2, B1):

- **Vinegar, 0.8376 M** (Chapter 4's canonical basis): [H⁺] = √(1.8×10⁻⁵ × 0.8376) = 3.883×10⁻³ → **pH 2.411 → 2.41, ionization 0.464%** [computed] — Chapter 8's pins, the 5% gate passed at a fiftieth of its allowance.
- **Household ammonia, 2.8771 M** (Chapter 20's bottle audit): [OH⁻] = √(1.8×10⁻⁵ × 2.8771) = 7.196×10⁻³ → **pH 11.857 → 11.86** [computed] — the bottle pin reproduced (the ATSDR Kb 1.774×10⁻⁵ variant lands at 11.854, inside the same print).
- **Washing soda, 0.100 M Na₂CO₃**: Kb(CO₃²⁻) = 2.128×10⁻⁴, [OH⁻] = 4.613×10⁻³ → **pH 11.664 → 11.66** [computed] — Chapter 8's pin; the honest band across the tabulated Ka2 spread (4.7–5.6×10⁻¹¹) is **11.63–11.66**, printed as the band it is.
- **Baking soda, 0.100 M NaHCO₃** — the amphiprotic mean, and the one formula in the battery with no concentration in it: **pH = ½(pKa1 + pKa2) = ½(6.367 + 10.328) = 8.347** on the 4.3×10⁻⁷/4.7×10⁻¹¹ basis, **8.314** on the 4.2/5.6 band ends → the book band **8.31–8.35** [computed], Chapter 8's 8.35 pin at one edge, the web band 8.3–8.4 containing all of it. The mean is not an approximation of anything — it is the exact position of a species that is both the acid and the base of its own pair (E7 and E8 are the two arrows), and dilution cannot move it (Exercise 24.1's contrast: the carbonate's pH walks −0.50 per tenfold dilution; the bicarbonate's does not move at all).

**THE SPECIATION SEAM: the acids that are mostly gas (Chapters 8's and 17's flags paid together).** Carbon dioxide dissolves (E4: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃) — but only about **0.2–0.3% of it hydrates** [tabulated spread]: the hydrated fraction is tiny, the molecule is the molecule, and the constant the tables print as "Ka1 of carbonic acid" is an *apparent* constant charged against all the dissolved CO₂, hydrated or not. The arithmetic of the split (Lab 24.2, B6): with the hydration ratio Kh ≈ 1.7×10⁻³, **Ka(true, H₂CO₃) = Ka(app)(1 + Kh)/Kh ≈ 2.5×10⁻⁴ → pKa ≈ 3.60 [computed]** — which reproduces, from the house's own arithmetic, the photolysis literature's measured pKa of true carbonic acid, **~3.5–3.6 [convergent]**, against the apparent **6.35–6.38** everyone quotes. The same seam runs through Chapter 17's sulfurous shelf: the Raman literature says the pot is mostly molecular SO₂, the tables' Ka1 (1.2–1.5×10⁻², pKa ~1.8–1.9; Chapter 17's 1.92 pin at the band's edge) is the total-SO₂ convention, and E25 (**SO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂SO₃**) is bookkeeping the trade and the tables share — the flag Chapter 17 left is paid here by showing the two acids that are mostly gas are the *same lesson twice*: **when the acid is a gas in solution, the constant you quote had better say which species it is charged against** ([flagged sketch] where used; the sulfurous row stays recognition-level — no sulfurous bench is scheduled, Chapter 17's gas-train discipline holds).

**The speciation ladder the trades already run.** Two species-pair joins the engine prices fresh this session, both [computed] on pinned constants:

- **The pool pair (Chapter 16's pKa 7.53 pin):** HOCl/OCl⁻ = 10^(7.53 − pH). At pH 7.2: **68% of the jug's oxidizing face is HOCl, the active form**; at 7.5: 52%; at 7.8: 35%; at 8.2: 18% — the pool trade's "keep it 7.2–7.8" rule is this one equilibrium constant restated as a maintenance instruction, and the shelf's own test kits are the Q-meter.
- **The aquarium pair (Chapter 8's NH₄⁺ pin, Ka 5.556×10⁻¹⁰):** NH₃/NH₄⁺ = 10^(pH − 9.255). Free ammonia — the toxic face — is **0.55% of the total at pH 7.0, 1.7% at 7.5, 10.0% at 8.3, 26% at 8.8**. The aquarium trade's pH alarm is the position of one tie; and the chloramine fence of Chapters 8/16/20 stands beside it — the never-combination is also a *speciation* rule: what meets bleach is the pair, and the pair's toxic member rides the pH.

One more register face honored at recognition level: **the triiodide tie Chapter 18 flagged here** — I₂ + I⁻ ⇌ I₃⁻, K ≈ 700 [tabulated], the speciation behind every retail iodine bottle's iodide bank (Chapter 18's Lugol's arithmetic) and behind the starch test's persistence: the blue complex's iodine is triiodide's iodine, and the position — not the dose — decides how much is available. Composition rows priced (Lab 24.1): I₂ 253.808, I⁻ 126.904, I₃⁻ 380.712 [computed].

## Lecture 3 — The buffer bench: the tie that resists, from the kitchen to the blood

**The Henderson–Hasselbalch reading.** A buffer is a conjugate pair *present on both sides of its own tie* — and its working equation is Lecture 2's arithmetic re-arranged: **pH = pKa + log([base]/[acid])**. The pair's pH sits *at* its pKa when the two sides are charged equal, and moves only as the logarithm of the dose ratio — which is the buffer's whole trick: **the log tames the dose.** The bench prices three buffers, one per lecture-worthy property:

**The acetate bench — the teeth, priced.** A 0.100 M / 0.100 M acetic/acetate pair sits at **pH = pKa = 4.74–4.75** across the tabulated Ka band (1.76–1.80×10⁻⁵; Chapter 8's printed 4.75 pin inside it, the basis named at the join — the Ch 21 RA-4 lesson running). Now the teeth: add 0.005 mol of strong acid per liter — 5 mL of 1 M HCl, or about half a gram of the muriatic jug's arithmetic (Chapter 15's fence stands) — and the dose converts acetate to acetic, the ratio goes 0.095/0.105, and **pH 4.745 → 4.701: a shift of 0.04 [computed]**. The same dose into a liter of pure water: **pH 7.00 → 2.301: a shift of 4.70 [computed]**. The buffer's resistance is a hundred-fold, and the formal capacity formula prices it exactly: **β = 2.303C/4 = 0.1152 mol per liter per pH unit at the midpoint [computed]** — and the linearized check closes: 0.005 mol / 0.1152 = 0.043 — the shift the dose actually produced, to the third decimal. (Chapter 8's accidental buffer — vinegar over soda at pH 4.56, volume-independent — was this bench happening by accident in a pot; this chapter is its derivation.)

**The blood bench — the tie that keeps you alive.** The physiologists' pair is bicarbonate over carbonic acid, and its constant is tabulated as an *apparent* pKa′ = 6.1 at 37 °C [convergent] — the prime marking an apparent, physiological constant, exactly Lecture 2's honesty lesson wearing a medical coat. The blood's ratio: **20:1**, and the Henderson reading: **pH = 6.1 + log(20) = 6.1 + 1.301 = 7.40 [computed]** — the number on every hospital chart derived from one ratio and one constant. The lesson the household takes: the body's most tightly regulated number is a buffer's *position*, held by the same arithmetic as the kitchen's vinegar pot, with the lungs and the kidneys as its two levers (CO₂ out through the breath — the Dean–Stark lever, biologically; bicarbonate in through the kidney — the alcohol lever's cousin).

**THE BORAX SELF-BUFFER — the standard on the laundry shelf.** The chapter's one derived gem, and the reason boron enters the element table. Dissolve borax (E23: Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O → 2 Na⁺ + B₄O₇²⁻ + 10 H₂O, mass closure 381.363 exact) and the tetraborate anion hydrolyzes by a *stoichiometrically self-balancing* pair of arrows:

**B₄O₇²⁻ + 7 H₂O ⇌ 2 B(OH)₃ + 2 B(OH)₄⁻** (E22, BALANCED — mass closure 281.338 = 281.338 exact, charge −2 = −2),

which is boric acid and its own conjugate base *made in exactly equal amounts from the same anion* — the Henderson ratio is 1:1 **by the stoichiometry itself, at any concentration, with no measuring at all**: **pH = pKa exactly [computed]**. The tabulated pKa band: **9.14–9.24 [convergent]** — and the metrological fact that makes the shelf historic: **a 0.01 M borax solution, pH(S) = 9.180 at 25 °C, was the NBS/NIST primary pH standard** for the alkaline range for most of a century [convergent] — the institutes sold it as Standard Reference Material 187e, sodium tetraborate decahydrate, thirty grams to a bottle. The laundry shelf's booster and the cleaning trade's pH-9-class helper (the pool trade prints the working range 8.2–10.2 [convergent]; Chapter 22's N72 borate face and Chapter 13's rosocyanine the register's own boron colorimetry) is the same compound the nation calibrated its meters with — and this chapter is the first in the book that can say *why*: **the compound carries its own buffer ratio in its formula.** (The buffer-craft bench notes the boric-acid side's other face — the EU's 2010 reprotoxicity reclassification of the borates [convergent] — the safety table's row 3, and the reason the gloves come out.)

**The house's buffer census.** The soap bar (pH 9–10, Chapter 23's cured-bar print — the fatty-acid pair too weak to leave the band); the aquarium's bicarbonate bank (the trade's KH kits — this chapter's E12 arithmetic again, one shelf over); the soil's own cation-exchange and humic pair (recognition level — the garden trade's liming arithmetic is E10's reverse with organic furniture); the seltzer bottle's mineral salts (B6's shelf band join); and the two trade buffers the household buys without the word ever appearing: the pool's total-alkalinity kit (bicarbonate, priced) and the pharmacy's antacid shelf (Lecture 4's solid-state buffer). **The trades never print "buffer"; they print what the buffer does: "pharmacy-safe," "keeps pH stable," "KH 8–12."**

## Lecture 4 — The Ksp bench: the cloud, the kettle, and the debt with the ion pairs

**The solubility product.** For a sparingly soluble salt, Ksp is the tie of the dissolved ions: **Ca²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ ⇌ CaCO₃** (E9) ties at **Ksp = 3.36×10⁻⁹** (Chapter 4's anchor, Chapters 9/10's pin, re-verified). The Q-oracle of Lecture 1 becomes the cloud oracle: Q = [Ca²⁺][CO₃²⁻] above Ksp and the solid must fall; below it and the solid must eat. Chapter 9 priced the household's precipitations on this meter; this chapter derives the two pieces Chapter 9 promised here: the *coupled* carbonate speciation behind the hardness story, and the *activity* correction behind the gypsum seam.

**Limestone in pure water — the small number.** s = √Ksp = √(3.36×10⁻⁹) = **5.80×10⁻⁵ M** — 5.80 mg/L as CaCO₃ [computed], soft water, a cloud you cannot see. This is the "insoluble" of the tables: not zero, just small, Chapter 4's founding lesson re-derived at the bench that owns it.

**THE COUPLED CUBIC — the hardness story in one equilibrium (Chapters 8's and 9's forward flags paid).** Rain and soil water do not attack limestone with their bare carbonate demand; they attack it with carbon dioxide, and the two equilibria chain. Sum the three ties — calcite's Ksp (E9), carbonic's first rung (E5) and second rung (E6) — and the chain equation is Lecture 0's kettle row:

**CaCO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ Ca²⁺ + 2 HCO₃⁻** (E12, BALANCED — mass closure 162.110 = 162.110 exact; the calcium bicarbonate row composed at 162.110 in Lab 24.1),

with **K′ = Ksp × Ka1 / Ka2 = 3.36×10⁻⁹ × 4.3×10⁻⁷ / 4.7×10⁻¹¹ = 3.074×10⁻⁵ [computed]**. The stoichiometry gives [HCO₃⁻] = 2[Ca²⁺], so the mass-action expression collapses to 4s³ = K′[CO₂], and the position is a cube root:

**s = (K′[CO₂]/4)^(1/3)** — and every piece of the hardness story is now three lines of arithmetic [computed]:

- **Air equilibrium** (Henry 0.034 M/atm × 400 ppm → [CO₂] = 1.36×10⁻⁵ M): s = **4.71×10⁻⁴ M = 47.1 mg/L as CaCO₃** (the Ka1-band end 4.2×10⁻⁷ gives 46.8; the web-verified band 44–47 contains both) — the natural hardness of rain-fed water, sitting right at the soft/moderately-hard boundary.
- **Soil air, tenfold** ([CO₂] = 1.36×10⁻⁴ M): s = **1.015×10⁻³ M = 101.6 mg/L** — hard, from nothing but a deeper breath of soil gas.
- **Heavy soil air, ~3% CO₂** ([CO₂] = 1.0×10⁻³ M, the top of the 2–30 mL/L soil-air band [convergent]): s = **1.973×10⁻³ M = 197.5 mg/L** — very hard, the well-water the trade sells softeners against.

Three prints, and the cube root's own lesson: the CO₂ rose **73.5-fold** from air to heavy soil and the hardness rose only **4.19-fold** — the position's tame response to its strongest lever, the reason groundwater is merely *very* hard and not syrup. The USGS classes name the ladder [convergent]: soft 0–60, moderately hard 61–120, hard 121–180, very hard >180 mg/L as CaCO₃ — the trade's whole vocabulary is this cube root with the classes stapled on.
