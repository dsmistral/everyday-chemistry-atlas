## Lecture 2 — The Rayleigh Law (the mathematics of throwing water away)

**The derivation, discrete first.** A reservoir of water, volume V₀, D/H ratio R₀ (the abundance itself, 155.76 ppm), under electrolysis. The cathode consumes hydrogen and deuterium at rates whose ratio is the separation factor S — hydrogen preferentially, S > 1. Slice off a small volume dV as gas: the deuterium left behind concentrates slightly, the hydrogen leaves faster than its share. Per slice, the deuterium ledger is dR/R = (1/S − 1)·dV/V (deuterium lost at rate 1/S of its share while volume shrinks at rate dV/V), and integrating the slices from V₀ down to V gives the closed form: **R/R₀ = f^(1/S−1)**, with f = V/V₀ the volume fraction remaining. The enrichment factor the whole campaign bookkeeps: **E(f, S) = f^(1/S−1)**. The form's honesty is the lesson: the exponent (1/S − 1) is small — one ninth at the premise's S = 10 — so the enrichment grows as a fractional power of the volume thrown away: to gain a factor of E you throw away roughly E^(S/(S−1)) of your water.
