# Vol VI · Chapter 37 — Sodium: The Castner Cell at the Bench

## Lecture 0 — The Campaign Gate (the element everywhere, the metal nowhere)

Volume VI opens a different kind of bench. Volumes I–III built the reader's chemical literacy, Volume IV rebuilt useful things, Volume V drew the whole reagent network as one graph — and every chapter of them worked at the household's own scale, with the household's own temperatures, the kitchen ceiling the Safety Charter never once lifted. This volume goes where the premise finally forces it: the elements that the ruined world holds only as their own compounds, locked in lattices so stable that no kitchen fire, no displacement trick, no precipitation train will ever liberate them. The campaign's name says what it is: one element per chapter, one demonstrated route each, every one of them weeks of real work even for the practitioners who know exactly what they are doing. The campaign protocol is the volume's shared spine (P22, the map's own new pattern): a stated goal, staged milestones, mid-course diagnosis, per-stage yield bookkeeping, and an explicit keep-or-abandon verdict at the end — never a single heroic afternoon.

The pencil-and-paper case first, the way the book always starts. Take the household's sodium census — it is enormous, and Chapter 2 already priced most of it. Table salt is NaCl, 39.34 wt% sodium by the label arithmetic the reader has run since the first labs. Baking soda is NaHCO₃. Washing soda is Na₂CO₃·10H₂O. Soda ash is Na₂CO₃, which is Na₂O wearing a CO₂ coat, the coat trick of Chapters 14 and 30. Borax is the 11.34%-boron label of Chapter 3's exercise. Drain cleaner is NaOH — the very reagent this chapter will melt. The element is everywhere, in every room of the house, in pounds. **And the metal is nowhere.** Not one gram of metallic sodium exists in the ordinary household, or in the ordinary ruined city, because sodium metal reacts with the world itself — with the water in it, above all. The campaign's first lesson fits on one line: **sodium is one of the crust's most abundant metals wearing a full-body cast, and taking the cast off is an electrochemical operation.** [the abundance class: the standard geochemistry ranking, convergent; the household census: the book's own Ch 2–3 batteries]

So the goal is stated the campaign way, small and honest: **one kept gram of sodium metal, stored under oil, with its Faradaic bookkeeping closed.** Not a demonstration bead, not a flash in a dish — a sample that survives the week. And the honest ledger starts immediately, because the demand side of this campaign is nearly empty: the household has almost no use for sodium metal at all. Its reagent-network value is real but narrow — the strongest reducing agent the organic bench (Volume VII) will want, the drying and hydrogen faces, the ethoxide trade — and its retail face, where the world still works, costs less per gram than the electricity this chapter will spend making it. That is the campaign's second lesson, and P21's own (make-vs-buy accounting, the map's second new pattern): **buying wins more often than the Atlas premise admits, and the honest answer is itself content.** The campaign runs anyway — because this book's premise is the world where buying has failed, and because the practiced route is the dividend that survives the supply chain. The verdict at the gate is therefore already stated and will be re-tested at the close: *keep* — but keep at the recognition bench.

**The binding lines, stated as always — eleven this chapter (ten inherited, one new; the count stated for the review's gate).** The ten inherited lines stand as the book has held them: the tasting rule (silent here by construction — this chapter's benches are ledgers, nothing ever tasted), the toxic-metal fence (unworn and standing), the never-combination, the pressure line (this chapter gives it its hardest face yet — a cell that manufactures oxygen at one electrode and, when the water problem bites, hydrogen at the other; never sealed, never capped, not once), the flame line (no flame rides near the cell or the coalescing bath), the peroxide discipline, the bench/food separation, the scale line (the consumer-scale ceiling of Chapter 28 — and this chapter is the first that honestly lives *below* its own ceiling), the response line (its materials face: what to do when molten caustic or a sodium bead goes wrong, stated in the Safety Table with the never-water rule in capitals), and the sealed-source line (Chapter 29's own, inherited and silent). The one new line, this chapter's own contribution, the campaign's first: **the molten-caustic line — molten sodium hydroxide at 320–340 °C is corpus-demonstrated recognition-class knowledge, never a reader bench.** Molten caustic destroys flesh faster than the eye can blink, etches glass, attacks most metals, and holds its heat long after it looks innocent. The practitioners this chapter cites ran their cells with face shields, outdoors, at arms' length, after years of practice; the book will price their runs to the milligram and hand the reader none of their temperatures as an invitation. The campaign's benches, all of them, are **ledgers**.

**The element discipline: zero admissions, and one campaign target.** Sodium the *element* has been on the book's tables since Chapter 2 — the label arithmetic, the salts, the waterglass of Chapter 30's batch. What this chapter adds is the metal as a *target*, and three new rows to the battery, all priced before they are named: **NaOH·H₂O 58.012** (the monohydrate — the near-miss trap of Lab 37.1), **Na₂O 61.979** (the oxide under soda ash's coat), and **Na₂CO₃·10H₂O 286.138** (washing soda, the ten-water hydrate, 62.96 wt% water of crystallization — the heaviest coat in the book's sodium shelf). The discipline in one line: **the metal is admitted as a target, not as a bench species; the battery grows, the bench does not.**

## Lecture 1 — Electricity as a Reagent (the Coulomb ledger)

**How you could have found this.** Ask of the sodium shelf the book's oldest question — *what holds this stuff together?* — and the answer is a wall. NaCl's lattice is the strongest sort of ionic binding the reader has met; sodium's oxidation in air is faster than the reader can watch. Every chemical reducing agent on the household shelf is either too weak for this wall (carbon, hydrogen — smelting-class chemistry that fails on sodium's thermodynamics, not its economics) or so strong it is itself a hazard the book has already fenced. The discovery is the one the nineteenth century made: **if no chemical reagent will give the electron back to sodium, use the one reagent that is not a chemical at all — the coulomb.** Electricity as a reagent is the book's P5, and this chapter is its purest face: not a battery-making curiosity, not a plating trick, but the *only door* out of the strongest lattice on the shelf.

The history is short and the corpus carries it in the performer's own words: sodium "was first isolated back in 1807 by Sir Humphry Davy… pretty much exactly the same way" — a current through molten caustic — and the route "patented in 1888 by Hamilton Castner… the main industrial route… up until around 1926" [single-source: the corpus Castner paper's transcript, printed as found; convergent with the standard-history class]. Note what that history quietly says: **the bench cell and the industrial cell are the same invention.** Castner did not scale up a different chemistry; he scaled up *this* one, and it ran the world's sodium for nearly forty years, until the Downs cell's fused NaCl–CaCl₂ melt took the trade in the mid-1920s [the textbook class, convergent; the corpus run's own forward promise names the Downs cell as its successor, single-source]. The campaign opener is therefore unusual among this book's chapters: the reader is not approximating an industry from below — **the industry was this bench.**

**The Coulomb ledger.** Faraday's law is the ledger's whole grammar, and the reader has earned it across Chapters 4 and 27; this chapter is where it finally carries a target metal. One mole of electrons, z = 1 for Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na:

**m = M·I·t/(z·F)**, with M(Na) = 22.98977 g/mol and F = 96,485 C/mol — the book's Ch 27 pin. [computed: every figure in this lecture is a Lab 37.1 battery row]

Read the law as three campaign facts, because that is what it is here. **First, the ampere-hour is a chemical quantity**: one ampere-hour carries 0.037311 mol of electrons and is therefore 0.8578 g of *ideal* sodium — the grid in Lab 37.1 runs this conversion across every current and duration the bench will ever see, 12/12 cases round-trip exact. **Second, the theoretical cell is cheap beyond intuition**: at the performer's own 5 V and 5 A, a gram of ideal sodium costs 1.1658 Ah — 5.8290 Wh, less electricity than a bright flashlight burns in an hour. The reader who has priced nothing but reagents until now should sit with that number: the campaign's limiting reagent is not electricity, and never was. **Third — the honest one — the theoretical gram is not the real gram.** The corpus's own run, priced to the milligram in Lab 37.1: 5 A for an hour of melt time made a theoretical 4.2889 g and yielded **540 mg — 12.59% Faradaic** [single-source: the corpus Castner run, the yield honestly owned in-source; the register's own side-reading 12.6% reproduced]. The gap between the ideal and the demonstrated is this chapter's real subject, and the next two lectures are its anatomy: one failure mode is chemistry (the water), one is physics (the window), and neither is the practitioner's fault.

**Why molten, and never aqueous.** The reader's electrochemistry until now has been aqueous — the zincate bath of the corpus's zinc paper (N76), the copper recovery of Chapter 19's axis, the chloralkali cousins of the coming Chapter 42 — and every aqueous cell shares one rule: at the cathode, water is also a customer. For sodium there is no aqueous route at all, because water reduces first, every time, at any sane voltage: the cell makes hydrogen, and the metal stays a cation. The campaign's melt is not a stylistic choice; it is **the only way to remove the competing customer from the shop**. Molten NaOH has no water *if* the operator keeps it that way — and the cruel joke of this process, which Lecture 3 prices, is that the cell itself manufactures water at its own anode, and hands the competing customer back its seat.

## Lecture 2 — The Melt and the Window (physics the cell must obey)

**The melt.** Sodium hydroxide melts at 318.4 °C — "318 C actually," in the corpus performer's own correction of his own round number, and the two readings agree to the decimal the book needs [convergent: the handbook class; the in-source figure]. Everything in the Castner cell follows from that one number, and the chapter's temperature table is deliberately short:

| Substance | The point | The campaign meaning |
|---|---|---|
| NaOH melting point | 318.4 °C | the floor — below it, no ions move, no current, no metal |
| sodium melting point | 97.81 °C | the metal arrives as liquid beads in the hot zone (the performer's own "a little bit under 100 C" — agrees) |
| sodium boiling point | 883 °C | far above the window — the metal does not vaporize here (that face belongs to the campaign's later alkalis) |
| the window's far wall | ~340 °C | above it the product redissolves [single-source: the corpus run] |
| the coalescing bath | ~100 °C | the storage-side melt that merges the beads [single-source: the corpus run] |

**The window.** The cell's working band is the 21.6 °C between 318.4 and ~340 °C — "around 323 C (318 C actually), but if we go too much higher, say 340 C… sodium becomes slightly soluble in the sodium hydroxide melt… it will just dissolve back into the melt," and that, in the performer's flat verdict, "completely ruins the process" [single-source: the corpus transcript, printed as found]. This is the campaign's purest example of a pattern the book has met as a verdict before (Chapter 5's crystallization window, Chapter 14's firing bands): **a process temperature is not a target, it is a corridor — and the corridor here is twenty degrees wide, on an instrumentless bench.** How does the campaign operator hold a 21-degree corridor with no thermocouple? The corpus's answer is beautifully instrumentless and beautifully honest: the melt's own *look* and the cell's own *amperage* are the thermometer — the solid charge liquefies slowly rather than at once (the slow-dissolution datum), the current surges the moment the melt goes ionic ("5 V → 5 A" at the melt), and the run is kept on the low side of the band, extracting early rather than driving hot. The reader should notice what that discipline *is*: the duty cycle of Lecture 3 doing double duty as temperature control — process chemistry and instrumentation collapsed into one habit.

**Who floats.** The product's fate in the cell is decided by density, and the densities are the book's own convergent pins: sodium 0.968 g/cm³ (solid, 20 °C), sodium hydroxide 2.13 g/cm³ (solid crystal), sodium chloride 2.165 g/cm³ (the Chapter 4 pin, along for the comparison). At 45.4% of the hydroxide's density, the metal cannot stay down: every bead born at the cathode rises through the melt the way ice rises through a pond, riding 1.20× its own density of buoyant margin — and the corpus observation carries the melt itself, because the performer's sodium demonstrably rises and is collected [the in-source observation printed as evidence; the solid-density anchors convergent]. That is the whole design logic of the Castner collector, and it is worth saying plainly: **the cell needs no moving parts because the product separates itself.** The geometry is gravity's business, and the campaign inherits it for free.

**The handoff, stated now because the campaign will need it.** The reader should bank one contrast immediately, because Chapter 38 will invert it: molten aluminium is *denser* than the cryolite-class melt it is born into, and sinks to the cell's floor — the density-inversion datum of the corpus's Hall–Héroult paper (N133), the campaign's next chapter. Sodium floats to a collector at the top; aluminium sinks to a sump at the bottom; the two collectors of this volume sit at **opposite ends of their cells**, and the campaign's apparatus discipline will make the reader prove which is which before any current flows. [single-source: the N133 datum; the convergent solid densities above]

## Lecture 3 — The Water Problem (the cell's own sabotage, priced)

**How you could have found this.** Write the cell's ideal bookkeeping from the electrode chemistry alone. At the cathode, the dry ideal: 4 Na⁺ + 4 e⁻ → 4 Na. At the anode, the hydroxide must give up its electron: 4 OH⁻ → O₂ + 2 H₂O + 4 e⁻. Add them and the cell's equation is **4 NaOH → 4 Na + 2 H₂O + O₂** — balanced to the atom, and carrying the process's whole tragedy in its middle term: **the anode manufactures water.** Not as a mistake, not as an impurity — as the stoichiometric companion of every oxygen atom the cell liberates. The corpus performer states the consequence in the flattest possible words: "Water is a lot easier to electrolyse than sodium hydroxide, so it subtracts from our yield" [single-source: the transcript, printed as found]. The sabotage has two arms, and the ledger prices both.

**Arm one: the coulomb thief.** The water the anode makes dissolves into the melt, diffuses to the cathode, and — being easier to reduce than sodium ions — takes the cathode's electrons for itself: 2 H₂O + 2 e⁻ → H₂ + 2 OH⁻. Every molecule that does this steals a coulomb that was booked for metal. Lab 37.1's ceiling battery makes the arithmetic exact: run the full recycle and the cell's net equation collapses from the dry ideal to **2 NaOH → 2 Na + H₂ + O₂** — the same oxygen, the same caustic, and *half* the sodium per electron. **The water problem's theoretical ceiling is exactly 50.0%** — and the reader should feel the vertigo of that number: a perfectly built, perfectly dry-till-the-first-minute Castner cell *cannot* exceed half efficiency once its own anode-water finds the cathode. The corpus run's 12.59% sits below the ceiling for reasons the ledger names honestly: a real cell also loses current to wire resistance, to the melt's own impurities, and to the dissolved water's second arm.

**Arm two: the product thief.** Worse than stealing electrons, the water steals the product already made: 2 Na + 2 H₂O → 2 NaOH + H₂ — the metal's own fire equation (Lab 37.2's balance battery runs it twice, once as "fate" and once as "fire," because they are the same sentence). Sodium beads that survive the cathode can be dissolved back by the very water the anode made, which converts hard-won metal back into feedstock and hands the hydrogen to the headspace. This is why the pressure line earns its capitals in this chapter: a badly water-shorted cell is simultaneously making hydrogen at one electrode and oxygen at the other, over a hot caustic melt, in whatever vessel the operator built. **Never sealed. Never capped. Not once.**

**The anode ledger, per hour.** Lab 37.1 prices the sabotage at the corpus's own current: at 5 A, the anode's ideal bookkeeping manufactures **1.6804 g of water per hour** — against a demonstrated product rate of 0.540 g/h. Read that ratio twice: at the run's honest efficiency, **the anode makes 3.11 g of water for every gram of sodium the cell yields. Water outmasses the product three to one.** No amount of operator skill changes the anode's arithmetic; the only question the operator controls is where the water goes, and how long it is allowed to stay.

**The duty cycle.** The corpus's process answer — the one this chapter's map entry names — is the run-extract-heat-cool-restart discipline: run the cell while the melt is fresh; extract the beads early (the corpus run pulls at ten minutes, not at the hour); **heat** the melt above the working band so the water leaves as steam; cool back into the window; restart. The cycle is not a ritual, it is a water-inventory maneuver — each turn of the cycle spends heat to drive the anode's own product out of the melt, buying back the dry conditions the cell had at minute zero. And the honest verdict arrives with it, in the performer's own words: the process is "just bad" — and immediately, in the same breath, "quite convenient for small quantities" [single-source, printed as found]. That is not a contradiction; it is P22's whole teaching in one sentence. **The campaign keeps the process because the small-quantity regime is the only regime this bench has, and the bookkeeping — not the hope — is what makes it survivable.**

## Lecture 4 — The Cell as Apparatus (improvisation priced, lessons paid for)

**The cell body.** The corpus cell is a stainless-steel measuring cup — the kitchen's own vessel, pressed into the hardest service on the book's shelf [single-source: the corpus run; P6's improvisation face, worn openly]. The choice is not quaint, it is materials chemistry: molten NaOH etches glass (the book has met this rule at the waterglass chapter's torch fusion — a two-channel corpus rule, "molten sodium hydroxide will etch and destroy glass") and attacks a long list of metals; stainless steel and nickel are the survivors, and the campaign's apparatus rule follows: **no glass in or near the melt, steel and nickel only, and the cup that held the last run is not going back to the kitchen — the bench/food line worn at the vessel itself.** The refurbish datum belongs to the same honesty ledger: the cup is wire-brushed between runs, not replaced [single-source] — the campaign's frugality is a stated face, not an accident.

**The graphite→nickel lesson.** The corpus's first attempt failed honestly, and the failure is this lecture's centerpiece because it is the campaign's method in miniature: **the graphite cathode fell apart in the melt.** The expectation was anode erosion — and indeed the graphite *anode* does erode slightly, which the performer owns as a standing cost — but it was the *cathode* that failed, and the fix was empirical: a nickel cathode swap, and the cell ran [single-source, the observation and the fix printed as found]. The mechanism is honestly open in the corpus — no in-source explanation is offered, and the book will not invent one — but the *lesson* generalizes the way campaign lessons must: **in molten caustic, electrode material is a consumable hypothesis; design the cell so the hypothesis can fail cheap, and keep the spare on the bench before the first amp flows.** The reader who wants the pattern named has met it before: it is the honest-failure face (P9) wearing an apparatus coat, and the campaign will re-meet it in Chapter 38, where the consumable anode is not a failure at all but the process's own design.

**The storage face.** The product's afterlife is the campaign's quietest discipline, and the corpus holds it in two datums: the beads are stored under mineral oil, and they are coalesced — heated gently under the oil at ~100 °C until the beads merge into one handleable glob [single-source]. The 2.19 °C margin between the coalescing bath and sodium's melting point (Lab 37.2's arithmetic) is the chapter's tidiest instrumentless specification: the bath is chosen *just above* the metal's melt point, close enough to merge the beads, far below anything that could trouble the oil's flash. And the storage face is a three-element pattern in this book's own corpus now — lithium under oil (N100), cesium under oil (N105), sodium under oil (N132) — the alkali archive, one rule for the whole family: **metal under oil, oil in a sealed labeled container, container far from water, in quantities sized in grams, not kilograms.**

**The amalgam warning, recognition-only.** The corpus also holds the sodium–mercury pair (N316) — the demonstration that sodium dissolves into mercury to form an amalgam, and the register's own hazard line is printed here exactly because it is the recognition-class teaching in one sentence: the combination "is usually enough to boil the mercury, and light the sodium on fire" [single-source, the partial-head-level gather, flagged; P11's recognition face]. The book's mercury discipline (Chapter 28's fence, Chapter 43's coming half-chapter) already fences mercury to recognition; sodium-amalgam making is fenced with it. **The chapter teaches the amalgam exists; it teaches nothing else about making one.**

## Lecture 5 — The Other Route and the Verdict (make-vs-buy, honestly closed)

**The chemical route the shelf does own.** For every element this campaign will chase, there is one question to ask before any cell is built: *is there a chemical reduction the household shelf can actually pay for?* For sodium, the corpus's answer is yes — the menthol route (N04): sodium hydroxide reduced by magnesium, NaOH + Mg → Na + MgO + ½ H₂, balanced to the atom in Lab 37.2's battery, run at a gentle reflux in a high-boiling organic solvent with menthol riding as a catalyst, the metal forming as a dispersable bead-shed that the operator collects from the cooled mix [the route class: single-source corpus, printed at route level; the equation computed]. The route's beauties are real: no 318 °C melt, no 5-ampere supply, no oxygen anode — magnesium does the electrochemistry for you, pre-paid at the shelf. Its costs are equally real: a flammable solvent held at reflux; hydrogen as the byproduct (0.532 L per gram of sodium at 25 °C — Lab 37.2's figure); magnesium turnings that burn if mishandled; and a separation train at the end — the beads arrive dispersed in solvent and suspended solids, not as a collected glob floating free. The corpus's own family tree is the honest gloss: the sodium route is the ancestor of the potash routes the register holds (the hydroxide variant), and the replication test — an independent practitioner running the same route to check it — is itself in the register, the amateur literature's own peer review wearing a bench coat.

**The comparison, priced.** Lab 37.2's route ledger runs both doors on the same per-gram page, and the shared stoichiometric line is the teaching: both routes must cut the same sodium out of the same hydroxide, so **both pay 1.7398 g of NaOH per gram of metal** — the electro route pays 1.1658 Ah on top of it; the menthol route pays 1.0572 g of magnesium and a hydrogen byproduct on top of it. At stipulated exercise prices both routes cost *cents* per gram (the Exercise 37.5 gates), which delivers P21's verdict with unusual force: **money does not decide this campaign, and never did.** What decides it is hazard topology. The cell's hazards are a 320 °C caustic melt, an oxygen anode, and a hydrogen risk that appears exactly when the process is failing; the menthol route's hazards are a flammable solvent at reflux, a pyrophoric-adjacent metal charge, and hydrogen at a standing slow rate. Neither hazard set belongs in a kitchen, both belong outdoors with a clear plan — and the campaign's verdict between them is the reader's to close in Exercise 37.5, with the book's own preference stated plainly: the cell is kept because its bookkeeping is *auditable* — every coulomb is countable, every loss has a term in the ledger — and auditable failure is the only kind a campaign can learn from.

**The verdict at the gate.** The chapter opened with the goal and the honest demand ledger; it closes here. **Keep.** The corpus's demonstrated arithmetic is the campaign's opening statement of work: 540 mg per hour-class run at 12.59% Faradaic, water outmassing product 3:1, the 100-gram scale priced honestly at **185 hours** of run time — "only a couple of people online" have made more than 100 g, says the corpus, and Lab 37.1's arithmetic shows why better than the sentence does [single-source; computed]. Against that: the route is *real* — Davy's own, Castner's trade, runnable at the bench in a measuring cup, its every failure mode named, priced, and honest. The campaign keeps the process, files the verdict, and hands the reader to Chapter 38, where the same coulomb ledger meets a melt that eats the anode on purpose and a metal that sinks. The gate's promise is the one P22 always makes: **the second campaign is cheaper than the first, because the ledger came with you.**
