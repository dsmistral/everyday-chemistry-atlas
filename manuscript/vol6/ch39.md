# Vol VI · Chapter 39 — Lithium: The Battery Route at the Bench

## Lecture 0 — The Campaign Gate (the metal the household owns only as an ion)

The campaign's third chapter opens with the strangest twist yet, and the honesty of the whole volume depends on stating it before anything else: **the household owns almost no lithium, and what it owns is not metal.** Sodium's chapter taught that an element can be everywhere while the metal is nowhere; aluminium's taught that both can be everywhere and the campaign can still have a point; lithium's teaches the third lesson, the one the modern world actually runs on: **the lithium the household owns is sealed inside devices, and inside the rechargeable cell it exists only as an ion.** The phone in the pocket, the laptop on the desk, the drill in the garage — the lithium in every one of them is dissolved into the chemistry of the cell, intercalated between the layers of a metal-oxide lattice, shuttling back and forth as Li⁺ and never once present as the metal. The one household object that contains metallic lithium at all is the *primary* lithium cell — the camera battery, the CR-class coin — and the corpus's own face for that route (N100, the Canadian Chemist's dump-battery teardown) is pinned recognition-only by Chapter 19's table. [convergent: the standard battery chemistry; the cell works because the lithium is an ion]

So this chapter runs the campaign's honest-negative arc on purpose, the way the map designed it: **gathered, attempted, failed, diagnosed** — and the fourth word is the deliverable. The corpus anchor is Scrap Science's four-part battery series, and its Part 1 is the register's cleanest honest negative: a performer who collected 163 watt-hours of dead cells, promised his viewers a lithium extraction, ran two different chemistries at it, obtained no lithium at all, and then — the part that makes it a chapter instead of a blooper — *diagnosed why*, on camera, with the flame test as the verdict instrument and the comment thread's mineral-chemistry diagnosis hearted as the answer. [single-source: N153, the run's own figures at transcript level] The campaign does not hide this outcome. The campaign *is* this outcome: the ledger of an attempt that closes its demand side honestly is worth exactly as much as the ledger of one that yields a gram — more, actually, because the failure carries the mechanism.

The goal, stated the campaign way, small and honest: **one closed bookkeeping of the battery-lithium attempt — the feedstock ledger priced in watt-hours and grams, the two competing methods written out with their hidden assumptions named before the run, the run narrated at its own figures to its own verdict, the verdict diagnosed with the chemistry that caused it, and the demand ledger closed the P22 way: what a practicing chemist in a ruined world actually needs lithium for, and what the dead-battery bin is really worth.** No gram of lithium is claimed anywhere in this chapter; the yield column will be empty for the third campaign chapter running, and this time the corpus's own performer states the reason on camera. The chapter's bench-scale deliverable is the recognition-bench skill the run itself used: the flame test as the instrumentless lithium verdict (P9), the discipline of Chapter 13's scheme worn at campaign scale, with the one new number the chapter adds to the Atlas: the wavelength.

The corpus's own run honored this line before any other — its first act was an overnight discharge — and this chapter follows it there.

## Lecture 1 — What the Cell Actually Contains (the anatomy, the ion that is not a metal)

The pencil-and-paper case first. Take the cell apart on paper before any tool touches one, because the paper version is where the campaign's target becomes clear. A lithium-ion cell is a jelly-roll of four layers: a cathode, which is a thin **aluminium foil coated with a lithium transition-metal oxide** (lithium cobalt oxide, LiCoO₂, in the classic chemistry; a nickel–manganese–cobalt blend in the NMC class); an anode, which is a thin **copper foil coated with graphite**; a polymer **separator**; and an organic **electrolyte** — a lithium salt dissolved in an organic solvent, soaking the roll. [convergent: the standard cell anatomy] The campaign-relevant chemistry is in that first layer: the lithium is *inside the oxide*. LiCoO₂ is a layered oxide with the lithium ions sitting between the cobalt–oxygen sheets, and the cell works precisely because those ions are mobile — they shuttle to the graphite on charge, back to the oxide on discharge, and at no point in the cell's life does the metal Li⁰ appear. The extraction problem is therefore not ore-to-metal; it is **ion-to-compound**: the campaign's realistic target is a shelf salt of lithium (the carbonate, Li₂CO₃), not the metal, and the metal step — molten-salt electrolysis of fused LiCl at better than 400 °C, the Castner/Hall–Héroult class of Chapters 37 and 38 — is priced below, fenced, and declined.

The molar bookkeeping of the target lattice, computed (P10, and Lab 39.1 re-derives every figure below): the atomic weight A(Li) = 6.94 [Ch 3 pin — the book's standing value, pinned at Chapter 3's own element table]; cobalt 58.933 [Ch 11 pin]. So LiCoO₂ = 97.871 g/mol — **already the book's own number: Chapter 11 printed it in its assay face, \"the battery cathode… 60.2% Co — the spent cathode is a cobalt ore by assay\"** [Ch 11 pin; re-derived below] — and the lithium fraction is 6.94/97.871 = **7.091 wt%**, this chapter's own new figure for the same lattice (Chapter 11 priced its cobalt; the campaign prices its lithium: the same coat, one-fourteenth lithium by mass, three-fifths cobalt by the chapter that owns the assay). The bookkeeping sentence that prices the whole chapter: *the lightest metal on the table is locked in a heavy oxide, at the smallest mass fraction the campaign has yet targeted.* Aluminium's chapter priced the metal at 26.982 g/mol out of 101.961 (26.4 wt% of its oxide); lithium's prices it at 6.94 out of 97.871. The campaign's third target is its poorest ore yet — by a factor of nearly four.

The feedstock ledger, at the corpus's own figures (N153, stated in-source): the run collected **163 watt-hours** of dead lithium-ion cells, which the performer priced as "**at least 12 g of lithium content overall**" — a floor, stated as a floor ("hopefully we'll extract a large portion of that if we're lucky"). The engine prices the floor's ratio: 12 g / 163 Wh = **0.0736 g of lithium per watt-hour**, and prices what that means per cell: a laptop-class 18650 cell at 3.7 V nominal and 2.5 Ah carries 9.25 Wh, so 163 Wh is a bit over 17.6 such cells, and the floor is **0.681 g of lithium per 9.25-Wh cell**. [single-source: N153's stated figures; the per-Wh ratio and the per-cell figures computed from them] The register notes the same heuristic appears again in the series' Part 4 recap ("around 160 watt-hours of lithium-ion battery") — the two statements agree, and the campaign carries the floor as a floor: the real content is somewhat higher, the honest minimum is 12 g.

And the contrast face, because the corpus owns one: the **primary lithium cell**. A primary cell — the CR-class coin, the camera battery — is built with an actual foil of metallic lithium as the anode, and the corpus's face for that route is the Canadian Chemist's dump-battery teardown (N100): lithium from dead cells, melted and stored under mineral oil, verified by the water-drop cascade — 2 Li + 2 H₂O → 2 LiOH + H₂, the balanced equation of Chapter 11's water-drop lecture, priced by Chapter 10's own figure at **1.7626 L of hydrogen per gram of metal** [Ch 10 pin; Ch 11's equation; the engine reproduces the figure exactly]. The corpus's verdict on that face is already the book's: Chapter 19 refused lithium at the scrap-metal table as recognition-only, and Chapter 12 recorded the same performer's *refusal* to electrolyze his LiOH process stream on the metal's combustion risk. The campaign holds both lines here: metallic lithium is a recognition face (the mineral-oil melt, the water drop, the crimson flame), never a product target; the target is the carbonate from the ion.

## Lecture 2 — The Two Methods and the Promise (the route debate, the hidden assumptions named first)

The run's premise was collaborative in a way the campaign should honor: the performer put the route question to his own viewers and got an even split, and the two answers are both real chemistry. [single-source: N153] **Method 1, the nitrate-decomposition route:** dissolve the electrode materials in hot nitric acid, evaporate to the mixed nitrates, thermally decompose the nitrates back to the oxides — and then water-leach, on the stated reasoning that \"the only oxide in that mixture which should be soluble is lithium oxide.\" **Method 2, the selective-precipitation route:** dissolve in acid, then precipitate everything except the lithium — aluminium, manganese, cobalt, nickel — and take the lithium out of the residual solution as the carbonate. The performer's hybrid plan ran Method 1 first with Method 2 as the fallback, for a reason the campaign will meet again in Lecture 5: the fallback *also* electrowins the cobalt and nickel, \"which I think people were keen to see.\" The campaign's job in this lecture is not to referee the vote; it is to write both routes out with their hidden assumptions priced *before* the run narrates them — because the run's failure lives entirely inside those assumptions.

Method 1's hidden assumption is the phrase \"should be soluble,\" and it hides two chemistry facts. The first is the air problem: lithium oxide does not wait around as Li₂O. Bare alkali oxides drink CO₂ out of the air on contact — Li₂O → Li₂CO₃ — and the carbonate is only weakly soluble, which is exactly the property that makes it the target of Method 2. The second is worse, and it is the diagnosis the whole chapter converges on: **at furnace temperatures, in intimate contact with the cell's own aluminium oxide and the crucible's ceramic, lithium oxide and carbonate stop being simple salts and become minerals.** The representative reaction, priced below: Li₂O + Al₂O₃ → 2 LiAlO₂, lithium aluminate, a refractory, water-insoluble lattice — the mineral trap. The nitrate step itself is sound (4 LiNO₃ → 2 Li₂O + 4 NO₂ + O₂ on decomposition); the assumption that fails is the *stay-put* step between decomposition and leach.

Method 2's hidden key is the solubility inversion, and this is the chapter's own new contribution to the book's solubility shelf. Lithium carbonate is the least-soluble of the common alkali carbonates — the exact property that makes it the precipitation target — and it has a temperature behavior almost unique among bench salts: **its solubility falls as the water heats.** At 25 °C, 1.29 g dissolves in 100 g of water; at 100 °C, only 0.72 g. [convergent: the standard solubility tables; the inversion is the industrial converters' own working principle] Almost every salt the book has crystallized so far — copper sulfate, the nitrates, the chlorides — goes the other way: hot water holds more, cold crashes it out. Lithium carbonate inverts: **you precipitate it hot, and it stays down cold**, and the industrial route (from brine leaches) does exactly that — the hot-precipitation discipline, worth 44.2% of the yield at the numbers above (1 − 0.72/1.29, computed in Lab 39.1). The precipitation chemistry, written the way the run wrote it: 2 LiOH + K₂CO₃ → Li₂CO₃↓ + 2 KOH — potassium carbonate 138.204 [Ch 14 pin], potassium hydroxide 56.105 [Ch 14 pin], both owned by the alkalis chapter; the engine prices the dose for the run's whole feedstock: the full 12 g of lithium, if the leach had carried it as LiOH, would need 119.48 g of K₂CO₃ — a quarter-kilo of the potash the kitchen already owns. And the equation's gate, stated because the run will fail exactly here: the carbonate only drops when the product of the ion concentrations clears the solubility product. **With lithium effectively absent from the leach, no dose of any carbonate can fire.** The run's \"we had enough… maybe not\" was not a dosing problem.

The salt shelf, priced once (all computed, all re-derived in Lab 39.1; the novelty greps live in the lab, and they found the shelf not entirely new — the honest census: two of these are the book's own pins, the rest genuinely new): the hydroxide LiOH 23.947 **[Ch 10 pin — the water-drop lecture's own product, re-printed by Ch 12's pin table]**; the carbonate target Li₂CO₃ 73.888, genuinely new, at 18.785 wt% lithium; the nitrate LiNO₃ 68.944, new; the oxide Li₂O 29.879, new; the sulfate Li₂SO₄ 109.936, new; the chloride LiCl 42.393 — new, and the fused-salt electrolysis feed of the fenced metal step; and the peroxide Li₂O₂ 45.878, new, which belongs to the corpus's one *successful* lithium-compound run. (The monohydrate LiOH·H₂O 41.962 joins them in the lab — the shelf form the peroxide run actually weighed.)

That successful face deserves its paragraph, because it is the honest counterweight to everything else in this chapter, and the campaign cites it as the make-vs-buy lesson printed *before* the failure: the register's lithium-peroxide gather (N467) — 50 g of LiOH, 125 mL of 35% hydrogen peroxide, the synthesis 2 LiOH + H₂O₂ → Li₂O₂ + 2 H₂O, kept under 60 °C, dehydrated under vacuum — with the yield honestly stated at 25 g against a patent's advertised 83%, which the engine prices: 50 g of LiOH is 47.90 g of theoretical Li₂O₂, so 25 g is **52.20%** — the register's \"52%\" held to the digit by the machine. [single-source: N467, the run's own figures] And the product's doctrine is beautiful bench chemistry: lithium peroxide is the spacecraft scrubber salt — 2 Li₂O₂ + 2 CO₂ → 2 Li₂CO₃ + O₂ — it eats the crew's carbon dioxide and breathes the oxygen back. [single-source: N467's own doctrine, convergent with the standard spacecraft-chemistry class] But read the sourcing line: the LiOH was *bought*. The corpus's one clean lithium-compound synthesis starts from the shelf salt — which is the demand ledger's answer arriving early: **at this bench, lithium chemistry begins at the purchased carbonate or hydroxide; the battery is not the cheaper road to either.**

## Lecture 3 — The Run, Part I: Discharge, Dissect, and the Acid That Ate the Budget

Now the run itself, at its own figures, honored the campaign way — narrated as it happened, with the discipline and the failure both kept. [single-source: N153 throughout; the run's figures are the corpus's own]

**The discharge discipline came first, and it is the new binding line worn before it was written.** \"Before we can start extracting all of our electrode materials we don't want the batteries to catch fire on us… the best thing to do… is discharge them all completely\" — the control circuitry ripped off, wires soldered on, the cells discharged overnight into a bath of water with electrolyte in it (the performer's NaOH solution — sodium hydroxide 39.997 [Ch 14 pin]), with a nickel strip laid across the terminals of the stragglers to short them flat. [the discharge discipline: convergent with the industrial dismantling standard — dead-flat cells only] The campaign notes the tool order: this step is not chemistry, it is the safety gate, and it ran before anything else touched the cells.

**The dissection** is the anatomy of Lecture 1 made physical: the cells \"kind of fold apart into two electrodes, one being an aluminium electrode coated in our oxide mixture and one being our copper electrode coated with just some graphite.\" The membranes and plastic went to the discard with the copper — \"pretty much useless\" — and the comment thread's small riot at that word is worth keeping: *with a name like yours, I was not expecting you to throw out the copper bits.* The campaign's own bookkeeping agrees with the comments, and it is the honest kind of correction: the copper foil is the densest pure metal in the cell (Cu 63.546 [Ch 3 pin]), the anode of Chapter 12's own benches, and a dead cell is a copper mine by weight. The discard was an economy of attention, not of chemistry — and the campaign's demand ledger will price it properly in Lecture 5.

**The nitric failure** is where Method 1 met the real cell. The dissolution was run on the electrode material in hot, home-made nitric acid at approximately 75% concentration (the acid the performer had made in his own prior episode — the book's own Chapter 15 seam: the mineral-acids chapter owns that synthesis), and the consumption was catastrophic: **24 hours on, half the home-made batch was gone and only two batteries' worth of electrode material had dissolved.** \"It took me a whole day to make that nitric acid and I'm not going to do that another five times\" — and that sentence is the honest sound of a bench economics verdict (P22): the route died on its reagent bill, not on its chemistry.

The performer's own theory blamed the aluminium: \"there is quite a bit of [aluminium] and I think that's the reason we were using so much nitric acid.\" The engine prices the theory, and it holds up (Lab 39.2, computed): each 9.25-Wh cell carries on the order of 1.5 g of cathode foil plus its coat, and the foil's own oxide skin eats acid on the way through — Al₂O₃ + 6 HNO₃ → 2 Al(NO₃)₃ + 3 H₂O — so the foil alone, never mind the oxide coat's cobalt chemistry, is worth roughly a sixth of a mole of acid per cell, and the batch of ~17 cells was structurally impossible on a hobby acid budget. The bookkeeping is a campaign lesson, not an excuse: **the cell's cheapest layer — the foil the lithium never touched — priced the reagent before the target got its turn.** And the comment thread contributed the lab-level correction the run never saw in time: on the cobalt chemistry, the nitric route \"actually works — you just need to add some hydrogen peroxide to convert the cobalt in the electrode from +3 to +2\" — LiCoO₂'s cobalt sits as Co(III), kinetically stubborn against non-oxidizing dissolution, and the peroxide trick is the standard leaching medicine. [comment-level, the register's external-expert class; flagged, not corpus-demonstrated]

**Plan B, the furnace.** The remaining electrode material went into the direct thermal decomposition: 1000 °C, 15-minute holds, fed slowly, \"the plastic and solvent burning off,\" with the stated expectation that \"any aluminium should either melt at the temperatures that we're using or convert to the oxide, neither of which are in a form that will be soluble in water later.\" The crucible did not survive — \"I did almost get it out of the crucible without breaking the crucible, but we have had a casualty there\" — the cursed-instrument humor that runs through the whole series, kept here because it is also true: the campaign's furnace benches have broken their vessels before (Chapter 38's corpus run lost its own to the melt). The baked mass came out as a fused, mineral-looking oxide brick — and *that description* is the diagnosis arriving before anyone names it.

## Lecture 4 — The Run, Part II: The Alkaline False Dawn and the Flame-Test Verdict (the negative, diagnosed)

The water leach of the baked brick came back "very strongly alkaline" — \"oh yeah, that is exactly what we wanted to see… very very promising.\" Five distilled-water washes produced 800 mL of solution \"very very basic… beyond the range of my pH paper.\" The campaign teaches the false dawn for what it is, and this is the chapter's most transferable lesson: **alkalinity is not lithium evidence.** Anything soluble-and-basic in that brick — sodium and potassium residues from the cell's chemistry, stray alkali from the separator and electrolyte salts, even partly-soluble aluminate — turns a leach alkaline, and a pH strip past its range says \"strong base,\" never *which* base. [the instrument ceiling honestly: the pH paper's range is the run's own stated limit] The hypothesis the run was actually leaning on was: *lithium oxide, leached, gives LiOH* — Li₂O + H₂O → 2 LiOH, the hydroxide at 23.947 g/mol [Ch 10 pin] — and the alkaline reading is consistent with that, with a dozen other things, and with almost no lithium at all. Consistent-with is not evidence; the campaign prices it exactly that way.

**The failed precipitation.** Potassium carbonate was dosed into the leach — \"if we're lucky as I add this we will see a cloudy precipitate start forming… we had enough… maybe not\" — and nothing came down. A neutralization attempt with acid then produced \"some sort of precipitate but not from the carbonate… I don't know what that precipitate could possibly be, it's definitely not lithium\" — almost certainly aluminium hydroxide dropping out as the base was eaten (the Al(OH)₃ 78.003 face of Chapter 19's pin, and the very precipitate Chapter 38's salvage loop ran on purpose). The chemistry of the empty beaker is the Ksp gate of Lecture 2: 2 LiOH + K₂CO₃ → Li₂CO₃↓ + 2 KOH fires only when the ion product clears the line, and with the lithium absent from the start, the dose had nothing to talk to. The precipitant was never the problem. The leach was.

**The boil-down, and then the verdict instrument.** The 800 mL was boiled away and the residue crystallized (P6 — the evaporation discipline the book has worn since its earliest benches), and what the run did next is the reason this chapter exists in a book about instrumentless chemistry: it took the residue to a flame. **The flame test is the campaign's own Chapter 13 scheme — the clean loop wire, the loop-tool flame — and lithium is one of that chapter's colors:** the carmine/crimson red that Chapter 13 explicitly warns is confusable with calcium's orange-red and strontium's crimson, and that chapter's own caveat applies with full force here — **sodium contamination**. The wavelengths, printed here as the chapter's new numbers: lithium's resonance red at **670.8 nm**; sodium's overwhelming yellow doublet at **589.0 and 589.6 nm** — a color the eye reads at trace levels that would drown a lithium signal orders of magnitude larger, which is why the classical bench masks it with cobalt glass (the filter that passes the red and eats the yellow — Chapter 13's own tool). [convergent: the standard flame-emission lines and the cobalt-glass mask; the color scheme Ch 13's pin] The run's verdict, at its own words: \"the flame test was pretty much conclusive and we're pretty sure there is no lithium in whatever it is we've extracted from our oxide mixture… I think it's pretty clear that our lithium is still in here [the oxide residue].\" A yellow flame, no carmine — the instrumentless negative, delivered with the one tool every kitchen owns.

**The diagnosis — the arc's fourth beat, and the chapter's deliverable.** Where did the lithium go? The run's own answer was \"still in the residue: the decomposition process just wasn't able to get the lithium into a soluble form,\" and the comment thread, in the register's external-expert class, supplied the mechanism and got the performer's heart for it: **the bake fused the lithium into the mineral mass.** \"Lithium will be well incorporated into that mineral mass you formed after fusing… it's not present as lithium oxide, rather lithium minerals, so won't just selectively dissolve out in high yield\" — at 1000 °C, in contact with the cell's own alumina and the crucible ceramic, the lithium entered the refractory lattices; the representative equation is Li₂O + Al₂O₃ → 2 LiAlO₂, lithium aluminate (65.920 g/mol, computed), a mineral that water does not argue with. The thread's second diagnosis is the air-passivation theory — \"there will be no lithium oxide… it starts reacting with air… making lithium carbonate which is weakly soluble and very stable thermally\" — and both mechanisms point the same way: *the leach was not the step that failed; the bake was.* The run had built, at furnace temperature, the exact wrong product: not a soluble oxide but a rock. The peroxide commenter's Co(III)→Co(II) correction stands beside them as the same lesson from the nitric side: **the lithium cell is not an ore body that politely separates — its layers protect each other, and every route has to fight the cell's own design.**

The honest close, in the run's own words and the thread's, kept together: \"it'd be easy to say that in this video we didn't really achieve anything… but I'd say that we have succeeded somewhat in reducing the volume of the electrode materials\" — and the hearted comment under it: **\"It's not a failure, now we know two ways that don't work.\"** The campaign files the verdict the way the arc designed it: gathered (163 Wh, 12 g floor), attempted (two routes at real bench cost), failed (no lithium at any step, the flame test negative), diagnosed (the mineral trap, the passivation theory, the Co(III) obstacle) — and the diagnosis is the product. The yield column is empty, and for the first time in the campaign the chapter can print the performer's own burnout honestly too: \"this has taken me two or three weeks to get to this stage and I'm a bit sick of it seeing as we didn't actually get any lithium.\" The ledger keeps it.

## Lecture 5 — What the Series Got Instead (the transition-metal harvest, and the verdict at the gate)

The honest-negative arc has a fifth beat the map drew and the corpus delivered: the pivot. Part 3's description, a year later: \"We've almost given up on extracting lithium from our batteries now, so a cobalt extraction is plan B.\" What the series actually mined out of those 160-odd watt-hours is the cell's other shelf: **the manganese, cobalt, nickel, and copper** — the transition-metal harvest, every gram of it real. [single-source: N146, N147; Part 2 listing-surfaced but ungathered — the honest gap stated; the 2016 prior N98 — about a gram of cobalt from roughly two batteries, by the arc-furnace and aluminium-foil displacement route]

The manganese came out by redox state (the MnO₂ 86.936 [Ch 12 pin] of the electrode coat, separated and purified across the series); the cobalt and nickel came out by electrowinning and then by the register's first separatory-funnel metal-pair separation: the Part 4 episode dissolved the electrowon Co/Ni metal in azeotropic hydrochloric acid (Co + 2 HCl → CoCl₂ + H₂; Ni 58.693 [Ch 12 pin]), and separated the pair by ligand chemistry — thiocyanate swapped onto the cobalt, the tetrathiocyanatocobalt(II) complex \[Co(SCN)₄\]²⁻ (charge −2, the engine checks it), taken up into self-extracted butanone while the nickel stayed aqueous and green, then stripped with sodium carbonate (cobalt carbonate down, the butanone regenerated for the next pass) — the ligand-plus-solvent discrimination the episode itself called the alternative to \"the levels of witchcraft that is the separation of the rare earth metals.\" The electrowinning itself improved on camera across the series — \"a much more metallic kind of product here instead of that black flaky stuff\" — and the engine prices its axis while it is here: cobalt at 58.933 [Ch 11 pin] and z = 2 plates at 1.0994 g per ampere-hour (the Faradaic bookkeeping at the book's F = 96,485 [Ch 27 pin]). The campaign's summary sentence: **the battery gave up its transition metals to the bench that fought for them, and kept its lithium from the bench that wanted it most.**

The demand ledger, closed the P22 way — what a practicing chemist in a ruined world actually needs, and what the dead-battery bin is worth:

- **The cell is worth more as a unit than as ore.** A charged-or-rechargeable cell is *electricity, stored* — the single most refined commodity the household owns, and the one the ruined world cannot remake at any bench in this book. Dismantling a working cell is burning food. The campaign's battery bench is for the *dead* bin only — the cells that no longer hold their charge — and the bin should be emptied into new cells (the pack-rebuild route) before it is ever emptied into acid.
- **The dead bin is a transition-metal mine.** Copper foil (63.546 [Ch 3 pin]), cobalt, nickel, manganese — every one a Chapter 11/12 metal the bench can actually win, and the series proved it at gram scale. The mine's hardest gram is the lithium: 12 g of it, locked in the mineral trap the moment it is furnace-baked, priced at 7.091 wt% of the cathode coat. The campaign's verdict on the run's own chemistry is therefore kind and final: *the leach-first routes of the modern hydrometallurgical recycler — dissolve selectively, precipitate selectively, never bake the lithium into a rock — are the only routes that work, and they need the acid budget of a working lab.* The corpus's failed bake and the failed nitric batch are the two negative controls that prove the point.
- **Lithium itself: buy the salt.** The compound the practitioner needs is the carbonate or the hydroxide — the shelf salts — and the modern world sells them at commodity prices in a form the ruined world will stockpile (ceramics, greases, the pharmacy's carbonate-class medicines — the register's own fence-adjacent boundary, \"Making Bipolar Meds from Batteries,\" is registered, ungathered, and stays beyond this book's hazard charter). N467's peroxide synthesis is the model: *bought LiOH, clean compound, honest 52.20%.* The make-vs-buy verdict of Chapter 21 (P21) is here P22's: **the battery is not the cheap road to the salt; the salt is the cheap road to the chemistry.**
- **The metal: recognition-only, and rarely.** Metallic lithium's bench faces in the corpus are the primary-cell foil (N100's mineral-oil melt — recognition-only per Chapter 19), the displacement reagent for potassium and cesium (N78, N105 — the register's alkali-by-lithium family, both recognition-class), and the electrolysis nobody will run (Chapter 12's recorded refusal on the combustion risk). The demand ledger for the metal at a household bench: effectively zero. The lightest metal is the campaign's first honest *no.*

**The verdict at the gate. Keep — the attempt as the ledger, the diagnosis as the deliverable, the cell as the unit, the salt as the buy.** The honest-negative arc's third station closes with the empty yield column standing for the third chapter running — and this station's own sentence is the corpus's, kept verbatim in the record: now we know two ways that don't work, and the campaign adds the mechanism. The handoff to Chapter 40 is the map's own promise: **magnesium and calcium — the molten-salt pair** — two metals the household owns as Epsom salt and as limestone, separated by one neutron and one whole reactivity class, and the melt whose density inversion (Chapter 38's handoff datum) points the collector the opposite way. The campaign's fourth and fifth metals will come from the electrolysis the book has already priced twice; the third taught it what the cell does to a shortcut.

## Lab 39.1 — The Feedstock and the Flame Engine (Outputs Verbatim; a fresh zero-shared-code build)
