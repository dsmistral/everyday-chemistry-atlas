# Vol VI · Chapter 38 — Aluminium: The Hall–Héroult Cell at the Bench

## Lecture 0 — The Campaign Gate (the metal the household already owns)

The campaign's second chapter opens with a twist Chapter 37 did not have, and the honesty of the whole volume depends on stating it first: **this time the household already owns the metal.** The beverage can in the hand, the foil in the drawer, the window frame in the wall — all of it is metallic aluminium, already reduced, already refined, sitting there at alloy-grade purity. The campaign's first lesson is therefore not "the element is everywhere and the metal is nowhere" (sodium's sentence); it is stranger: **the element is everywhere, and the metal is everywhere too — and the campaign runs anyway, because the cell is not about supply.** It is about the reduction discipline: the one process the modern world actually uses to take aluminium back out of its oxide, priced at the bench the way the Atlas prices everything. When the scrap stream is gone — in the ruined world this premise builds, where the cans have all been remelted and the last foil is decades used — the oxide is what remains, and this chapter is what the oxide demands.

The pencil-and-paper case first, the way the book always starts. Aluminium is 8.2 wt% of the crust — the most abundant structural metal on the shelf, third among all elements after oxygen and silicon [convergent: the standard geochemistry ranking]. But that abundance is a lattice, not a resource: the aluminium of the world is locked in aluminosilicates (the clay class of Chapter 30's kaolinite) and in the oxides, and the oxide itself, Al₂O₃ — alumina, corundum, the abrasive on the shelf's sandpaper — melts at 2072 °C [convergent], a temperature the household bench cannot reach and no kitchen fire approaches. The pre-electrolysis world knew this wall intimately: before 1886, aluminium was a precious metal, fetched for more than gold in the 1850s, and the Washington Monument was capped with a 100-ounce aluminium casting in December 1884 — the largest of its time, a jewel on a monument [convergent: the standard-history class; the monument datum]. Two years later, two twenty-two-year-olds broke the wall, and the price collapsed into the commodity class the reader's kitchen now enjoys. [the history: convergent; the corpus's own Hall–Héroult paper (N133) carries the process at transcript level]

So the goal is stated the campaign way, small and honest — and this chapter's goal is smaller than sodium's, on purpose: **one closed bookkeeping of the Hall–Héroult cell — the coulomb ledger at z = 3, the melt's chemistry named and priced, the consumable anode's ledger balanced, the honest yield column left as the corpus left it: empty.** No kept gram is claimed. No bench yield is demonstrated anywhere in this chapter, and the ledger will say so in its own voice — the corpus run's first metal was a globule too small to weigh, and its second run's outcome is unseen even at the transcript level [single-source: N133, the filmot truncation class]. The chapter's deliverable at the reader's bench scale is the salvage loop of Lecture 5 (solution chemistry, the book's own safety ceiling), and the cell itself is taught at the recognition bench, fenced below.

**The binding lines, stated as always — eleven this chapter (ten inherited, one new; the count stated for the review's gate).** The ten inherited lines stand as the book has held them since Chapter 30's list: the tasting rule (silent by construction — the benches are ledgers and solution trains), the toxic-metal fence (unworn and standing), the never-combination, the pressure line (this chapter's cell breathes CO and, when the feed is damp, worse — never sealed, never covered), the flame line (no flame near the furnace head or the melt), the peroxide discipline, the bench/food separation (the can that enters the loop never returns to the kitchen), the scale line (the consumer ceiling of Chapter 28 — and this chapter's cell lives entirely *below* the ceiling, at recognition), the response line (its materials face: what to do when acid, alkali, or a hot vessel goes wrong, stated in the Safety Table), and the sealed-source line (Chapter 29's own, inherited and silent). The one new line, this chapter's own contribution, the campaign's second: **the fluoride-melt line — the 950–970 °C cryolite-class fluoride melt is corpus-demonstrated recognition-class knowledge, never a reader bench.** The reasons are the melt's own chemistry: a fluoride bath at bright-heat temperatures, an HF face the moment the feed is damp, an attack profile that takes glass and most ceramics, and off-gas that carries CO. The practitioners this chapter cites built their cell outdoors, upwind, at a scale the Safety Table prices honestly; the book hands the reader the melt's *chemistry* and none of its temperatures as an invitation. The campaign's cell is a **ledger**; the loop is the bench.

**The element discipline: zero admissions, and the fence's hardest face yet.** Aluminium the *element* has been on the book's tables since the density ledgers began — and this chapter adds six new rows to the battery, all priced before they are named: **AlCl₃ 133.341**, **AlCl₃·6H₂O 241.431** (the hydrate trap — the label "aluminium chloride" is 55.23 wt% anhydrous in its hexahydrate; the same active charge weighs 1.8106× more off the hydrate shelf), **CO₂ 44.009**, **CO 28.010**, **H₂ 2.016**, and **NaCl 58.443** — the salt row printed at the Chapter 15 convention (Cl 35.453), the seam against the 58.440 of Chapters 1/4/5/37 named in the lab and never patched, because the book keeps each chapter at its owner's convention. And one thing this chapter will *not* compute: cryolite. Na₃AlF₆ is a fluoride, and Chapter 18's binding line — *the parser will never parse an F-bearing formula in any chapter of the Atlas* — here gets the face it was written for: the melt that carries the whole process is **named in prose, recognized as the mineral it is, and refused by every calculator in the book.** The discipline in one line: **the battery grows, the bench does not; the melt is named, never computed.**

## Lecture 1 — Electricity as a Reagent at z = 3 (the triple electron price)

**How you could have found this.** Ask of the alumina on the sandpaper the book's oldest question — *what holds this stuff together?* — and the answer is the strongest lattice the reader has met on this campaign. Carbon cannot do it: the smelting chemistry that takes iron from its ore fails on aluminium's thermodynamics, not its economics — the oxide is too stable, the carbide and the volatile impurities interpose, and the blast furnace route simply does not exist for this metal. The nineteenth century's chemical answer was to spend an even hungrier metal: Deville's route reduced aluminium chloride with sodium, and it made aluminium the precious metal of the 1850s — dearer than gold, which is the textbook datum and the monument's capstone [convergent: the standard-history class]. The discovery is the one the campaign has now made twice, and it is the same discovery every isolation chapter will make: **when no chemical reagent will pay the electron debt, pay it with the coulomb.** Electricity is the reagent that is not a chemical (P5), and in 1886 — the same year the monument's metal was still novel — Charles Martin Hall in Ohio and Paul Héroult in Normandy, independently, aged twenty-two both, filed the same invention within weeks of each other [convergent: the standard history; both born 1863, both died 1914, the coincidence the sources print as found].

The z = 3 in this chapter's title is the whole difference from sodium's cell, and the ledger states it in one line. Sodium asks one electron per atom; aluminium asks three: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al. Faraday's law carries the same grammar the reader earned in Chapters 4, 12 and 27 — **m = M·I·t/(z·F)**, with M(Al) = 26.982 g/mol and F = 96,485 C/mol, the book's standing pins — and the campaign reader should read the z as a price, because that is what it is: the same ampere-hour that bought 0.8578 g of ideal sodium buys only **0.33558 g of ideal aluminium**, a 2.5561× pay cut [computed: Lab 38.1's per-Ah conversion, run at full precision — the mass-per-electron ratio M(Na)/[M(Al)/3] is the same figure]. Aluminium's heavier atom repays a third of the tax; the valence collects the rest. **The campaign's second lesson: the electron price is set by the target's column of the periodic table, and no engineering discounts it.**

The bench case, priced honestly. The corpus's own run books 72,000 C — 10 A for two hours — and the ledger makes it exact: 0.746230 mol of electrons, 0.248743 mol of aluminium, **6.7116 g theoretical** [computed: Lab 38.1 [5]; the corpus's own bookkeeping agrees at the display digit]. Against the theory the corpus sets its own expectation — about 74% of theory, roughly 4.97 g — and the engine prints that too: **4.9666 g expected**. And then the honest column, the one this chapter will not fill: run one produced a tiny globule, **unweighed**; run two's outcome is **unseen** at the transcript level [single-source: N133]. Read the three numbers in order — 6.7116, 4.9666, unweighed — and you have the campaign's whole teaching about demonstration in one column: theory, expectation, and the empty cell where a yield would go. **No gram is claimed that a balance never saw.**

**Why molten, and never aqueous.** The rule is sodium's rule, worn at a new electrode: at a cathode in water, water is also a customer, and for aluminium the aqueous route fails twice over — the metal reacts with the water it would be born into (Chapter 19's own axis: aluminium dissolves in both acid and alkali, releasing hydrogen), and the hydroxide films the product the instant it forms (Al(OH)₃, the gel the reader has precipitated since Chapter 11). The cell must therefore run in a medium with no water at all, hot enough to keep its product liquid — and that requirement is what makes the next lecture's mineral the most important shopping item in the campaign.

## Lecture 2 — The Melt and the Sink (cryolite, the thousand-degree discount)

**The problem the mineral solves.** Alumina melts at 2072 °C — a wall no bench and few furnaces can pay, and even industry found uneconomic to pay directly. The Hall–Héroult trick is not to melt the alumina at all: **dissolve it.** Cryolite — sodium hexafluoroaluminate, Na₃AlF₆, a mineral mined out of one Greenland deposit for a century — melts near 1012 °C [convergent: the database class; the 1025 °C vendor variant also circulates, disclosed] and takes alumina into solution freely: the corpus carries the bench-class datum at up to ~25 wt% dissolved alumina [single-source: N133], while the industrial cell runs its bath lean at 2–4 wt% feed [convergent] — the same melt at two different designs, both honest, the bench rich because a small cell cannot afford a feed system, the plant lean because the anode effect punishes over-feeding. The bath runs at 950–970 °C [convergent], and the thousand-degree discount is the whole economics of the metal: the oxide never becomes a liquid pure; it becomes a *solute*. [the temperature table with every label: Lab 38.2 [8]]

**Three properties, one mineral.** The corpus names the three facts that make cryolite the solvent and not just a bystander [single-source: N133, the properties named at transcript level]. *First, the solubility* — alumina dissolves to a working concentration, which no common salt matches. *Second, the electrochemical seat* — the melt carries the current without being the one that pays for it: the fluoride framework sits inert while the dissolved oxide's ions take the electrodes' business, which is why the bath is solvent and spectator both. *Third, the density inversion — the campaign's own datum.* Molten aluminium at cell temperature runs ~2.30 g/cm³ against a melt under 2.1 [convergent: the melt densities at 950–1000 °C] — **the metal is denser than its bath, and it sinks.** The collector of a Hall–Héroult cell is therefore a sump at the *floor*, and Chapter 37's handoff closes here in one sentence: sodium at 45.4% of its bath's density rides 1.20× its own density *upward* to a skimmer; aluminium rides ~1.10× *downward* to a sump; **the campaign's two collectors sit at opposite ends of their cells, and the operator who confuses them has built the wrong pot** [the N133 datum; the convergent anchors]. And one quiet dividend the inversion pays: a metal that sinks under a blanket of melt needs **no inert atmosphere** — the bath itself keeps the air off the product [single-source: N133].

**The refusal beat — the fence made executable.** Cryolite is a fluoride, and this is where the chapter's element discipline stops being a convention and becomes a wall with a chemistry reason behind it. The Chapter 18 binding line has never parsed an F-bearing formula in any chapter of the Atlas; here the line earns its keep, because the fluoride chemistry of a hot melt is not reader-class knowledge under any honest safety charter: damp feed in a fluoride bath makes HF; the bath attacks glass and most ceramics; the off-gas carries the anode's CO. So the chapter does with cryolite what the fence demands and the honest book wants anyway: it *names* the mineral (recognition — the reader should know Na₃AlF₆ when a materials label says cryolite), it carries the database's molar-mass class (209.94, the figure a handbook would print [convergent: the database class, carried in prose only]), and it computes **nothing**: no mass from the engine, no HF equation, no composition or procedure beyond the meter's row. Lab 38.1's injection battery makes the refusal executable — the parser returns its designed line on Na₃AlF₆ and on AlF₃ both: *REFUSED: F-bearing formula (the Ch 18 binding line)*. **The fence is not a rule the chapter obeys; it is a result the chapter prints.**

The reader who wants the melt's honest portrait at the recognition bench has it in one paragraph: a white mineral, translucent enough to earn its name ("ice-stone" — the Greek behind the word [the etymology class, convergent]), melting near the bright-heat band, dissolving the sandpaper's abrasive like sugar into tea, carrying the current while the oxide pays it, holding the product under its surface, and — fed damp — making the one acid the book will never teach at a bench. That portrait is the chapter's whole cryolite offering. The rest is the ledger's business.
## Lecture 3 — The Consumable Anode (the electrode that is a reagent)

**How you could have found this.** Write the electrode chemistry the way the reader has written half-equations since Chapter 12. The cathode is the campaign's target line: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al — three electrons per atom, the price of Lecture 1. The anode is where this cell surprises: in the sodium cell the anode's business was to *make* something (oxygen, and the water that ruined the yield); here the anode's business is to *be consumed*. The oxide ions of the dissolved alumina arrive at the carbon, and the carbon takes the oxygen personally: **C + 2O²⁻ → CO₂ + 4e⁻** — balanced to the atom and to the coulomb (Lab 38.1's ion arithmetic closes both rows). Scale the half-equations to clear the electrons — ×4 on the cathode, ×3 on the anode — and add, and the cell's equation is one line: **2Al₂O₃ + 3C → 4Al + 3CO₂** [computed: Lab 38.2 [12], the balance battery's first row]. Read the equation the way a purchasing agent would, because that is the honest reading: for every 4 mol of metal, the bill is 3 mol of *carbon*. **The anode is a reagent wearing an electrode's clothes.**

**The carbon ledger.** Priced per gram of product — the unit every campaign ledger shares — the anode's consumption is **0.33386 g of carbon per gram of aluminium**, and the CO₂ that leaves weighs 1.22329 g per gram of metal [computed: Lab 38.1 [6]]. The industrial cell pays the same bill at scale: the corpus carries the replacement cadence — the anode is swapped "every few days" in a running pot [single-source: N133; the cadence is the industrial class, convergent in kind] — and the reader should notice what that cadence *is*: not a failure being repaired, but a reagent being refilled. The shopping face is Chapter 37's own source in a new aisle: gouging carbon (N134) — the cheap graphite rods the hardware store sells for cutting steel — is the bench-class anode material, and the campaign's frugality (P6) has rarely had a cleaner face: the cell's most exotic requirement is met by the welding aisle.

**The Ch 37 lesson, inverted.** Chapter 37's apparatus lesson taught that a graphite *cathode* fell apart in molten caustic — an electrode failing before the process did — and generalized: electrode material is a consumable hypothesis, design the cell so the hypothesis can fail cheap. This chapter is the generalization's proof case: the graphite anode here is *designed to disappear*, at a rate the ledger predicts to the milligram, and the difference between "failing" and "consumed" is nothing but **whether the bookkeeping expected it** (P9 wearing a design coat — the honest-failure pattern, its positive face). The campaign's apparatus discipline, stated once for both chapters: **an electrode the ledger priced is a reagent; an electrode the ledger forgot is a failure.** The reader who runs the comparison exercise (Ex 38.4) will prove the anode is a design feature from the equation alone — no corpus quote needed.

**The off-gas discipline.** The anode's CO₂ is not the only gas a real pot makes: CO rides along (the carbon's partial business, the same CO the book has fenced since the charcoal chapters), and in a fluoride bath with damp feed the off-gas carries worse [the HF face: recognition-class, stated in the Safety Table's first line and nowhere else]. The discipline the corpus demonstrates is short and physical: the cell runs outdoors, the operator stays upwind, and nobody leans over a pot to see better [single-source: N133; the practice class]. This chapter's benches never test the discipline — the cell is recognition-class — but the Safety Table wears it, and Lecture 0's fluoride-melt line is where it lives.

## Lecture 4 — The Honest Run and the SiO₂ Diagnosis (the cell cannot refuse a reducible oxide)

**The run, booked honestly.** The corpus's demonstration run is the campaign's most honest hour, and the chapter prints it in the ledger's own voice [single-source: N133, the whole paragraph]. The cell ran — 10 A, two hours, 72,000 C — and the theory says 6.7116 g; the corpus's own expectation (about 74% of theory) says 4.9666 g; and the product was **a tiny globule that was never weighed**. The second round's outcome is unseen even at the transcript level [the filmot truncation class]. So the honest yield column — the one number a mining report or a patent would lead with — is **empty**, and this chapter leaves it empty on purpose: the run demonstrated the *process* (the cell worked, metal existed in the sump, the chemistry did what the equation says) and demonstrated no *yield* whatsoever. **The campaign's demand ledger for honesty: a process demonstration is not a yield; a yield is what a balance reads.** (P9, the honest-failure pattern, and the empty column is the pattern's cleanest face yet — sodium's 540 unassailable milligrams in Chapter 37 were a luxury.)

**The diagnosis — and why it is a *feed* problem, not a *cell* problem.** The corpus's own verdict on the run is the second lesson, and it is the chapter's most transferable one: the feed — 98% commercial alumina, the powder the shelf actually sells — "really probably isn't pure enough" [single-source: N133, the performer's own words, printed as found; the register's paraphrase: the honest tiny-yield verdict + the SiO₂ diagnosis]. The impurity that matters is silica-class, and the reason is electrochemistry, not sloppiness: SiO₂ is an oxide of a metal-ish element whose reduction potential sits *close enough to aluminium's* that the cell cannot refuse it. A Hall–Héroult cathode pays electrons to whatever reducible cation arrives — aluminium from the good feed, silicon from the bad — and the product comes out as an alloy, or the impurity rides the sump as a crust, and either way **the metal is not what the label promised.** The teaching, stated the campaign way: **a cell does not purify its feed; the purity must arrive in the feed.** That is why the industrial world spends a whole process (the Bayer refineries, Lecture 5) making alumina *before* any electrolysis, and why 99.5%-plus smelter-grade alumina exists as a product class at all [convergent: the industrial-class datum]. The exercise gate plants the pattern pair: P9 (the honest failure) + P12 (the bookkeeping that names the cause) — the diagnosis is only worth having because the ledger priced the expectation first.

**What the run did prove.** The reader should close the ledger the way the campaign closes every ledger, on both sides. Against the run: no weighed metal, feed-purity diagnosis standing, the outcome honestly unseen. For the run: the cell *ran* — a bench-class crucible held a fluoride melt at temperature, current flowed through it, metal appeared where the density inversion said it would, and the anode was consumed exactly as the equation books. **The physics was demonstrated; the product was not** — and that is a real and honorable chapter of the campaign record, filed the way the corpus filed it. The pattern the map will carry to Chapter 39 (lithium, from batteries): the honest-negative series is a designed arc — gathered, attempted, failed, diagnosed — and this chapter is the arc's second station.

## Lecture 5 — The Salvage Loop, the Bayer Contrast, and the Verdict

**The reader's bench-class deliverable: the second-round route.** The corpus's answer to the feed-purity diagnosis is the chapter's own deliverable, and it is the one train in this chapter the reader may actually run — at solution scale, indoors, inside the book's standing ceilings [single-source: N133, the route stated; the book prices it]. The route: scrap aluminium → hydrochloric acid (the Chapter 15 reagent, the cans dissolving with the hydrogen the reader has met since Chapter 11) → the aluminium chloride solution → sodium hydroxide (the Chapter 14 drain-opener reagent) to precipitate aluminium hydroxide → filter, wash, dry → calcine at 900 °C (the Chapter 30 kiln class) → **alumina pure enough for the cell's ledger.** The loop's figures per 100.0 g of cans at 95.0 wt% aluminium: **385.12 g of HCl** in; **5.2813 mol of hydrogen out — 129.2 L at the Chapter 10 molar-volume pin** (a gas bill the pressure line must be dressed for even at solution scale); **422.47 g of NaOH** in; **274.64 g of Al(OH)₃** out with **617.31 g of NaCl**; and after the calcination, **179.50 g of Al₂O₃** plus 95.14 g of steam — mass closures exact to the printed digit on both stages [computed: Lab 38.2 [10]]. The route's *purity* logic is the teaching: the acid takes the aluminium into solution and leaves the can-liner waste behind; the filtration takes the hydroxide out and leaves the dissolved impurities behind; the wash takes the salt out; the calcination takes the water out. **Every impurity the run-1 diagnosis named has a stage of the loop that removes it.**

**The honest reading — a loss on one ledger, a win on the other.** The loop trades 95.0 g of *remeltable metal* for 179.50 g of oxide — which is the same 95.0 g of aluminium, wearing its oxygen coat (P21's accounting, run at campaign scale). On the supply ledger that is pure loss: the metal was already reduced, and remelting it at 660.3 °C (a temperature the Chapter 30 kiln already reaches) recovers it at ~100% for the cost of fuel [convergent: the melt point; the kiln class]. On the purity ledger it is the only win available: a cell cannot refuse a reducible oxide (Lecture 4), so the oxide must arrive clean, and the loop is how clean oxide is *made* — which is, the reader should notice, the Bayer process's own sentence run at solution scale.

**The Bayer contrast.** The industrial alumina the smelters feed on is not mined as oxide: it is *refined* from bauxite — aluminium hydroxides laced with iron and silica — by dissolving the aluminium fraction in hot concentrated sodium hydroxide (the amphoteric face the reader has owned since Chapter 19: Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH + 3H₂O → 2NaAl(OH)₄, balanced in the battery), then precipitating and calcining the hydroxide back out [convergent: the Bayer history — Carl Josef Bayer's process, the standard-history class]. The loop of this chapter runs the *same* amphoteric chemistry with the polarity reversed by history: the Bayer plant starts from rock and delivers hydrate; the household starts from finished metal and delivers oxide *back* to the cell that could make it metal again. **The cans are not the household's ore — they are the Bayer plant's product, the smelter's ingot, and the rolling mill's sheet, arriving pre-reduced at the door.** The loop is a purification train, never an extraction, and the chapter's census exercise (Ex 38.7) asks the reader to close the demand ledger honestly on that fact.

**Make-vs-buy, closed (P21).** At the stipulated exercise prices (electricity $0.15/kWh; alumina $2.75/kg), the cell's line is computed without sentiment: 1.8894 g of alumina and 2.9799 Ah of charge per gram of metal, 11.920 kWh/kg at 4.0 V ideal — 16.108 at the corpus's 74% — for a cell total of **$6.98 per kilogram**, against **remelt at ~zero** [computed: Lab 38.2's gates]. The industrial band the sources carry is 13–15 kWh/kg at ~90–96% current efficiency [convergent] — the same arithmetic at industrial honesty, sitting neatly between this chapter's ideal and its corpus-class expectation. The verdict the lecture files is therefore P21's, stated at campaign scale: **for supply, remelt wins, and it is not close; the cell exists for the discipline** — the practiced route, the audited ledger, the knowledge that survives the supply chain. When the last can is remelted, the oxide and this chapter are what remain.

**The verdict at the gate.** **Keep — the cell at the recognition bench, the loop at the solution bench.** The campaign's second chapter closes its demand ledger the honest way: the household metal is remelted (P21's verdict, unappealable); the cell is taught, priced, and fenced below the reader's bench; the loop is run, closed to the milligram, and handed over as the deliverable; the empty yield column stands as the corpus left it. The handoff to Chapter 39 is the map's own promise: lithium, from batteries — the honest-negative series as a designed arc, and a metal the household owns *in a device*, waiting for a campaign of its own. The gate's promise is P22's, kept twice now: **the ledger came with you, and the third campaign is cheaper than the second.**
