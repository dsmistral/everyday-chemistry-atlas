# Vol I · Chapter 7 — Heat, Fire, and Fuels

Chapter 6 priced the still's fuel line in latent heats and left the fuel itself unexamined: a number of megajoules per kilogram, taken from a table, spent to make vapor. This chapter opens that tab and pays it in full: **fire is a chemical reaction — fuel plus oxygen giving hot products — and the reaction has a price tag, a bill of materials, and a waste stream, all of which can be computed, measured, and booked like any other ledger in this book.** The premise's discipline applies to the oldest tool in the house: the heat that runs every bench in this Atlas comes from a reaction, and a chemist who treats fire as mysterious heat is doing the same thing a cook does who treats a recipe as magic. The corpus supplies this chapter's practitioners whole: the two-barrel charcoal retort with its 50% mass loss measured on a scale (N85), the earth-kiln pyrolysis with its counter-burn draft control and +200 °C end-use datum (N88), the pit/mound/permanent-kiln method comparison (N89), the CO/H₂ off-gas flare theory and the honest second-run yield drop (N91), the hotter-burn demonstration (N92), the low-conductivity diagnosis, the doused-vs-retort verdict, and the reignition hazard (N93), the red-hot firing cue and the stacking-yield causality (N95), and the water-gas working-sign with the ~2-kWh electric-activation economics (N96).

The chapter's honest contracts, stated up front: **the ΔH values are tabulated measurements, quoted with their sources, and the stoichiometry is exact arithmetic on top of them** — but the *hearth efficiencies* (what fraction of a fuel's joules actually lands in the pot) are measured, not assumed, and the chapter's calorimeter exists precisely to measure one; **the wood and charcoal heating values are mid-band numbers from a spread** (wood ~15–20 MJ/kg depending on dryness, charcoal ~28–34) — the hearth fuels are natural products, not reagent-grade substances, and the Atlas prices them as bands, not false certainties; and **fire is the Charter's most dangerous tool after strong acids** — carbon monoxide is invisible, odorless, and produced by every badly-drafted flame, and this chapter's safety discipline (the draft, the detector, the never-an-accelerant rule) is as binding as the never-sealed rule was to Chapter 6. The energetics binding line holds here in its home territory: fire *handling* at hearth scale is bench practice; energetic-material *making* is not, and never appears.

## Lecture 0 — The hearth before the theory

Sit in front of a candle and describe only what you see, in the notebook, for one page: a wick standing in a pool of melted wax; the wax climbing the wick; a teardrop of light an inch above the wick, blue at the bottom edge, bright yellow in the body, dark in the center; the flame flickering when you breathe near it; the glass over it fogging (Chapter 6's distillate, again); the wax pool shrinking. You have just written the first thermodynamic observation in human history — the one that predates every instrument — and the whole chapter is the bookkeeping for it.

The candle is a fuel *handling system*: the wax is the fuel, the flame is where fuel vapor meets oxygen, the wick is a pump. Nothing burns that is not first a vapor or a gas — the solid wax melts, the liquid climbs, and *vapor* burns at the flame's surface. That single observation organizes everything: a fire is a **reaction zone** where fuel vapor and oxygen meet, and its shape, its color, and its products all report on how well that meeting is going. A flame that cannot get enough oxygen goes yellow and deposits soot (unburned carbon — the reaction writing its failure on the ceiling); a flame with a good oxygen supply burns blue and clean.

The chapter's two questions, asked at the hearth and answered in arithmetic: **how much heat does the fire deliver?** — thermochemistry and calorimetry, the lectures and labs ahead — and **what does the fire leave behind?** — carbon dioxide and water when the meeting goes well, carbon monoxide and soot when it goes badly, and ash when the fuel carried minerals. Both questions are bookkeeping; neither is a mystery.

## Lecture 1 — Combustion as bookkeeping: the bill of materials

A combustion reaction is a balanced equation, and the balancing is the bill of materials. For any fuel made of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen — which is every fuel in the house — the complete-combustion equation writes itself:

**C_xH_yO_z + (x + y/4 − z/2) O₂ → x CO₂ + (y/2) H₂O**

One line of algebra, and every fuel's oxygen demand follows. Lab 7.1 runs it for the household fuels: methane needs 2 mol O₂ per mol (3.99 g O₂ per g fuel), propane 5 (3.63), butane 6.5 (3.58), ethanol 3 (2.08 — the molecule carries an oxygen of its own, so it asks for less), paraffin wax 38 (3.45), and plain carbon 1 (2.66). And since the oxygen comes from *air* — only about one-fifth of it by volume, 23.2% by mass — the air demand is the O₂ demand divided by the air's oxygen share: **roughly fifteen grams of air for every gram of hydrocarbon fuel** (Lab 7.1's table). A butane lighter's 5 g charge consumes 77 g of air — about 64 L of it, a bathtub of atmosphere per lighter (Exercise 7.1). This is the fact the fire triangle states in words: *fuel, oxidizer, heat* — remove any one and the fire dies, and the oxidizer is the one people forget because it is invisible and everywhere. The candle in a sealed jar goes out when the jar's oxygen is spent (Chapter 6's never-sealed cousin, seen from the fire's side: the still must never be sealed because pressure builds; the flame suffocates because the oxidizer runs out — the same gas, the two ends of its story).

The products, booked like any ledger's outputs: burning a gram of methane makes 2.74 g of CO₂; a gram of butane, 3.03 g; a gram of wax, 3.12 g (Lab 7.1). The flue gas is *heavier* than the fuel burned — the missing mass is the oxygen, pulled from the air and sent back combined with carbon and hydrogen. A fire does not make smoke disappear; it *adds* to the atmosphere's CO₂ and water account, exactly, by the equation's coefficients.

**Incomplete combustion is the same ledger with the meeting going badly**: not enough oxygen, too much fuel vapor, a cold flame zone. Carbon burns only halfway: **2C + O₂ → 2CO** instead of C + O₂ → CO₂. Lab 7.1 prices the failure both ways — the CO route releases only **28.1%** of carbon's available energy (110.5 of 393.5 kJ/mol), and the remaining 89.9 kJ/mol is sitting in a colorless, odorless, poisonous gas that *still burns* (CO + ½O₂ → CO₂ releases the other 283.0 kJ/mol). The waste and the hazard are the same number: a yellow, sooty, lazy flame is writing CO on the air, and the safety table's first row exists because of it. The draft is not a comfort feature; it is the reaction's oxygen supply.

## Lecture 2 — The reaction enthalpy: the fire's price tag

Every reaction releases or absorbs energy, and combustion releases more of it than almost anything a household can do. The bookkeeping unit is the **reaction enthalpy** ΔH — the energy the reaction releases at constant pressure, negative for release, measured in kJ per mole of reaction as written. For combustion the values are tabulated (Lab 7.1's price list, [convergent]): methane −890.3 kJ/mol, propane −2219.9, butane −2877.5, ethanol (liquid) −1367, octane −5471, carbon −393.5, carbon monoxide −283.0, hydrogen −241.8 (to steam). The per-kilogram prices follow by division — Chapter 6's fuel-line discipline applied to every fuel in the house: methane 55.49 MJ/kg, propane 50.34, butane 49.51, gasoline's octane stand-in 47.89, ethanol 29.67, candle wax 42.0, carbon 32.76. The hearth fuels join the list as bands (wood ~16 MJ/kg air-dried, charcoal ~30 — the ranges flagged in the lab): the fossil and liquid fuels cluster near 50, the alcohols near 30 (their own oxygen is mass that burns nothing), and wood, half water and half cellulose, near 16.

**Hess's law is the ledger's double-entry rule, and the chapter's cleanest demonstration is the water-gas loop**, machine-shown in Lab 7.1 with the corpus's own chemistry (N91's CO/H₂ off-gas flare; N96's water-gas working-sign): hot carbon plus steam reacts **C + H₂O(g) → CO + H₂** at **+131.3 kJ/mol** — *endothermic*, the fire's own heat absorbed to split water. Then the products burn: CO + ½O₂ → CO₂ (−283.0) and H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O(g) (−241.8), together −524.8. The net: 131.3 − 524.8 = **−393.5 kJ/mol — exactly carbon's direct combustion, to the last digit**. Two routes, one destination, one price: energy is a state function, and Hess's law is the guarantee that the books close no matter which page of the ledger you write them on. This is P12's yield bookkeeping transplanted to energy — the joules balance like the grams do — and it is why ΔH tables can be built from each other at all.

The loop is also a working chemist's tool, and the corpus uses it twice: the retort's off-gas flare (N91 — the pyrolysis gases burn at the stack, which is why a working retort shows a flame at its vent; the theory datum, in-source) and the water-gas sign (N96 — the *working sign* that the charge is hot enough: water hitting incandescent carbon makes CO + H₂, which burns with its own character). The same chemistry, read one way, is a reaction; read another way, is a diagnostic; read carelessly, is a reigniting hazard (Lecture 4's safety row).

## Lecture 3 — Calorimetry: measuring what the fire actually delivered

The ΔH table says what a fuel *can* deliver. What it *actually* delivered to your pot is a different number, always smaller, and the difference is the chapter's own honesty. The measuring instrument is the oldest one in the house: **a known mass of something, a thermometer, and a change in temperature**. The engine is one line: **q = m·c·ΔT** — the heat delivered equals the mass times the specific heat times the temperature rise. Water's specific heat is 4.184 J/(g·K) [convergent], and it is the star of the table: **9.3× iron's per gram** (Lab 7.2), which is why the kettle's job is mostly about the water and only secondarily about the metal, and why water is the universal coolant, calorimeter fluid, and heat-bank (Chapter 4's colligative star, repriced as a heat reservoir).

The bench instrument is the **soda-can calorimeter**: an empty aluminum can, 100 g of water, a thermometer, a tiny alcohol lamp or a candle underneath, a wind screen of anything. Burn a weighed charge, read the ΔT, price the delivery. Lab 7.2 runs one: 0.80 g of ethanol (23.74 kJ available) raises 100 g of water + the 13 g can by 20.0 K, so the delivery was (100 × 4.184 + 13 × 0.900) × 20.0 = **8.60 kJ — an efficiency of 36.2%**, with the remaining ~64% booked as the loss ledger: convection past the can, radiation missing it, the water's own evaporation, the flame's hot exhaust leaving un-caught. The instrument's honesty is that the escape is *measured*, not guessed — P12's ledger discipline applied to energy — and the exercise battery pushes it: a lid and a wind screen (Exercise 7.3's improved run, ΔT 28.0 K) buys the efficiency to 50.7%, and every improvement is visible in the same arithmetic.

Two cross-pins from the book's own chapters fix the calorimeter's place in the train. First, **the kettle-to-dry ratio**: heating a liter from tap to boil costs 355.6 kJ; boiling that liter *away* costs 2256 kJ — **6.34×** the heating step (Lab 7.2, using Chapter 6's own latent heat, its RA-1-corrected 2.256 MJ/kg). Every evaporative rung in the train — Chapter 5's crystallizer, Chapter 6's still — has been quietly paying this multiplier; the hearth is where the bill is read whole. Second, **the food Calorie trap**: 1 food Calorie = 1 kcal = 4.184 kJ — the label's "Cal" is the chemistry kilocalorie, a 1000-fold trap in the units, machine-shown in the lab with a sugar cube (2.4 g sucrose = 39.6 kJ = 9.5 Cal): the body runs on the same combustion chemistry this chapter measures, at the same exchange rate.

The appliances, priced by the same engine (Lab 7.2): the kettle delivering 1.50 kW to its water draws 3.75 kW of fuel power at a 40% stove capture — 243 g of methane per hour, a number a gas meter could check; the candle is a **~58 W heater** (5.0 g of paraffin an hour at 42 kJ/g) — a light that happens to make heat, not a stove (65 minutes per cup of coffee at a generous 30% capture, Exercise 7.2). And the efficiency question is *always* the second half of any fuel quote: the price list of Lecture 2 says what the fuel holds; the calorimeter says what the apparatus caught; a quoted efficiency without its measurement is a guess wearing a number's clothes.

## Lecture 4 — Fire without air: the retort (P7's first full face)

Take the oxidizer away from the heat and fire's other face appears: **pyrolysis** — heating a substance until its molecules come apart *without letting it burn*. This is P7 (thermal decomposition) at its largest and oldest: the charcoal retort, the technology that turns wood into a fuel that is twice as hot per gram, stores without rotting, and burns nearly smokeless. The corpus's practitioners built it at every scale, and the chapter follows them as its own track: Cody's two-barrel retort with the yield weighed on a scale and the make-or-buy answered by quality (N85), the earth-kiln mound with the counter-burn draft control and the entry-by-entry seal cues, and the +200 °C end-use datum for the product (N88), the pit/mound/permanent-kiln method comparison (N89), the sequential-firing reusable mound and its honest second-run yield drop (N91), the hotter-burn demonstration (N92), the low-conductivity failure diagnosed and the doused-vs-retort verdict with its reignition hazard (N93), the brick-kiln firing with the red-hot firing cue and the stacking-yield causality (N95), and the water-gas working-sign with the electric-kiln steam activation — electricity as process-energy, ~2 kWh of it for the activation run (N96).

**The retort's ledger is the chapter's second theme in one run** (Lab 7.2, N85's datums): 10 kg of air-dried wood holds ~160 MJ; the pyrolysis drives off half the mass (the 50% loss, measured on a scale, [single-source corpus]); the 5 kg of charcoal product holds ~150 MJ. The gap — 10 MJ, 6.3% — went up the stack as pyrolysis vapor and off-gas, *some of it burned visibly at the flare* (N91's CO/H₂ theory, the same water-gas chemistry as Lecture 2's Hess loop), plus the sensible heat of the charge and the kiln walls. And the punchline is the **concentration, not creation**: 16 → 30 MJ/kg is 1.875× per kilogram — pyrolysis does not make energy, it *packs* nearly the same joules into half the mass, by driving off the mass that wasn't fuel. The honesty note rides along, quoted from the survey record itself: N85's quality was *asserted, not measured* — the yield is scale-read, no assay — a practitioner's honest datum, quoted as one [single-source].

The retort is also this chapter's best **hazard lesson**, because it fires three at once. The **off-gas is flammable** (the flare is a feature of a working run — and a hazard of a leaking one); the **draft controls the burn** (N88's counter-burn discipline is oxygen-supply management, Lecture 1's lesson in earthenware); and the **product reignites** (N93's doused-vs-retort verdict and N96's water-gas sign: water on hot charcoal makes CO + H₂, which flares — the same endothermic reaction that made the off-gas, now running in your ash bucket). The safety table's retort row is written straight from these runs, and the red-hot firing cue (N95) is the practitioner's own thermometer: the stack's color is a temperature reading, read with the eyes, the way the whole Atlas reads instruments off the world.

## Lecture 5 — The ledger extended: when to pay for which fire

The fuel list is a price list, and P10's rule prices it: **the end-use holds the pen.** Energy per kilogram is one axis; the others are storability (a log rots, propane keeps a decade, charcoal keeps forever), safety (a cylinder is a bomb with a schedule if mistreated — Chapter 6's line, applied to a fuel instead of a still; a liquid fuel's flash point says when its vapor starts burning), availability (the premise's question: what does the world around you actually hold), and residue (wood makes ash and creosote; the spirit lamp makes almost nothing). Exercise 7.5 scores the hearth's candidates on all five for three end-uses — the kettle, the spirit lamp, the retort's heat — and the verdicts are all honest trades: the propane cylinder wins portable density but loses the premise's availability test; charcoal wins storability and smokelessness but has to be *made* (Lecture 4's whole retort, 50% of the wood's mass spent); the alcohol lamp wins the bench (clean, cold enough for glassware, extinguishable with a lid) at 29.67 MJ/kg — a third less than the hydrocarbons, because a third of its mass is oxygen it brought along.

**Electricity joins the price list as the oxidizer-free fuel** (N96's kiln datum, ~2 kWh for the activation run): no flame, no CO, no ash — the same joules delivered with none of the reaction's bookkeeping, at the price of needing a grid or a generator. The corpus uses it exactly where flame cannot go (steam activation inside a closed kiln), and the Atlas's P5 line already knows it: electricity is a reagent *and* a heat source, and the fuels chapter books it as the second hearth.

And the honest endings, stated like Chapter 5's and Chapter 6's: **fire cannot deliver what the fuel does not hold** (the ΔH table is a ceiling, and the efficiency only subtracts from it); **fire's waste stream is not optional** (CO₂ always, CO whenever the draft fails, soot whenever the flame goes yellow, ash whatever the mineral content — the flue is part of the apparatus, and indoors without a draft it is part of the *room*); and **the hot end of the fuel world is not this chapter's to climb** — the oxidizer chemistries that burn without flame (Chapter 15's acid ladder, Chapter 17's oxidizers) are forward-flagged with their owning chapters, and the energetic-materials line is the Charter's oldest rule, restated in its home chapter: fire at hearth scale is knowledge and practice; energetic synthesis is excluded in all sessions (Chapter 28 owns the recognition half).

## Exercises
