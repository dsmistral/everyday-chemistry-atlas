# Vol I · Chapter 5 — Separation Train I: Mechanical and Evaporative

Chapter 4 taught you what a solution *is* — one phase, its solute mixed down to the molecular scale, counted by molarity, bounded by solubility. This chapter is about getting things *out of mixtures*: the sand from the salt, the mud from the water, the crystals from the mother liquor, the product from its impurity. Almost every bench task in the rest of the Atlas ends with a separation, and the premise's discipline applies to them exactly as it applied to the arithmetic: **a separation is a machine built on a physical-property difference — no difference, no separation — and the Atlas's job is to teach you to pick the difference deliberately, price the rung honestly, and bookkeep the loss.** The pattern that owns this chapter is the P2 separation train, and its rungs are older than chemistry: gravity, a hole, the sun, and patience. The corpus supplies the practitioners' own faces — the wash/settle/decant loop and the design-your-solubility filtration (N58/N59), the vacuum assist (N59, N97), the levigation of a potter washing clay in a creek trench (N94), the crystal forms of the drying ladder (N86), and the good-enough separation accepted for its end-use (N62).

The chapter's honest contracts, stated up front: the settling physics is computed with Stokes' law **inside its stated laminar domain** (the domain boundary itself machine-solved — the model says where it stops working, as the Ksp ideal model did in Chapter 4); the crystallization arithmetic runs on Chapter 4's own solubility table, so every number here is pinned to a reviewed chapter; and the loss ledger is the P12 discipline turned into the train's price list — every rung keeps a fraction, the fractions multiply, and you know the bill before you pour.

## Lecture 0 — The three jars (before any notation)

Fill three jars the same way and stir the same spoon of dirt into each, then stand them on the windowsill overnight and draw what you see, twice — now, and in the morning. No numbers yet; the drawing is the lecture.

**Jar one: muddy pond water.** At evening it is brown and opaque. By morning it has *sorted itself*: a layer of grit on the bottom, clearer water above. Nothing was done to it. Gravity is a separation engine that runs for free, all night, in silence — you did not even have to be awake.

**Jar two: water and sand.** Same story, faster — the sand drops out in seconds. Whatever the difference is between jar one and jar two, it is a *degree*, not a kind: the sand grains are the mud's particles, only bigger.

**Jar three: salt water.** Stir as much salt as you like — it vanishes (Chapter 4), and the jar *never* sorts itself. No grit appears, no layers form, not tonight and not next year. The drawing's third panel is the chapter's deepest datum: **jar three cannot be separated by any amount of waiting, because there is nothing left to settle — the salt is dissolved, molecular-scale, part of the single phase.** A separation needs a difference; a solution has hidden the difference between "salt" and "water" so completely that gravity, the strongest and cheapest worker you have, cannot find it.

One more kitchen object before the notation: the coffee maker your civilization left behind. Note what it does, in order: it *steeps* (dissolution, Chapter 4), it *filters* (the paper holds the grounds; the liquid passes — the grounds are jar two, the coffee is jar three), and if you leave the pot standing, fine sediment *settles* in the last cup (jar one). The entire separation train of this chapter is already sitting on the counter, and the spoon you stirred with is the only piece of equipment you are guaranteed to own.

## Lecture 1 — The train: five rungs, one difference

The separation train (P2) is a decision ladder, and the discipline is to **climb it in order of cost, and to pick the rung that exploits the largest physical-property difference** — the biggest gap between what you want and what you do not want is always the cheapest separation available. The rungs, with the property each one rides on:

1. **Settle** (density + particle size — free, slow, needs nothing but a vessel and patience). Jars one and two.
2. **Decant** (pour the clear off the settled — free; the finishing move of the settle).
3. **Filter** (particle size — the medium's holes against the particles' sizes; costs a paper and a little time).
4. **Evaporate** (volatility — the water leaves, the solute stays; costs fuel and watching).
5. **Crystallize** (solubility vs. temperature or concentration — Chapter 4's curves weaponized; costs fuel or patience, pays in purity).

Two more rungs exist and are named now, promised later: **distill** (volatility of both components — Chapter 6 builds the still; Chapter 4 already met the azeotrope ceiling that bounds it) and **chromatography** (a thousand tiny rungs of one rung's principle — the corpus upgraded it from demonstration (N33, *Chemistry Magic*) to home practice (N64's TLC plates); Chapter 13 runs it at the bench).

The train sorts *all of matter* by what it responds to. Heterogeneous mixtures (jars one and two — you can point to the parts) respond to the mechanical rungs: settle, decant, filter. Homogeneous mixtures (jar three — the solution) ignore the mechanical rungs entirely and force the evaporative ones: evaporate, crystallize, distill. **The first bench question is therefore always the same: is my mixture jar one, jar two, or jar three?** — and the notebook habit this chapter builds is the one-line pre-separation ledger: *what do I want (A), what is it mixed with (B), and what is the biggest physical difference between A and B?* Answer that and the train picks itself.

The practitioners' faces: the corpus's copper-compounds chain purified by **repeated wash/settle/decant** — the train's rungs 1–2 run as a *loop*, each cycle another dilution of the impurity (N58); and the oldest recorded face of all, a potter levigating creek clay — the slip drained, "the rocks and sand are left behind in the trench" — which is this exact lecture performed ten thousand years ago, with the creek as the wash water and the trench as the filter (N94).

## Lecture 2 — Gravity's rungs: settling and decanting

Why did jar one clear overnight while sand drops in seconds? Because a particle sinking in water is slowed by viscosity, and the slowing is ferociously size-dependent: **Stokes' law says a particle's settling speed goes as the *square* of its diameter** — v = Δρ·g·d²/(18η) — so halving a grain's size quarters its speed, and shrinking it tenfold slows it a hundredfold. Lab 5.1 turns the law into the bench's own settling table, and the table is worth reading as the three-jar lecture in numbers:

- **Sand (0.1–0.5 mm):** seconds. Settle, decant, done — the rung's cost is the time to walk to the shelf.
- **Fine silt (0.05 mm):** about 45 seconds per 10 cm of column. Still free.
- **Fine silt (10 µm):** about 19 minutes per 10 cm. The mug of pond water clears while you make the coffee.
- **Clay (2 µm):** about **7.7 hours** per 10 cm — the overnight jar, computed. Clay is why muddy water *stays* muddy: not "dirty water," but water holding particles too small for gravity to collect on any human timescale.

And the honest label, stated where it belongs: Stokes' law is a *laminar-flow* law, and Lab 5.1 machine-solves its own domain boundary — the law is trustworthy for particles below roughly **61 µm**; above that the printed speeds overpredict (the sand settles in seconds regardless — the verdict stands, the number does not). This is the same discipline as Chapter 4's Ksp caveat: the model is stated, bounded, and honest about its seam.

**Decanting** is the settle's finishing move: pour the clear layer off the sediment, slowly, ideally down a stirring rod laid against the pour lip (the rod breaks the water's grip and the pour stays quiet). The decant's own honest trade is written into the gesture: **you always leave a little liquid behind** — pour too eagerly and the sediment comes with you; pour too greedily and clarity costs yield. The notebook records which you chose and why (P12's first appearance in this chapter).

When the particles are too fine to filter (gummy, colloidal — Lecture 3's boundary case) the settle/decant pair becomes a *loop*: wash the solid with clean water, let it settle, decant, repeat. Each cycle is a fresh dilution of whatever the trapped liquid held — and Lab 5.1's ledger shows the loop's astonishing leverage: with 5 g of trapped liquor, three washes remove **99.92%** of the trapped impurity, at a product cost of *micrograms*, because the product's insolubility was chosen before the first pour (N59's design — the batch's copper carbonate would need its own backyard swimming pool of water to dissolve; N58's copper chain ran exactly this wash/settle/decant loop as its purification).

## Lecture 3 — The filter: a price list of holes

A filter is nothing but a price list of holes, and the purchase decision is arithmetic: **the medium's pores against the particles' sizes.** The household's list, read against Lab 5.1's particle table: the colander (millimetre holes — pasta, not silt), the sieve (N95's wicker grading of charcoal is this rung at craft scale), the paper towel and coffee filter (pores of order tens of micrometres [convergent, range flagged] — catches sand, passes silt, never catches clay), cotton and cloth (a coarser and a variable paper), and the bought end — the fine glass frit and the membrane filter, which the Atlas does not pretend to improvise. What stays on the medium is the **residue** (or **cake**); what passes is the **filtrate** — and the filtrate of a clean filtration is jar three: a solution, ready for Chapter 4's arithmetic.

Three bench disciplines make the filter honest:

1. **Wet the paper first.** A dry paper absorbs, channels, and lets solids creep under its edge; a wet paper seals to the funnel and its pores are already full of the only liquid allowed through.
2. **Wash the cake — briefly, deliberately.** The cake comes off the filter wet with mother liquor, and everything the liquor held rides into your product when it dries. A small portion of clean water poured over the cake displaces it — and the wash is *not free* in general: every wash liquid touches the product and can dissolve it, so the wash's cost is the product's own solubility (Lab 5.1 prices N59's wash at ten micrograms — free *by design*, because the insolubility was the point of choosing that product; the same wash on a soluble product would be a different bill entirely).
3. **Design before you filter.** The corpus's masterclass (N58): before filtering a solution that holds your product, *consult the solubility table* — if the product's solubility is high it rides through the paper with the filtrate (which is what you want when the *impurity* is the solid), and if the product is the solid you want its solubility low and the impurity's high. The copper chain's expected yields were "fully dissolved by design, filterable without product loss" — the arithmetic consulted *before* the pour, not after the loss.

**Vacuum is the filter's accelerator:** a Büchner funnel over a suction flask, pulled by a hand pump (N59's own gear — with its honest zero recorded: the filtration apparatus was *purchased*, not improvised, and the Atlas records that make-or-buy verdict rather than pretend otherwise; N97's vacuum work is the same lever at amateur scale). The physics is one sentence: the pump lowers the pressure below the paper, and the atmosphere pushes the liquid through — gravity demoted to a spectator. The cautions that ride with it: heavy-walled glass only (an ordinary jar under vacuum is an implosion waiting for a calendar), the flask seated stably, and the pump released *before* the pump handle meets the cake — a cracked cake channels air and the wash's displacement ends.

**The mechanical-loss honesty case.** The corpus's own filtration (N59) recorded the filter paper tearing during transfer and crystals coming off "gummy" — the loss *named* in the source, not smoothed; and its companion episode (N58) turned the same loss class into the corpus's flagship bookkeeping: 47 g found against 49.4 g theoretical, "nearly all of the shortage was mechanical loss," 95% — the loss ledger with the cause written on the line. The train does not punish loss; it punishes *unbookkept* loss.

## Lecture 4 — The evaporative rungs: to dryness, or to crystals

Jar three does not respond to gravity or to holes — the only differences left to exploit are the ones the *solution* still has: its water is volatile, its solute is not, and its solubility depends on temperature. Two rungs live on exactly those differences, and they have **different destinations**:

**Evaporate to dryness** takes all the water: simple, total, needs only heat and patience — and traps *everything* the water held. The crust that remains is the solute and the impurity and the dust and whatever the last drops scoured off the dish — **purity by dryness is zero.** Dryness is for when the residue is one substance (boil down pure brine, get pure salt) or when you want the *water* gone regardless of what stays.

**Evaporate to saturation, then crystallize** is the purification rung: stop short of dryness, and let Chapter 4's curves do the sorting. The solute's own crystals form *from a solution that still holds the impurity*, and the growing crystal's ordered lattice is fussy about what it lets in — so the impurity stays behind in the **mother liquor**, the last liquid, and the harvest is crystals plus a dirty remainder. Chapter 4 computed the recrystallization yields (KNO₃ gives up 87.2% of itself on cooling 100 °C → 20 °C; NaCl refuses the method at 8.7%); this chapter computes the *other half* of the method — **how much water must leave before the first crystal appears**, the saturation threshold:

- A jar of saturated brine, boiled: the first salt crusts after only **8.2%** of the water leaves (Lab 5.2) — the flat curve works *for* you at the boil.
- The same salt in the sea: seawater must lose **90.25%** of its water before its first crystal (Lab 5.2) — the salt garden's brutal economics, and why evaporation ponds are measured in hectares (Ex 5.3 prices a kilogram of salt at 28.6 kg of seawater).
- A saturated KNO₃ jar at the boil: **87.2%** of the water must leave — but *cooling* the same jar recovers 87.2% of the *salt* with no fuel at all (Chapter 4). The steep curve says: cool first, boil second (P10's fuel line applied to the train).
- The candymaker's 2:1 sugar syrup at the boil: the first crystals appear after **59.2%** of the water leaves (Lab 5.2) — every stage of candy-making is the first-crystal threshold crossed hot, then the *rate* of crossing timed against the thermometer, which is why candy is the most disciplined separation art in the kitchen.

The crystals themselves are objects with rules, all Chapter-2/Chapter-4 territory repriced here: **seed crystals** start the harvest (a dust speck or a poured-in pinch); **slow growth from a cool, quiet, barely-supersaturated liquor grows big, clean crystals** — fast growth from a boiling pan traps liquor in the lattice and the crystal's purity decays with its haste; **the hydrate trap rides the harvest** — the crystal you collect carries its waters of crystallization (Epsom salt's seven; the corpus's whole dehydration ladder, N86, exists because the *store* crystal is the heptahydrate and the *drying-agent* crystal is the monohydrate — same salt, different harvest); and **plaster's set is this lecture's kinetic face**: calcium sulfate hemihydrate slung on a TLC plate grabs its half-water back as dihydrate needles — recrystallization itself, run on a two-minute working clock that the corpus's plate-maker timed slide by slide (N64).

The rung's honest boundary: some solids refuse to crystallize from water at all (glasses, syrups that candy-check into amorphous masses), and some impurities co-crystallize (isomorphous salts that fit the lattice — the water-softener's Epsom contaminating rock salt is the classic household case). The train's answer to both is the same as always: a bigger difference or a different rung.

## Lecture 5 — The train as a ledger

The train's last lesson is an accounting one, and it is the chapter's summit because it is what separates a kitchen from a lab: **every rung keeps a fraction, and the fractions multiply.** A transfer spills 0.5%; the cake holds back 2%; the crystallization leaves 12.8% of the product in the mother liquor; the drying dish loses another 0.5% — and Lab 5.1's cumulative ledger prices the whole chain: 0.995 × 0.98 × 0.872 × 0.995 = **84.6 g of a 100 g theoretical batch arrives in the jar**, known *in advance*, written *before* the first pour (P12). The practitioner's version is N58's 95%-of-theoretical with the loss attributed line by line — the corpus's flagship datum — and the Atlas's rule is its direct descendant: **the yield is booked at every rung, or it was never yours to report.**

**Purity is decided by the next step, not by pride (P10).** The corpus's cleanest statement is the bromine isolation (N62): the reaction worked, the separation was *not* pushed — ~100 mL of relatively dilute bromine water, "not nearly as much as expected" — and it was accepted without apology because the intended end-use (testing organics for unsaturation) simply did not need more. That is not laziness; it is the correct reading of a separation's purpose. The opposite face is Chapter 4's azeotrope wall (N46, N83): when the end-use *does* demand the ceiling, the mechanical and evaporative rungs hand off to Chapter 6's still, honestly, at the boundary where they end.

**And the train ends where the differences run out.** What mechanical and evaporative separation *cannot* do: split two solutes whose curves are too similar (two salts both riding the water), split two dissolved liquids (that is Chapter 6's volatility business), or split a solute from its own solvent when neither will leave (that is Chapter 6's steam and azeotrope territory, and beyond it, chemistry itself — Chapter 9's reactions as separations: *change the substance so the difference exists*). Naming what a tool cannot do is the Atlas's honest-scope discipline, and the handoffs are flagged, not blurred: Chapter 6 takes volatility; Chapter 9 takes reactivity; Chapter 13 takes the thousand tiny rungs of chromatography.
