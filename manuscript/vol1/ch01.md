# The Lab in a Ruined World

*Volume I — Foundations of the Home Lab, Chapter 1*

**In this chapter:** the premise, made concrete; the Safety Charter (binding, restated in full); kitchen glassware and what survives; heat sources as a temperature ladder; the notebook as the lab's primary instrument. Two computational labs are executed in-text with their real outputs. Graded exercises close the chapter, including one pattern-unknown diagnostic (◆) and open-ended explorations (◐).

**Verification labels used throughout this book** (binding convention, ranked by strongest evidence available):

- **[computed]** — the arithmetic was executed and shown; you can re-derive it.
- **[convergent]** — consistent across two or more independent canon sources, named.
- **[single-source]** — one corpus/canon source demonstrated it; cited, flagged for bench confirmation.
- **[speculative]** — a reasoned guess, labeled as such. Never presented as verified.

No unverified claim in this book will ever read as verified.

## Lecture 0 — The Inventory (pencil and paper, before any notation)

Sit down with a pencil. No chemistry notation yet — none is needed, and that is the point of Lecture 0.

The premise, stated once: supply chains are gone. No Sigma catalog, no Amazon, no hardware-store restock. You still want clean water, soap, medicine, dyes, batteries, glazes, and the ability to find out *what something is*. The question that decides whether any of that is possible is not "what reactions do I know?" It is: **what do I actually have?**

Here is the claim of this lecture, and it is the foundation the whole Atlas stands on: *a modern home is already a chemical warehouse.* The industrial supply chain did not bypass your house — it passed through it, leaving finished goods on every shelf. The chemist's first act is therefore not a synthesis. It is an inventory.

### The pencil-and-paper exercise

Take one sheet of paper. Draw three columns: **Kitchen**, **Utility/laundry/garage**, **Medicine cabinet**. Now, from memory, list twenty products and, for each, what its *active ingredient* is. Do not look anything up yet — the exercise is to discover how much you already know, and where the holes are.

Worked example, one line, fully: **vinegar**. What is in it? Water and acetic acid, roughly 5 g per 100 mL in the standard grocery bottle [convergent: label data and any general-chemistry text]. That single line already encodes three facts you will use for the rest of the book: there is a *compound* (acetic acid), it is *dissolved* in water at a *known concentration*, and both facts came off a **label**. Concentration as a number will get its formal treatment in Chapter 4 (Water and Solutions). You do not need the notation to act on the fact.

When I ran this exercise cold, my sheet looked like this (yours will differ — that is data, not error):

| Product | Active ingredient (from memory) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Vinegar | acetic acid, ~5% | acid, food grade |
| Baking soda | sodium bicarbonate | weak base, mild abrasive |
| Table salt | sodium chloride | may contain iodide + anti-caking |
| Bleach | sodium hypochlorite, ~5–8% | oxidizer, degrades with age |
| Hydrogen peroxide | H₂O₂, 3% | oxidizer, decomposes in light |

| Wallplaster/drywall | calcium sulfate (gypsum) | plaster of Paris on heating |

Count the *chemical classes* sitting in an ordinary kitchen: acids (acetic, hydrochloric, phosphoric, carbonic), bases (bicarbonate, hydroxide, borate, carbonate), oxidizers (hypochlorite, peroxide), reductants (alcohol, iron, graphite), salts (chloride, sulfate, tetraborate, thiosulfate), metals (tin, iron), and solvents (water, alcohol). That is a first-year laboratory's worth of starting material.

**How you could have found this.** Nobody hands you this inventory. You find it the way the whole field found it: you read labels, and when a label says "contains sodium hypochlorite 5.25%", you ask what sodium hypochlorate — *hypochlorite* — is good for, and the answer (oxidizer, bleach, chlorination chemistry) unlocks Ch 15–16 of this Atlas before you have weighed a single gram. Label-reading is not clerical work; it is the acquisition channel of the entire ruined-world lab. This move is important enough to be the book's **first pattern, P1 — Read the label (OTC sourcing)** — and it has its own chapter (Ch 2).

One caution before the next lecture, because it decides everything after it: the inventory above contains products that can blind you (drain cleaner), poison you (bleach + acid mixtures), and start fires (steel wool near a battery). The premise's romance stops exactly here. In a ruined world there is no poison-control hotline, no emergency room, no eye bank. **Safety is not the first chapter of this book by editorial convention; it is the load-bearing wall.** Lecture 1 is the charter.

## Lecture 1 — The Safety Charter

**The idea of this lecture, alone:** an amateur lab runs on an *error budget*, and for eyes, lungs, and skin that budget is **zero**. Every rule below is a consequence of that one idea.

### Encounter

Watch a working home chemist — the corpus this book was built from is full of them — and you notice the same pre-flight ritual before anything gets heated: goggles on, gloves checked, and a decision about *where the reaction will sit* relative to open air. One of the corpus's flagship safety performances (The Home Scientist, "Lab Safety", corpus N56) opens with a distinction most hobbyists never learn: **splash hazards need splash goggles; impact hazards need impact-rated goggles** — the vinegars and bleach solutions of the household world fling droplets that a vented impact-rated shop goggle will happily let through, while grinding and any pressurized work does the reverse. The two ratings are not interchangeable. That is the difference between "wearing goggles" and *engineering* PPE — the craft is in the classification.

### The struggle, honestly

Everyone who has worked at a kitchen counter knows the temptation: it is "just" a five-minute filtration of table salt solution; surely goggles can sit this one out? The rule-out is arithmetic, and it is worth doing once by hand:

- Probability of a splash in any single quick task: small — call it 1 in 1,000 per task [speculative as a number; the point is structural].
- Tasks across a working year of ruined-world chemistry: hundreds.
- Cost of the unlucky splash in a hypochlorite or drain-cleaner session: permanent eye damage, with no ophthalmologist in the premise.

A small probability multiplied by an unrecoverable cost, repeated daily, is how amateurs lose eyes. You do not get to argue with the product. This is why the charter below is phrased as rules and not suggestions — it is the one place in the book where "because I said so" is the *derived* answer.

### The Charter (binding; restated in full)

This book's Safety Charter, in force for every chapter and every reader:

1. **Hazard assessment before every experiment, written in the notebook before the first pour.** Every experiment gets a table: chemicals (with concentration and amount), physical hazards (heat, glass, pressure), environmental hazards, minimum PPE, failure modes (what happens if it boils over, cracks, or gets spilled), quench procedure, disposal route, and an explicit "do not scale up" note.
2. **Restricted experiments (absolute exclusions, from the project's binding energetics line):**
   - No step-by-step synthesis of explosive or energetic materials. Hazard *recognition* knowledge (what dangerous things look like, how they arise accidentally, how to avoid creating them) is in scope and gets its own chapter (Vol III Ch 28) — the procedures are not.
   - No high-pressure or sealed-vessel procedures. Ever.
3. **Air management:** any step that fumes, smells, or generates a gas happens **outdoors**, or under improvised ventilation (a firm cross-breeze with the work far upwind of your face counts as the bottom rung; a proper fume hood is Chapter 6's build project territory, not a prerequisite).
4. **PPE minimums per task class**, from the splash/impact classification:
   - Any liquid with pH far from neutral or any oxidizer in open handling: **splash goggles**.
   - Grinding, cutting, hammering, any pressurized work: **impact-rated goggles**.
   - Caustics (lye, drain cleaner): splash goggles **plus** nitrile gloves, and a pre-set quench basin of clear water within arm's reach *before* opening the bottle.
5. **The access rule:** everything becomes, in effect, a poison or a fire. Children, pets, and untrained adults do not enter the work area; chemicals never live in food containers — the fatal error class of home poisoning is a caustic in a soda bottle. (The corpus's model home lab — The Home Scientist's own lab tour, N55 — runs a strict child-access rule and never relaxes it, even in "just five minutes" conditions.)
6. **Known-dangerous combinations, memorized now, before any other chemistry:** bleach + acid → chlorine gas; bleach + ammonia → chloramines; bleach + drain cleaner (H₂SO₄ type) → heat, spatter, and chlorine; acid + metal → hydrogen (explosive range!); oxidizer + organic solvent → fire. The full recognition treatment is Vol III Ch 28; these five are the ones that kill in kitchens.
7. **Incident response is part of the experiment:** water for eyes (15 minutes of flushing, hold the eyelid open), water for skin caustics (rinse before anything else), sand or a lid (never water) for an organic-solvent fire, and a written "if this goes wrong" line in the notebook *before* the run.
8. **The scale ceiling:** every procedure in this Atlas is sized for the stated amounts. Scaling up multiplies heat released, gas evolved, and consequence — and it voids the hazard assessment you wrote. Do not scale up.

The Charter is enforced not by the author but by the review process: every chapter of this book passes an adversarial review in which a designated skeptic attacks exactly these lines, and every objection must be resolved by machine-checked calculation or convergent citation, not rhetoric [the review protocol itself is recorded in the project's Progress Report].

### The PPE decision table (the classification, made routine)

| Task class | Examples | Eyes | Hands | Location |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dry handling, no dust | weighing salt, folding filter paper | none mandated | none mandated | any surface, clean after |
| Dissolving, mild (near-neutral, dilute) | salt in water, sugar in water | splash goggles | none | any |
| Acid/base solutions, any concentration | vinegar work, muriatic dilution | splash goggles | nitrile | cross-ventilated |
| Caustics (NaOH, drain cleaner) | lye solution prep | splash goggles | nitrile | outdoors or strong cross-breeze, quench basin set |
| Oxidizers open (bleach, peroxide) | dilution, testing | splash goggles | nitrile | ventilated, never near acids |
| Fuming/gas-generating steps | acid + carbonate, any HCl heating | splash goggles | nitrile | **outdoors** |
| Grinding/impact | mortar work, glass cutting, wire work | impact-rated goggles | as task demands | clear area |
| Flammable solvents open | alcohol handling | splash goggles | nitrile | no flames in the room, lid discipline |

*The table is a floor, not a ceiling — and it is [convergent]: it matches the corpus's safety episode (N56) and the standard canon's PPE chapters (Thompson's *Illustrated Guide to Home Chemistry Experiments*, safety chapter) with no contradictions found.*

## Lecture 2 — Glass and What Survives

**The idea of this lecture, alone:** choosing apparatus is *matching a material's properties to the job* — and the property that kills kitchen glassware is **thermal shock**, the stress from a sudden temperature difference.

### Encounter, at the sink

Every kitchen has three glasses that matter: the **drinking glass** (soda-lime silica — cheap, everywhere), the **baking dish** (some are borosilicate; most modern ones are tempered soda-lime), and the **preserving jar** (soda-lime, thick-walled, often misused as a reaction vessel). The corpus's improvised-apparatus tradition — from the 1924 *Boy Chemist*'s jam-jar condensers to the modern performers' kitchenware substitution (The Home Scientist's lab tour, N55: proper glass where it counts, kitchenware where it doesn't) — works *because* its practitioners know which is which, not because "glass is glass."

### The one property to internalize

Thermal shock failure happens when one face of the glass is hot and the other is cold; the differential strain exceeds the material's strength. Rough, manufacturer-data planning bands [convergent across glassmaker references; treat as bands, not precision values]:

- **Borosilicate:** tolerates sudden ΔT on the order of 150 °C and more — boiling water into a room-temperature borosilicate flask is routine.
- **Tempered soda-lime:** strong against mechanical impact *and* modest thermal gradients, but it fails dramatically and completely (the whole pane at once) when its limit is exceeded; never assume it for direct flame.
- **Ordinary soda-lime (drinking glasses, jars):** the danger zone starts at sudden ΔT of roughly 40–50 °C. Boiling water into a cold jar is a coin flip; a jar on a direct flame is a scheduled failure.

From these bands the ruined-world glassware hierarchy falls out by *derivation*, not memorization:

1. **Direct flame / hot-plate contact:** borosilicate only (a real lab flask is one of the few items worth buying *before* the ruin — the make-or-buy score of Ch 2 applies).
2. **Hot-water and steam work (baths, warming, slow crystallization):** any clean glass whose ΔT stays under ~40 °C — jars are fine here, which is why the water bath is the workhorse of Chapter 5.
3. **Cold or room-temperature work (dissolving, filtering, storing):** everything, including bottles — but see the access rule (never food containers, Lecture 1).

### The water bath: the universal heat interface

If Lecture 2 leaves you with one habit, make it this: **a hot water bath, not a flame, is the default heating instrument of this book.** A pot of water on a stove *is* a thermostat capped at 100 °C (in plain air — altitude and solutes move the cap, a fact we compute in Chapter 7). It cannot overshoot. It delivers heat through the largest possible ΔT that ordinary glass survives. It is the "double-boiler as water bath" improvisation — **P11, Apparatus improvisation** — in its purest form: zero special equipment, one safety property purchased for free.

P11 carries a stated price, and the corpus quantifies it beautifully: when The Home Scientist calibrated a disposable pipette gravimetrically (N63), the *nominal* instrument and the *actual* instrument disagreed — 100 drops weighed 3.62 g, giving 0.0363 mL per drop and 27.5 drops per mL, not the printed value. **Improvised apparatus always comes with an accuracy penalty; the professional move is not to avoid the improvisation but to *measure the penalty and write it down.*** We verify that arithmetic in Lab 1.1 below — it is the bookkeeping you will run on every improvised instrument you ever build.

### The make-or-buy line for glassware

The corpus's canon split is real and worth naming here: Thompson's *Illustrated Guide* is a **BUY** book (proper glassware, purchased, with a starter-kit list — corpus N39 records his location-and-gear episode), while the ruined-world premise is **MAKE** by necessity. This book's resolution is not ideology but route economics (**P10**, applied to apparatus): score each item by (a) availability after the ruin, (b) hazard of improvising it, (c) accuracy penalty, (d) what it lets you do that nothing else does. On that score: *buy-or-preserve* borosilicate flasks (uniquely load-bearing, hard to improvise), *improvise* baths, stands, funnels, and storage (cheap, low-accuracy-demand), and *never* improvise pressure vessels (the Charter's sealed-vessel exclusion makes this a rule, not a preference). The full scoring method gets its worked examples in Ch 2; here the takeaway is the *shape* of the decision.

## Lecture 3 — Heat: the Temperature Ladder

**The idea of this lecture, alone:** every heat source is defined by *two* numbers, not one — its **peak temperature** and its **controllability** — and a heat source's failure mode is a function of the pair.

A ruined-world chemist inherits the temperature ladder below. The bands are planning data [convergent: standard references agree on these ranges; treat every band as approximate]:

| Rung | Source | Band (approx.) | Control | Dominant failure mode |
|------|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | ambient / warm-water bath | 20–60 °C | excellent | none (too gentle to fail) |
| 1 | boiling-water bath | ~100 °C | excellent (self-capped) | boil-dry → the pot itself becomes Rung 3 |
| 2 | oven (household) | 40–250 °C | good (setpoint, slow) | thermal shock of contents; runaway near max |
| 3 | stovetop, dry vessel | 150–400 °C surface | fair (coarse steps) | local scorching; soda-lime glass failure |
| 4 | alcohol lamp / candle flame | 800–1000 °C at the working zone | poor (flame "steering" only) | soot, tipping, glass shock from flame contact |
| 5 | propane/butane torch | up to ~1900–2000 °C | poor-to-fair | instant glass failure; setting fires |
| 6 | electric arc (homemade) | ~3000 °C | none (hand-built) | UV, molten metal, catastrophic misfire — **out of Ch 1 scope; Ch 19 territory under its own charter section** |

**How you could have found this.** You do not need a thermocouple to place a source on the ladder — you need one observable per rung, and they are all around you: a warm bath is touchable; a boiling bath is not and bubbles at a steady murmur (the cap, and it *holds*, which is the point of Rung 1); an oven's dial is a printed thermometer; a flame's working zone is where copper wire glows; and a torch is where everything you own either melts or burns. Calibration by cheap orthogonal evidence — the same instrumentless-verification instinct (**P9**) that Chapter 13 will systematize — starts here, at the stove.

Two corpus datums pin the ladder's amateur ceiling. A ceramic hotplate-stirrer pushed to 540 °C appears in a sulfuric-acid purification session (corpus N82) — the top of *controlled* amateur heat [single-source]. And the corpus's arc-furnace builders (chromium, cobalt episodes; N79, N98) reach Rung 6 for slag-breaking melts at ~3000 °C — deliberately, behind face shields, outdoors, and with the Charter's exclusions engaged — which is exactly why Rung 6 sits at the *far end* of this book's sequence, not its start.

**The ladder as a safety instrument.** Note the pattern in the failure-mode column: *control collapses faster than temperature rises.* The practical rule that falls out: **match the rung to the job's highest safe temperature, then take the lowest rung that clears it.** Water bath when water-bath will do (Ch 5's crystallizations), dry stovetop for calcination-class work (Ch 7–9), flame only for glass-bending and ignition duties, torch never near a reaction vessel, arc furnaces: Chapter 19, read the Charter first. And the boil-dry failure of Rung 1 — the humble water bath's one way to become Rung 3 — is why the notebook entry (Lecture 4) always records *time-on-heat*.

## Lecture 4 — The Notebook: Your Instrument of Record

**The idea of this lecture, alone:** in a lab with no instruments, the notebook is the instrument — **unrecorded work is unverifiable work**, and unverifiable work is a rumor, not a result.

### Encounter: the flagship self-correction

The corpus's single best notebook performance (The Home Scientist synthesizing copper compounds, N58) goes like this: weigh out exactly 100.35 g of copper sulfate pentahydrate; predict the theoretical product mass from stoichiometry; run the synthesis; weigh what you got — 47 g against a predicted 49.4 g; and then, instead of rounding up to "success," write the yield as 95% and *attribute the missing 5% to named mechanical losses*. That is the whole discipline in one entry: a prediction made **before** the run, a measurement after, an honest difference, and a loss ledger instead of an excuse.

We re-derived that arithmetic by machine in Lab 1.1 below — and the re-derivation is itself a notebook lesson: the source's stated theoretical mass (49.4 g) and the cleanly recomputed value for copper carbonate from the same starting salt (49.66 g) differ by half a percent, and finding that gap is *exactly what a notebook is for*. (Resolution, worked in the lab: the percent-yield verdict — "about 95%" — is robust to the gap, which is why the source's conclusion stands even after its rounding is exposed.)

### The honest zero

The notebook's second pillar sounds strange until you have watched the corpus: **negative results are results.** The performers this book is built from state yields they did not measure ("not really measuring it" — the cesium isolation, N105), attempts that did not fire (the lithium electrolysis considered and *rejected* on combustion risk, N100), and episodes whose method could not even be verified (the age-gated aluminum-powder entry recorded as honest zeros across every pattern, N102). None of these were hidden; they were logged. In a ruined world this is not humility but survival: the inventory of *what does not work* is half of your technology base, and the entry that says "route rejected, here is why" is the one that keeps next winter's chemist from re-burning your mistake.

### The entry format (this book's binding template)

Every run gets all six lines — no exceptions, however small the run:

1. **Date and purpose** — one sentence: what question this run answers.
2. **Inputs, with numbers** — masses to 0.01 g where a balance exists (Ch 2 improvises one if not), volumes, concentrations, source-of-each-reagent (which shelf, which bottle — inventory links matter in a world without restock).
3. **Predicted outcome** — written *before* the first pour. (Mass you expect, gas you expect, color you expect. This is the step that turns accidents into data.)
4. **Procedure + observations** — what you actually did and saw, including what you *changed* from the plan. Times and temperatures against the Lecture 3 ladder. Colors, smells (waft, never sniff — Charter), textures.
5. **Verdict with the loss ledger** — actual vs. theoretical; every missing percent attributed to a *named* loss (transfer, filtration retention, spill, drying) or left as an honest gap.
6. **Disposal + next question** — where the waste went (the Charter's last line) and what this run made you want to know next.

The template is [convergent]: it is the union of the canon's notebook chapters (Thompson's *Illustrated Guide* devotes a full early chapter to lab notebooks) and the corpus's demonstrated practice (the N58 loss ledger; the gravimetric calibration entry of N63), and neither source contradicts the other on a single line.

This entry format **is** the pattern the book calls **P12 — Yield bookkeeping**, promoted to a first-class move because the corpus showed it firing across dozens of independent performers. In a ruined world, the notebook is the only durable storage medium your lab has. Guard it accordingly — and its first entry, today, is the Lecture 0 inventory sheet.

## Computational Lab 1.1 — The Molar Bookkeeping Engine (P12)

*The labs are this book's demonstrations. They are executed in-session and their real outputs are printed here; "should run" is never enough. A small-case test is a detector, not a proof — the round-trip sweeps below are exhaustive over their stated small domains, and that is the strongest claim they carry.*

**Purpose.** Build the mass ↔ mole ↔ concentration converter once, correctly, and verify it as a *round-trip bijection* — every gram figure in a notebook must survive the trip out to moles and back — then run it on the corpus's two flagship datums (N58's yield, N63's calibration).

**Implementation.** A formula parser (hydrate-dot notation included, e.g. `CuSO4.5H2O`), a molar-mass table for the elements of this chapter's world, and the two-line conversion core: `mol = mass / M`, back = `mol × M`.

**Executed outputs (this run, verbatim):**

```text
Parser cross-check (CuSO4.5H2O = Cu1 S1 O9 H10):        PASS
Molar masses (g/mol):
  CuSO4.5H2O = 249.677      CuCO3 = 123.554      NaHCO3 = 84.006
  NaCl = 58.440             H2O = 18.015          CaCO3 = 100.086
  KAl(SO4)2.12H2O = 378.316

Mass↔mole round-trip bijection, all 7 formulas, 0.5 g steps over 1–3000 g:
  cases: 41,993   passed: 41,993   max abs error: 4.55e-13     PASS (exhaustive over domain)

Case A — the N58 flagship yield (single-source corpus datum, arithmetic computed):
  100.35 g CuSO4·5H2O ÷ 249.677 g/mol = 0.401919 mol Cu
  theoretical CuCO3 = 0.401919 mol × 123.554 g/mol = 49.66 g
  source's own stated theoretical: 49.4 g   (gap 0.26 g = 0.5% — the source's rounding)
  found: 47 g
  percent yield, source's numbers (47 / 49.4):   95.1%
  percent yield, computed theoretical (47/49.66): 94.6%
  VERDICT: the "95%" conclusion is robust to the rounding gap [computed + single-source]

Case B — the N63 gravimetric pipette calibration (single-source, computed):
  100 drops = 3.62 g at density 0.998 g/mL
  volume per drop = 3.62 / 0.998 / 100 = 0.03627 mL
  drops per mL = 1 / 0.03627 = 27.57
  source's stated values: 0.0363 mL/drop, 27.5 drops/mL   — agrees to rounding [computed]
```

**What the lab teaches, beyond the pass marks.** (1) The bijection is what makes a notebook number trustworthy: any mass you write down can be reconverted losslessly, so errors are *detectable* at the next entry instead of compounding. (2) The N58 case shows the correct relationship between a source and its arithmetic: trust the *method*, re-derive the *numbers* — the 0.5% gap does not overturn the yield verdict, but it would have been invisible without the machine check. (3) The N63 case is the accuracy penalty of improvised apparatus (Lecture 2), in decimal form — and notice that the source itself did the calibration *and wrote the penalty into its own notebook*, which is the entire culture of P12 in one entry.

## Computational Lab 1.2 — The Dilution Ladder

**Purpose.** Concentration is the number the whole ruined-world inventory trades in (Lecture 0: "5%", "3%", "~30%"), and the single move that connects all of them is `C1·V1 = C2·V2`. Verify it as a bijection — every (stock, target, volume) triple must survive the dilution and back-computation — then generate the serial-halving ladder, the corpus's signature calibration technique (the salicylate colorimetry episode, N74, walks a 150 → ~9.5 mg/dL ladder of halvings against a control).

**Executed outputs (verbatim):**

```text
Dilution round-trip C1·V1 = C2·V2:
  domain: stocks 0.1–12.0 M (0.1 steps) × targets 0.1 M..C1 (0.1 steps) × final volumes {10, 25, 50, 100, 250, 500, 1000} mL
  cases: 50,820   passed: 50,820   max error < 1e-12     PASS (exhaustive over domain)

Serial-halving ladder from 150 mg/dL (the N74 methodology):
  rung 0: 150.0000    rung 1: 75.0000    rung 2: 37.5000    rung 3: 18.7500
  rung 4:  9.3750    rung 5:  4.6875    rung 6:  2.3438  (mg/dL)
  first rung below 10 mg/dL: rung 4 (9.375) — matches the source's reported
  lowest rung (~9.5) within reporting precision [computed + single-source]
```

**Interpretation.** The ladder rungs are not new chemistry — they are Lecture 0's "5%" turned into an instrument: each halving is one `C1V1=C2V2` step with equal volumes, so *k* halvings divide by exactly 2^k, and the lab verifies that geometric fact rung by rung. Why halving rather than arbitrary factors? Because with no pipettes, equal-volume splits (two identical cups, filled to a marked line) are the one volume operation improvised apparatus performs accurately — the ladder is the P11 accuracy penalty, *designed around*. That interlock — a mathematically exact method chosen because of an apparatus limitation — is a signature of this whole book.

## Exercises

*Graded ●○○ (recall/direct), ●●○ (two ideas combined), ●●● (novel/multi-step). Every exercise has a hint and a worked answer. The ◆ exercise has no hint — your task is to diagnose which tool applies; the answer names the pattern. ◐ exercises are open-ended.*

**Exercise 1 ●○○.** Your Lecture 0 sheet lists "muriatic acid, ~30%". What two facts from the label itself let you write "HCl, ~30% w/w" into your inventory with confidence, and what would make you *not* trust the label?

*Hint.* Lecture 0 put three facts in one vinegar line; the label columns are the same three.
*Worked answer.* The product name ("muriatic acid") names the substance; the active-ingredient line ("hydrochloric acid 31.45%" or similar) gives identity and assay — identity + concentration, read off the label: **P1**. Second way to the same fact: cross-check the use ("for lowering pool pH") against known pool-aisle chemistry — pool pH decreasers are muriatic acid or sodium bisulfate, and the label distinguishes them, so *use* corroborates *identity* (a mini P9, verification by convergent evidence). Do *not* trust the label if the bottle is old (HCl fumes and vent, so old bottles run below label strength — Ch 6 quantifies this) or if there is no active-ingredient line at all (Ch 2's grades problem).

**Exercise 2 ●○○.** You need 250 mL of 0.5 M sodium chloride solution and your stock is 5.0 M. How much stock and how much water? Solve it two ways.

*Hint.* Lab 1.2's one move, both ways.
*Worked answer.* Way 1, `C1V1 = C2V2`: V1 = (0.5 × 250)/5.0 = **25 mL stock, diluted to 250 mL** (add water to the mark — "diluted to", never "plus 225 mL" to a cylinder; volume is not additive with precision, Ch 4 computes the correction). Way 2, the factor view: the dilution factor is 5.0/0.5 = 10×, so take 250/10 = 25 mL and dilute tenfold. Both are **P12** bookkeeping; the factor view is the ladder's logic (one 10× rung vs. ~3.32 halvings — the ladder would need equal-volume splits, so a direct 10× is the improvised-friendly choice here).

**Exercise 3 ●●○.** You are going to warm 500 mL of 5% vinegar in a large open borosilicate bowl on a boiling-water bath, driving off water to concentrate it (Chapter 6's opener). Write the hazard-assessment table the Charter requires (Lecture 1, rule 1) — hazards, PPE, failure modes, quench, disposal, scale note — and state which two Charter rules do the most work here.

*Hint.* The Charter's table columns are listed in rule 1; the vinegar line of Lecture 0 has the identity and concentration.
*Worked answer.* Chemicals: 5% acetic acid, water — food-grade but concentrated acetic acid on skin/hierarchy still irritates; physical: 100 °C bath, borosilicate bowl (ΔT within borosilicate band — Lecture 2), steam; environmental: trivial. PPE: splash goggles (a bubbling acid solution is a splash class by the Lecture 1 table); gloves optional. Failure modes: (1) boil-dry of the *bath* — the failure that turns Rung 1 into Rung 3 (Lecture 3); mitigation: time-on-heat in the notebook, top up the bath; (2) bumping/spatter as concentration rises; (3) bowl cracks (thermal band says no, if borosilicate is genuinely what you have — the assessment's one verification step: *confirm the bowl*, because soda-lime at 100 °C with a cold counter draft is inside the danger band). Quench: remove heat, let cool on the bath; nothing exotic. Disposal: it's vinegar — down the sink is fine; *the concentrated product* is your reagent, label and store it (access rule: never a food bottle). Scale: doubling the volume doubles time-on-bath and spatter energy — the assessment does not transfer; do not scale. The two rules doing the most work: **rule 1** (the written table is what caught the bowl-material question) and **rule 3/rung-matching** (water bath, not flame — the choice that makes every failure mode mild). Second way to the same conclusion: run the Lecture 3 rule "lowest rung that clears the job" — the job needs ≤100 °C, so Rung 1 is the matched rung, and each failure mode above is milder at Rung 1 than at any higher rung — the same assessment derived from the ladder instead of the Charter. (P11 + P10 + Charter.)

**Exercise 4 ●●○.** In Lab 1.1's Case A terms: a notebook says 50.0 g of a salt hydrate gave 18.9 g of product, theoretical 21.0 g. What is the percent yield, what does the loss ledger have to explain, and what is the *smallest* named-loss ledger that closes it exactly?

*Hint.* Percent first; then remember the ledger is in grams.
*Worked answer.* Percent yield = 18.9/21.0 = **90.0%** — so the ledger must account for 2.1 g. Smallest exact ledger: two named losses, e.g. transfer loss 1.2 g + filtration retention 0.9 g = 2.1 g; every named loss must be a *mechanically plausible* class (transfer, filtration retention, spill, drying), not a fudge factor — if you cannot name it, leave it as an honest gap (Lecture 4's rule). Way 2: work in fractions — yield 0.90, so losses 0.10 of theoretical = 2.10 g; the fraction view is what generalizes to multi-step routes (each step's yield multiplies — the cumulative-route-yield face of **P12**, Chapter 9's network arithmetic).

**Exercise 5 ●●●.** The ruined-world inventory needs three reagents next month: table salt (NaCl), baking soda (NaHCO₃), and dilute hydrochloric acid (~1 L of ~2 M). Your scavenged map says: two grocery stores (both looted of cleaning products, salt and baking soda intact), one pool-supply aisle intact (muriatic acid 31.45% and sodium bisulfate), and a hardware store with lye-based drain cleaner and muriatic acid at unknown assay. Plan the sourcing with **P10** route economics, and flag every Charter contact the plan creates.

*Hint.* Score per P10: (a) availability, (b) apparatus demand, (c) hazard, (d) yield & separability — and remember P1's failure modes (additives, low assay).
*Worked answer.* NaCl: grocery salt — availability high, zero apparatus, hazard trivial; *additives*: iodide and anti-caking are present (P1 failure mode); accept for most uses, note the Ch 13 interference (iodide shows up in some wet tests), and set a "clean salt" need to be met by recrystallization (Ch 5) rather than a second source. NaHCO₃: grocery baking soda — P1 caveat is *baking powder* (a different product with added acid — the trap), otherwise clean. HCl: two routes — (i) pool-aisle muriatic 31.45% diluted (needs only Lab 1.2's arithmetic: to make ~1 L of ~2 M from 31.45% w/w, Ch 4's w/w→molarity conversion applies; apparatus: none; hazard: the *dilution itself* — always acid into water, outdoors or cross-ventilated, splash goggles + nitrile per the Lecture 1 table); (ii) hardware muriatic at unknown assay — rejected: unknown assay violates P1 (no label trust, Exercise 1's rule-out) and would require Ch 13 verification before use, an apparatus demand in disguise. Sodium bisulfate is a *different* acid source (bisulfate), not an HCl substitute without extra chemistry (Ch 8) — parked. So: salt + baking soda at the grocery, pool muriatic diluted by the book. Charter contacts: one open-acid handling session (dilution — goggles, gloves, ventilation, quench basin set per rule 4), and the *storage* contact: diluted acid in a labeled non-food bottle under the access rule. Two ways to the acid decision: P10 scoring (i beats ii on every axis) and P1's own trust rule (a label that doesn't state assay is not a label) — same verdict, two named tools.

**Exercise 6 ◆ (pattern-unknown; no hint).** A scavenged plastic jar holds a white powder. The label is in a language you cannot read. Your notebook from the previous owner says only "backing soda" — a typo, and you must decide today whether the jar is baking soda (NaHCO₃) or baking powder before a recipe-dependent synthesis.

*Worked answer (diagnoses the tool).* The question "is this the stuff I think it is, with no instruments" is exactly **P9 — instrumentless verification**, chained with **P1** (the label failed; the test *is* the new label). The convergent cascade, each test orthogonal: (1) the vinegar test — NaHCO₃ fizzes promptly in vinegar (weak acid + bicarbonate → CO₂, the Chapter 8/10 reaction); baking powder fizzes *by itself* when wet (its built-in acid fires — the "acid-base system in one can" of Lecture 0's sheet); (2) the water test alone separates them: plain water dissolves NaHCO₃ to a mildly alkaline, non-fizzy solution; baking powder fizzes on plain water contact; (3) taste is *not* a test (Charter: unknowns never in the mouth — even in a ruin, especially in a ruin). Verdict rule: two orthogonal positives before the notebook entry is written (P9's convergence rule), and the entry itself follows the Lecture 4 template with the test results as observations. The deeper diagnosis: the exercise is Lecture 0's "read the label" inverted — when the label is gone, *you* write the new label, and you earn it with evidence.
