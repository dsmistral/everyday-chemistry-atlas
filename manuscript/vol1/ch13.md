# Vol I · Chapter 13 — Analysis Without Instruments: The Wet Qualitative Scheme

Chapter 9 built the precipitation network; Chapter 10 taught recognition as a separate skill; Chapter 8 taught the eye to read color as concentration. This chapter is where those three threads are braided into the ruined-world lab's crown achievement: **the identification of an unknown substance with no instrument more sophisticated than a drop counter, a white dish, and a plan.** The premise has never been starker: the spectrometer is gone, the chromatograph is gone, the balance may be a borrowed kitchen scale — and yet the question "what is this powder?" still has a rigorous answer, because chemistry has always had one. The wet qualitative scheme — flame, curd, cloud, color, and ratio — was the professional's toolkit for a century before the first spectrograph, and the corpus supplies it whole: the pipette calibrated gravimetrically to 27.5 drops per milliliter (N63), the TLC plates cast from recycled drywall (N64), the lead-paint cascade that runs a presumptive screen and two confirmatory falls in one spot plate (N60), the fingerprinting series that identifies a person the way this chapter identifies a powder — by orthogonal evidence, never one test (N61/N65/N66/N67), the presumptive panel run on law-abiding pharmacy proxies with a two-reagent cross-verification rule (N71), and the colorimetry ladder run down from a 1000-ppm standard to a 7-ppm floor by nothing but halving (N72/N73/N74). **Identification is never one test; it is the convergence of cheap orthogonal evidence — and a verdict is only as good as the witnesses that agree on it.** The chapter's four questions, in bench order: **what makes a test trustworthy, how do you read a cation, how do you read an anion — and how do you assemble the verdict into a scheme that prices its own certainty?**

## Lecture 0 — The instrumentless premise (identification without the machine)

Stand in the ruined-world lab before an unlabeled jar. What can you actually *do*? You cannot send it away; there is no away. You cannot put it in a machine; the machine's maker is a decade gone. What you have is what this book has built in twelve chapters: a cabinet of household reagents whose behaviors you can predict (Chapters 8 and 9), a set of physical separations that sort mixtures by their biggest property differences (Chapters 5 and 6), a gas-bench that recognizes carbon dioxide, hydrogen, and oxygen by their manners (Chapter 10), a redox table that predicts which metal displaces which (Chapter 11), and a meter that reads free energy on contact (Chapter 12). Identification is those chapters *used backwards*: not "what will this reagent do" but "which reagent's behavior, observed, eliminates what the unknown cannot be."

The founding act of the instrumentless lab is already in the corpus: **calibrate the drop.** N63's gravimetric pipette calibration — count 100 drops, weigh the water, divide — turned a disposable pipette into a 27.5-drops-per-milliliter volumetric instrument [computed and pinned in Chapters 1 and 4]. That is the whole method of this chapter in miniature: *the instrument is not absent; it is built from arithmetic applied to what is at hand.* The same spirit casts TLC plates from drywall scraps (N64) and builds a colorimeter's calibration curve from seven halvings of a single standard (N72). The mark-one-eyeball, run against a ladder, is a quantitative instrument — with a stated, honest precision floor.

The second founding act is a discipline, not a device: **never trust one test.** A single color, a single flame, a single curd — each has lookalikes, contaminants, and failure modes. Sodium drowns every flame in yellow (the trap of Exercise 13.5); copper's green flame and boron's green flame are twins (N72's own comparison point); a white curd can be three different chlorides. The professional scheme therefore *plans its witnesses in advance*: a presumptive test that is sensitive but not specific, then confirmatory tests that fire on different chemistry entirely (N60's design — black sulfide fall first, then golden iodide and yellow chromate on fresh portions). Two witnesses that share no failure mode, agreeing, are worth more than one expensive measurement; that is P9 — convergence of orthogonal evidence — and it is this chapter's name for what the whole book has been practicing.

The third founding act belongs to the notebook: **a verdict is a sentence with its evidence attached.** "It's carbonate" is not a result. "Fizzed in vinegar (vigorous, sustained), clouded limewater unconditionally (the 1.4 × 10⁻⁷ M threshold, Ch 10), pH 11.4 in solution (the Ch 8 ladder), no barium-chloride fall (not sulfate)" — that is a result, and Chapter 1's binding entry format was built to hold it. The lab's honesty culture applies with special force here: the wet scheme's verdicts are *inference from evidence*, never certainty (Chapter 9's form-trap rule: hydrates, polymorphs, and basic salts masquerade as one another; polymorph identification without instruments is a convergence argument, printed as one).

And the premise's gift, stated once and then used everywhere: **cheap tests, repeated orthogonally, beat one expensive test.** The ruined world did not invent this economy; it merely re-derives what every bench chemist knew in 1920. The corpus's fingerprinting series runs it in its purest form — iodine fumes, ninhydrin spray, superglue vapor, gentian violet stain, each blind to what the others see — and the last lecture of this chapter is that series read as method.

## Lecture 1 — The evidence toolkit (convergence, controls, and the drop)

**What makes a test trustworthy.** Three properties, checkable in advance, decide whether an observation can carry a verdict: **sensitivity** (does it fire at the concentrations you actually have — the serial-dilution ladder exists to answer exactly this, N60/N72's shared methodology), **specificity** (does it fire on the suspected ion *only* — silver nitrate fires on chloride, but also on bromide and iodide; the confirmatory witness must differ in kind), and **orthogonality** (do the two tests share a failure mode? Two color tests driven by the same complexation share one; a color test plus a flame plus a solubility behavior share none). The scheme's every verdict below is built on this triad, and every exercise asks *why this witness earns its vote* — never just "what color."

**The control well.** The corpus's most transferable habit is the plain-water control: run the reagent on a blank *beside* every unknown, in the same tray, at the same time (N72's protocol: the halving ladder run against a plain-water control well; N74's ladders likewise). The blank prices the reagent's own color and the water's own contaminants — and the household's water is *not* pure (Chapter 4's dissolved-solids arithmetic), so the blank is never a formality. A white ice-cube tray or a flat white saucer is the ruined-world spot plate; each depression is one test, one portion, one witness. **Never return a spot plate to food use** — the safety table's row 8.

**The drop as instrument.** N63's calibration (27.5 drops/mL; one drop ≈ 0.036 mL, the Chapter 4 pin) converts the pipette into the bench's only precision device. The dilution arithmetic it enables is Chapter 4's C₁V₁ = C₂V₂ at its cheapest: one drop of stock in 100 drops of water is a 1:100 dilution, executed and counted in seconds. The gentian-violet bath of the forensics section is mixed 1:9 by the same counted drops (N67's ladder chaining N63's pipette — the register's own observation); the presumptive reagents of the anion bench are dosed by drops onto pinches. The discipline is Chapter 1's old rule in a new coat: *state the precision you actually have* — a drop-mixed reagent is honest to roughly ±5%, and the ladder method tolerates that because it compares *like with like*.

**The presumptive/confirmatory structure.** The lead cascade (N60) is the model, and it deserves to be read as a design: a **presumptive test** is chosen for maximum sensitivity — sodium sulfide, which drops nearly every heavy metal as a dark sulfide, blackest for lead (PbS, Ksp ~3 × 10⁻²⁸, the deepest "gone" in Chapter 9's battery) — and a **confirmatory test** is chosen for *different chemistry with different failure modes*: golden PbI₂ and yellow PbCrO₄, iodide and chromate where sulfide was the witness, precipitation where color was the signal. At a 0.0100 M dosing the three anions leave lead at 3 × 10⁻²⁶, 2.80 × 10⁻¹¹, and 7.10 × 10⁻⁵ M respectively — a **21.4-order-of-magnitude span in one spot plate** (Chapter 9's arithmetic, re-derived in Lab 13.1 and the reason the cascade *works*: each anion chooses a different depth of "gone"). Presumptive tests screen; confirmatory tests name; and the two together are the bench's version of the two-witness rule.

**The serial-halving ladder.** The concentration instrument of last resort: half the solution, add water to volume, mix, repeat. Each rung halves the concentration; seven rungs take a 1000-ppm standard below 10 ppm [computed, Lab 13.2]. The ladder's power is not the dilution — it is the *rungs as a comparison set*: an unknown of unknown concentration, run beside the ladder, is read by matching its color to the rung it resembles (N74's visual colorimetry: interpolate between rungs and you have a measurement honest to the rung spacing; N72's 540-nm instrumental bridge named as what the eyeball replaces). Chapter 8's colorimetry ladder re-pins here unchanged; the two-axis lesson rides with it — *position/identity and intensity/concentration are different axes, and the ladder reads the second without ever needing the first.*

## Lecture 2 — The cation bench: flame, ammonia, and the lead cascade

**The flame table, read honestly.** A clean loop (nichrome wire or a stainless pin, scrubbed and dipped in muriatic acid between tests — the Ch 4/8 pin), a pinches-worth of the unknown dissolved in a drop of water, a blue flame: the oldest cation screen there is. The colors, web-verified this session [convergent]:

| ion | flame | the honest caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Na⁺ | yellow (overwhelming) | sodium is *everywhere* — a trace drowns every other color; the great contaminant |
| K⁺ | lilac/pale violet | visible only through cobalt glass, which filters sodium's yellow |
| Ca²⁺ | orange/brick-red | persistent, gritty red-orange; distinguish from lithium by intensity |
| Cu²⁺ (and B) | blue-green | boron's flame-green is the twin (N72's curcumin-vs-flame comparison) |
| Li⁺ | purple-blue/carmine | deep red-pink, saturated |
| Sr²⁺ | deep crimson to brick-red | the fireworks red [convergent, verified this session] |
| Ba²⁺ | yellow-green/apple-green | the barium row, named for the knowledge-only anion bench below |

Two traps are printed with the table because they cost real verdicts: **the sodium trap** (a yellow flame says nothing — the test earns a verdict only in sodium's *absence*, which is itself evidence) and **the twin trap** (green says "copper or boron"; only a second witness separates them — N72's episode exists because curcumin answers what the flame cannot). The flame is a screen, never a naming test: it *orders* the bench's next move.

**The ammonia ladder — the reagent that separates by amount.** Household ammonia, added dropwise, is the cation bench's second pillar, and its logic is Chapter 9's hydroxide column made visible: a few drops drop the pale-blue Cu(OH)₂ curd; *excess* ammonia dissolves it to the deep royal-blue [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺ complex (M 227.726, Lab 13.1's composition row) — a color change Ch 8 taught and Ch 24 will own in full (the complex-ion flag). Iron answers differently: Fe³⁺ gives the rust-brown Fe(OH)₃ that ammonia *cannot* dissolve — so one reagent, read at two doses, separates copper from iron in a spot plate with no other witness needed. The general ladder (add more, watch what happens) is the qualitative scheme's one genuinely *graded* move — most tests are yes/no; the ammonia ladder is a sentence with clauses.

**The lead cascade (N60), the bench's spine test.** The episode's own design, priced at Chapter 9's constants: presumptive Na₂S → black PbS; confirmatory KI → golden PbI₂; confirmatory K₂CrO₄ → yellow PbCrO₄ (the three-anion span above). The spot-plate rule is absolute — lead, chromate, and sulfide at drop scale only, and the waste goes down Chapter 9's precipitation route: collect the heavy-metal solids, don't launder them into the drain. The cascade's deepest lesson is the one the corpus wrote it to teach: *the same cation, three different anion partners, three different colors and three different depths of insoluble* — the precipitation network read *backwards* is the identification engine. When the scheme needs to know "is this lead?", it does not ask a machine; it asks the network.

**Silver, and the two white curds.** Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ gives the white curd of AgCl that darkens in light (the photographic chemistry, Chapter 19's preview) — but Pb²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ gives a white curd too, and only the differentiators separate them (Exercise 13.4's whole subject): **hot water** — PbCl₂ dissolves at 1.1 g/100 mL cold and far more hot, so the lead curd dissolves on the warm plate while AgCl (1.9 × 10⁻⁴ g/100 mL, flat) does not [convergent, verified this session]; and **ammonia** — AgCl dissolves as [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ and re-precipitates the moment the solution is acidified, while PbCl₂ ignores ammonia entirely. Two white powders that look identical, separated in two steps by arithmetic that was already in Chapter 9's tables: the wet scheme in one vial.

## Lecture 3 — The anion bench: fizz, cloud, curd, and the Ksp matrix backwards

**Carbonate — the fizz with two witnesses.** The fizz is Chapter 9's driver 2, and the recognition is Chapter 10's; the bench adds the *quantitative* close: 0.500 g of washing soda is 4.7175 × 10⁻³ mol of Na₂CO₃, whose fizz is 0.2076 g of CO₂ — 0.1156 L at the 24.5 L/mol working pin — and whose neutralization prices at 11.26 mL of the 0.8376 M house vinegar (Exercise 13.1, all routes in Lab 13.2). The second witness is the limewater cloud, and its arithmetic is why the cloud is *unconditional*: saturated limewater carries [Ca²⁺] ≈ 0.0233 M, and CaCO₃'s Ksp (3.36 × 10⁻⁹) means a carbonate concentration of 1.4 × 10⁻⁷ M already clouds it (Chapter 10's chain, re-derived) — the fizz stream exceeds the threshold by four orders of magnitude before the first bubble breaks the surface. The honest seam stays flagged as Chapter 10 flagged it: free CO₃²⁻ from dissolved CO₂ is a coupled Ka–Ksp question that Chapter 24 owns; the *verdict* needs none of that, because the cloud is observed.

**Sulfate — the anion the X-ray room trusts.** BaCl₂ solution drops BaSO₄ as a fine white precipitate — insoluble in excess acid, which is the differentiator that matters (carbonate falls too, but acid dissolves it; the sulfate fall *survives* acid). The safety lesson is Chapter 9's barium fence, restated in its home context: *soluble* barium salts are poisons; BaSO₄ is what the X-ray room makes you swallow precisely because sulfate is the fence — the anion renders the toxic cation insoluble, solubility-is-safety in one sentence. The bench therefore handles barium chloride as a *reagent at drop scale, knowledge-only* (the safety table's row 3), never as a bulk salt.

**Chloride — the curd and the light.** AgNO₃ gives the AgCl white curd, darkening in daylight — the same chemistry Lecture 2 used to separate silver's curd from lead's, run here from the anion's side: add silver nitrate to the unknown; a curd that darkens in light and dissolves in ammonia with re-precipitation on acidification is chloride, two witnesses in one depression. Muriatic acid (the Ch 4/8 pin) is the household chloride standard the bench uses for controls — the control well discipline of Lecture 1 applied with the very acid the house already owns.

**Iodide — the blue that earns a color.** The iodized-salt chain (Exercise 13.3) prices the whole test: household iodized salt carries iodide at a label-class ~45 mg KI per kg (labels vary; the chain scales linearly with whatever the label says [single-source datum, the episode's figure]) — 34.4 ppm I⁻ — and 2.00 g of it dissolved in 10 mL water puts 68.80 µg of iodide in the test well: 5.42 × 10⁻⁵ M, deep in the regime where peroxide-oxidized iodine meets starch and the blue-black complex appears (the starch–triiodide blue is micromolar-scale sensitive [convergent] — one of the naked eye's most sensitive tests). The reagent faces are already in the house: 3% H₂O₂ from the first-aid shelf, starch from the pantry, and the tincture-iodine bottle (N61) is both the forensics section's fuming source and this test's positive control.

**Borate and salicylate — the two colorimetries.** N72's curcumin face: boron plus curcumin in strong acid forms rosocyanine, red-brown — the turmeric paper of Chapter 8 wearing its *other* hat (the acid–base indicator and the boron colorimeter are different chemistries of the same root, and the chapter keeps the two hats labeled). The episode's own method is this lecture's method in full: a 1000-ppm borax standard (borax is 11.34% B — Lab 13.1), halved down a ladder to its 7-ppm floor against a plain-water control, read by eye with the 540-nm instrument named as what the eyeball replaces. N74's salicylate face: Fe³⁺ turns salicylate violet (the Fe(C₇H₅O₃)₃ complex, M 467.187 in the lab battery) — aspirin's signature at the bench, with the basis pair printed once and forever (76.11% of aspirin's mass is salicylate *as the anion*; 76.67% *as the acid* — the episode's 76.11% is the anion basis, and no percentage should ever travel without its basis named, the Ch 10 RA-4 lesson made routine).

**The matrix, read backwards.** The anion bench is Chapter 9's 36-cell solubility matrix run in reverse: *given the cation, choose the anion whose precipitate differs in color and in depth.* Sulfate's witness is barium's acid-proof white; chloride's is silver's light-darkening curd; iodide's is lead's golden fall; chromate's is lead's yellow; carbonate's is not a precipitate at all but a gas — driver 2 — plus calcium's unconditional cloud. Different anions, different depths of "gone," different colors: **the network's columns are the identification scheme's rows**, and nothing new had to be built — Chapter 9's arithmetic was the instrument all along.

## Lecture 4 — Chromatography and the concentration ladder: the plate and the rungs

**The plate from the wall (N64).** The corpus's TLC episode casts plates from recycled drywall: the gypsum core (CaSO₄·₂H₂O, M 172.164 — the Ch 5/9 pin) re-fired to plaster, rehydrated with a binder (cornstarch or talcum as the store-bought substitutes), spread on glass, and set in a **2-minute window** [single-source, the episode's own timing datum] — the working time that separates a smooth plate from a cracked one. The plate is the cheapest separation instrument the ruined world can make, and it does what no spot test can: it *separates the mixture before the color question is asked* — the two-in-one stain that would confuse a test tube becomes two spots at two heights, each readable alone. This is Chapter 5's separation train at micro-scale: the solvent carries each component up the plaster by its own partition between the wet solid and the moving liquid, and the components arrive at different heights for the same reason they arrive in different fractions of a distillation — *differing willingness to leave*.

**Rf — the ratio that travels.** The retention factor is a quotient, and the quotient is the design: **Rf = spot distance / solvent-front distance**. Plate A's spots at 1.20, 2.40, and 3.60 cm under a 4.00-cm front read Rf 0.300, 0.600, 0.900; plate B's (the drywall plate) at 2.70 and 2.25 cm under a 9.00-cm front read 0.300 and 0.250 (Lab 13.1's battery). Two disciplines ride with the definition: **the round-trip** (Rf × front must reproduce the measured distance — the measurement is only trusted if it survives its own arithmetic) and **the ratio's reason** (Rf is deliberately insensitive to how far the front ran — a short plate and a long plate give the same Rf for the same system, which is why a published Rf is comparable across benches at all). Rf is not an identity card by itself — it is a *screening coordinate* that orders the confirmatory tests; the scheme never names from a spot height alone.

**The ladder as the calibration curve.** N73's Dragendorff episode prices the method end-to-end: the quinine subject at 83 ppm in tonic [convergent — the regulatory content figure, verified this session], a working standard at 80 ppm, and a serial-halving ladder — 80 → 40 → 20 → 10 → 5 ppm — run beside the unknown; the unknown is *read against the rungs*, and the read is a measurement with a stated floor (the last rung that still answers). N74's salicylate ladder does the same from 150 mg/dL down to 9.375 (the episode prints 9.5 at 2 s.f. [single-source print, the arithmetic beneath it computed]); N72's boron ladder from 113.4 ppm down to 7.09. Three episodes, one method: **the ladder is the instrument, the rungs are its calibration, and the eye is the detector** — with the control well always in the tray to price the reagent's own answer.

**The eyeball as colorimeter, honestly.** Chapter 8 built the colorimetry ladder for pH; this chapter generalizes it: *any* reagent whose color intensifies with concentration can be made quantitative by comparing unknown against rungs. The honest limits are part of the method and are printed with it: the eye reads *matching* well (side-by-side rungs) and *interpolation* crudely; it cannot read two dyes at once (interferences — N74's own iron-hydroxide interference datum is the class example: clean up or mask before you read); and its floor is the rung spacing, not a photomultiplier's. N72's 540-nm bridge is the named boundary: a spectrometer reads what the eye reads, more precisely — the method is the same ladder, the detector is the difference, and the ruined world runs it with the detector it has.

**The two-axis lesson, re-pinned.** Identity and concentration are different questions (Chapter 8's lesson, third printing): Rf and flame and curd answer *what*; the ladder answers *how much*. A scheme verdict that answers one and not the other is half a verdict — and the full scheme below always states which question its witnesses actually answered.

## Lecture 5 — The forensics section (FINDING-A): fingerprints, and the scheme assembled

**The optional section, sanctioned at the session-6 gate — and the corpus's most complete method-chain.** The fingerprinting series (N61/N65/N66/N67, with the dusting finale N69 named at register level) is the instrumentless-identification premise performed on a *person* instead of a powder, and the Atlas reads it as method: four chemistries, four surfaces, one convergence discipline.

- **Iodine fuming (N61) — the porous-surface classic.** Drugstore tincture iodine warmed in a sealed chamber (a Glad container) sublimes iodine onto the paper; the lipids of a latent print absorb it and the print blooms brown. The fix is the pantry's own chemistry: starch spray (pasta water) locks the brown as the blue-black starch complex; the quench is vitamin C — ascorbic acid reduces I₂ to colorless I⁻ and the stain vanishes (Exercise 13.6's pattern-hunt asks *why* the same bottle that fixes also erases; the resolution is Chapter 11's redox, and no worked solution is printed).
- **Ninhydrin (N65) — the amino-acid developer.** Ninhydrin (C₉H₆O₄, M 178.143) reacts with the amino acids of sweat to **Ruhemann's purple** [convergent] — the 0.6% w/v acetone formulation, the drugstore fingertip sprayer, and the kinetics datum the episode contributed: room temperature develops *sharper* prints over hours than a hot iron's fast blur — kinetics trading speed for resolution, Chapter 25's whole lesson in one bench choice.
- **Superglue (N66) — the non-porous specialist.** Cyanoacrylate vapor polymerizes along the moisture ridge of a print on glass or metal (moisture-initiated polymerization [convergent]) — a sealed container for 24 h naturally, or accelerated to minutes with a pad of ~0.5 M NaOH (the Crystal drain-opener substitution, the Ch 8 reagent). The white polymer casts the print in plastic.
- **Gentian violet (N67) — the adhesive-surface specialist.** The 1% drugstore stain, diluted 1:9 by N63's counted drops, develops the prints that adhere to tape's sticky side — then the ~1 M HCl clearing bath washes the background stain and leaves the print. The register's chain-point is the Atlas's point: *the pipette calibration (N63) is cited by the dilution (N67) — the instrument built in one episode is load-bearing in the fourth.*

The series' map — **porous → iodine/ninhydrin; non-porous → superglue; adhesive → gentian violet** — is the orthogonal-coverage lesson in its cleanest form: *the surface chooses the chemistry, and no one chemistry covers all surfaces.* That is why the scheme below, asked to identify a powder, never asks one test to cover all powders either.

**The presumptive panel (N71) — the legality lesson printed as a boundary.** Marquis reagent (formaldehyde in concentrated H₂SO₄) and Mandelin reagent (ammonium metavanadate in concentrated H₂SO₄) color a panel of *law-abiding pharmacy proxies* — the episode's design choice is itself the safety boundary the Atlas adopts verbatim: presumptive color panels are demonstrated on substances you can legally own, never on anything else [convergent compositions; the legality datum single-source and binding as a rule]. The episode's two craft rules generalize to every color test in this chapter: the **one-minute rule** (a color that appears after a minute of waiting is not the color — develop times are part of the method, not a footnote) and the **two-reagent cross-verification** (Marquis says, Mandelin confirms; never name from one reagent — the two-witness rule, imported whole). The chain closes the loop the register loves: N70's synthesized ammonium metavanadate *is* N71's Mandelin reagent — a synthesized node feeding the next episode's test bench, the reagent network in miniature. Dragendorff (N73) completes the panel: the bismuth–potassium iodide reagent [convergent], mixed from Pepto-Bismol's bismuth subsalicylate (the substitution verified in-register), screening alkaloids down to the quinine of tonic water.

**The scheme assembled.** The chapter's benches, wired into one decision tree for the unlabeled jar:

1. **Physical screen** (Chapter 5/11 tools): color, crystal form, density (the 20.0 g / 7.4 mL = 2.703 → aluminium identification class, Lab 13.2), magnet response, water behavior — dissolve/refuse/partial, hot vs cold (the gypsum seam's lesson).
2. **The flame screen** (Lecture 2): a yellow flame eliminates nothing; a non-yellow flame is a strong lead; the cobalt glass separates potassium from sodium's flood.
3. **The anion bench** (Lecture 3): fizz+limewater for carbonate; BaCl₂ for sulfate; AgNO₃ for the chloride curd; H₂O₂/starch for iodide; each with its control.
4. **The cation bench** (Lecture 2): ammonia ladder; the lead cascade when heavy metals are suspected; the NH₃/hot-water differentiators when the curds are white.
5. **Chromatography** (Lecture 4): when the answer is a *mixture* — separate first, test the spots after.
6. **The verdict, priced**: every identification states its witnesses, its failure modes considered, and its honest limits (form traps flagged per Chapter 9; hydrates named as the gravimetric rule requires; concentration answered by the ladder or flagged unanswered).

The scheme's certainty is priced like every instrument in this book: a two-witness verdict with orthogonal failure modes is strong; a one-test answer is a hypothesis wearing a verdict's clothes. Chapter 19 will deepen the metal-salt rows (Cu/Fe/Zn/Al/Mg/Sn/Pb/Ag, each with its bench signatures); Chapter 22 owns the dyes and fluorescence the tonic-water demo gestures at; Chapter 24 owns the coupled equilibria and complex ions this chapter borrowed and flagged; Chapter 27 replaces the eye with the electrode where the ruined world permits; Chapter 28 owns the hazard-recognition logic that this chapter's *never-taste, never-smell* rule only begins. The wet scheme is not the end of analysis — it is the end of *instrumentless* analysis, and the last chapter of Volume I.
