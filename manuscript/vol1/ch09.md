# Vol I · Chapter 9 — Salts and the Precipitation Network

Chapter 8 priced the cabinet's acids and bases; this chapter follows the salts they make — and the ones the world makes without asking: the teapot's scale, the bathtub's ring, the aquarium's cloud, the scum line on a wash-basin in hard water. **A salt is not a residue: it is a card in a network, and the network's whole grammar is one move — two dissolved salts swap partners, and if either new pairing refuses to stay dissolved, a solid falls out of a clear liquid.** That single move, metathesis, is how the corpus's practitioners built their reagents from retail bottles: the root-killer-plus-baking-soda copper ore (N59), the carbonate hub that turns one cheap solid into a whole shelf of copper salts (N30), the oxalate trick that *makes sulfuric acid* out of a weak acid and a precipitation (N16/N28), the titration-plus-gravimetric cross-assay where the second assay is itself a precipitation weighed on a balance (N57). The corpus supplies this chapter's practitioners whole, and the precipitation network is where the Atlas's P-index is densest: P3 (the metathesis-precipitation route), P9 (instrumentless verification — every cloud in this chapter is a verdict), P12 (yield bookkeeping on solids that must be washed and dried before they can be weighed), and P14 (precipitation-driven acid liberation), performed here at last after two chapters of forward flags.

The chapter's honest contracts, stated up front: **the Ksp values are tabulated constants**, every one web-verified this session and quoted as constants — the labs compute, they do not measure equilibrium (and where the tables disagree among themselves, the disagreement is printed, not hidden); **the solubility rules are a map, not a law** — "insoluble" is a number, and Chapter 4's promise is honored here: N59's 219,000-liter solubility datum, name-checked qualitatively in Lecture 4 of that chapter, gets its real derivation from Ksp in Lab 9.2, gap and all; **the rules predict *whether*, the Ksp prices *how much*, and a third question — *which solid* — belongs to the bench**, because real precipitates can be hydrates or basic salts, and the gravimetric rule (pin the form you actually weighed) is a discipline, not a footnote; and **solubility is itself a safety property**: the same barium that is swallowed as BaSO₄ in an X-ray exam is a poison as a soluble salt, and the anion — not the element — decides which fate. The tasting rule and the bench/food line run through all of it, as they have run through every chapter.

## Lecture 0 — Two clear liquids make a cloud

Dissolve a spoon of Epsom salt in water: clear. Dissolve a spoon of washing soda in another glass: clear. Pour them together: instant white cloud, settling to a fine mud. Nothing boiled, nothing fizzed — two transparent liquids made an opaque third phase out of nothing visible. That cloud is magnesium carbonate (plus a residue we will price honestly in Lecture 3), and it is the household's most accessible demonstration that **a dissolved salt is a pair of independent ions wearing one label**. The Epsom glass carried Mg²⁺ and SO₄²⁻; the soda glass carried Na⁺ and CO₃²⁻; the four ions do not remember which glass they came from. Magnesium met carbonate, refused to stay dissolved, and left. Sulfate and sodium — the leftovers — are still in the water, and you cannot see them at all.

The house is full of this event, running without supervision:

- **The kettle's scale** is calcium carbonate that fell out of hard water on heating — the carbonate was always dissolved, waiting for an excuse (Ch 4 priced the descale; this chapter prices the fall).
- **Soap scum** is the same event with soap: the stearate ion meets Ca²⁺ or Mg²⁺ from hard water and drops as an insoluble calcium/magnesium stearate — which is why hard water wastes soap until the hardness is gone, and why "soap" and "detergent" are different inventions (the detergent's sulfonate salt stays dissolved where the soap's carboxylate cannot).
- **The bathtub ring and the aquarium cloud** are carbonate and phosphate falls, wanted in nobody's plan, driven by the same arithmetic.
- **The mason-jar miracle** — water glass plus a crystal garden — is metasilicate meeting metal ions, a precipitation slowed to sculpture speed.

This chapter asks the event four questions, in order. *What happened, exactly* — which ions paired, and why do we write it as an equation with a downward arrow (Lecture 1)? *When does it happen* — the solubility rules, the working map every bench chemist carries (Lecture 2)? *How much of it happens* — Ksp, the number under "insoluble," the ion product, the common-ion lever (Lecture 3)? And *what is it good for* — the precipitation network: how the corpus turns retail bottles into reagents, makes acids out of precipitations, and weighs answers on a balance when no instrument exists (Lectures 4 and 5). One chapter, one event, four levels of depth.

## Lecture 1 — Metathesis: the partner swap and its four drivers

Write the swap generically: **AX + BY → AY + BX**. Two salts, dissolved, each a pair of ions; the partners re-sort. The question that decides everything is whether the new pairs stay dissolved. Water is a magnificent solvent for ions, but it has tastes — and the re-sorting either produces only pairs water loves (the liquid stays clear, and effectively *nothing happened*: mix, retaste, identical solution), or it produces at least one pair water refuses, and the refusal is the reaction. The four refusals — the four **drivers** — each force the swap to run to completion, and Chapter 8 already owns two of them:

1. **A precipitate forms.** The new pair is an insoluble solid; it leaves the solution, and by leaving it pulls the reaction with it (Le Chatelier's lever applied to a solid's exit). This chapter's whole business.
2. **A gas forms.** Ch 8's fizz: acid plus carbonate, H₂CO₃ that will not stay built, CO₂ that leaves. The escape of the gas is the driver (and Ch 4's never-latch rule is the safety face of it).
3. **Water forms.** Neutralization: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O — metathesis whose precipitate is the least ionic substance in the chapter. Every titration of Ch 8 was a metathesis running on this driver; the salt ledger of that chapter is this chapter's inheritance.
4. **A weak electrolyte forms.** Any product that stays mostly undissociated (water included; weak acids too) removes ions from the free-ion pool, and the swap runs to replace them.

**Net ionic equations: the spectators leave.** In the bath-cloud glass, Mg²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → MgCO₃↓ — that is the entire event. The sodium and sulfate appear on both sides unchanged; they are **spectators**, present for mass balance and absent from the story. The professional's habit (and the exam-proofing habit) is to cancel them and write the net. The Atlas adds its own use for them: spectators are the **currency ions** — Na⁺, K⁺, NH₄⁺, NO₃⁻, acetate — the ions whose salts are (essentially always) soluble. The network's first design rule lives here: **put your target on a currency ion's back and it stays in solution; the precipitate, when you need one, must be somebody else's pairing.** P3 and P14 are both this rule read forward, in two opposite directions (Lecture 4).

The household metathesis roster, this chapter's working set (all verified in Lab 9.1's balance ledger — every equation atom-balanced both sides):

- **The bath cloud:** MgSO₄ + Na₂CO₃ → MgCO₃↓ + Na₂SO₄ (driver 1).
- **The copper ore (the corpus's own run, N59):** CuSO₄ + 2 NaHCO₃ → CuCO₃↓ + Na₂SO₄ + H₂O + CO₂ — drivers 1 *and 2* stacked in one equation (the carbonate precipitates, the bicarbonate's leftover acid-base bookkeeping vents as gas; the foam and hiss the episode shows is the second driver made audible). Note the arithmetic of that bookkeeping: two bicarbonates per copper — one becomes carbonate, the other pays the H₂O and CO₂.
- **The driveway demo:** CaCl₂ (winter ice melt) + Na₂CO₃ → CaCO₃↓ + 2 NaCl — a white marble-colored fall from two hardware-shelf clears.
- **The neutralization pin (Ch 8):** HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O (driver 3).
- **The fizz pin (Ch 8/Ch 4):** CaCO₃ + 2 HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂ — the descale as metathesis (drivers 2+3, and the equilibrium story of *why weak acid dissolves "insoluble" carbonate* gets its full treatment in Ch 24; the lever was already stated in Ch 4 and is re-stated here in one line: the acid consumes CO₃²⁻ on the solution side, the product [Ca²⁺][CO₃²⁻] falls below Ksp, and the solid must dissolve to restore it).
- **The no-reaction, honored as a result:** CuSO₄ + 2 NaCl → *no net change* — the re-sort gives CuCl₂ and Na₂SO₄, both beautifully soluble; clear stays clear, and "nothing happened" is a genuine, checkable verdict, not a failure of nerve. Half of running this network is knowing when it does *not* fire.
- **The P14 engine (performed this chapter):** CuSO₄·5H₂O + H₂C₂O₄·2H₂O → CuC₂O₄↓ + H₂SO₄ + 7 H₂O (driver 1 doing thermodynamic work — Lecture 4).
- **The recognition cascade (N60, knowledge here, bench in Ch 13):** Pb²⁺ + S²⁻ → PbS↓ (black); Pb²⁺ + 2 I⁻ → PbI₂↓ (golden); Pb²⁺ + CrO₄²⁻ → PbCrO₄↓ (yellow) — three precipitations in three colors, the presumptive-then-confirmatory logic of forensic lead testing, cited here as the recognition pattern and forward-flagged to Chapter 13's wet scheme and Chapter 28's hazard-recognition chapter.

## Lecture 2 — The solubility rules, rebuilt from the cabinet

General chemistry prints the solubility rules as a table of exceptions; the Atlas rebuilds them as what they are — **a field map of the ion pairings water refuses**, with a household bottle pinned to nearly every entry. The working set (the full classical table, with the exceptions named and the battery's Ksp anchors attached where this chapter owns the number):

1. **Group 1 cations (Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, …) and NH₄⁺: always soluble.** No exception worth the name. Baking soda, washing soda, lye, table salt, ammonia cleaner — this rule is why the cabinet's own staples never precipitate each other, and why P3's design rule puts targets on these backs.
2. **Nitrates: always soluble.** (Acetates and chlorates essentially always; silver acetate is the borderline case, below the household floor.) The nitrate ion is the network's second currency — the anion of last resort when you need a cation in solution and *nothing else* may remain.
3. **Chlorides, bromides, iodides: soluble except Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, Hg₂²⁺.** Table salt and muriatic acid ride this rule; silver nitrate plus table salt is the classical white curd (AgCl, Ksp 1.77 × 10⁻¹⁰, darkening in light — the photographer's face of the same insolubility). Lead's chloride is the *borderline* member — soluble hot, insoluble cold — and that temperature behavior is itself a purification tool (Ch 13's territory, named here).
4. **Sulfates: soluble except Ba²⁺, Pb²⁺, Sr²⁺; Ca²⁺ slightly.** Epsom rides the rule; gypsum is the "slightly" — 0.24 g per 100 g of water (Ch 4's pinned value; N64's drywall route mines exactly this smallness); barium sulfate is the rule's hard edge (Ksp 1.08 × 10⁻¹⁰) and the X-ray-contrast face of Lecture 5's safety argument.
5. **Carbonates, phosphates, sulfites, oxalates, chromates: insoluble except Group 1/NH₄⁺** (chromate's calcium salt is the borderline member, below the household floor). The scum rule. The teapot scale, the bath cloud, the ore run, the P14 lever, and the kidney stone (CaC₂O₄, Ksp 2.7 × 10⁻⁹ — the human body's own precipitation network, mis-scheduled) are all this line. Magnesium carbonate carries this chapter's honesty case: "insoluble" at 0.220 g per liter — a cloud, yes, but a fifth of a gram stays behind in every liter (Lecture 3 prices it).
6. **Hydroxides: insoluble except Group 1 (Ba²⁺ the notable soluble-ish exception; Sr²⁺/Ca²⁺ slightly).** Milk of magnesia is the rule as a retail product; limewater is the "slightly" as a classroom classic; the blue gel of copper hydroxide is the rule at the bench. Silver's "hydroxide" will not stay built (it dehydrates to the oxide) — the map has a marsh or two, and naming one is worth more than pretending the map is smooth.
7. **Sulfides: insoluble except Group 1/2/NH₄⁺.** The blackest of the blacks (PbS, Ksp ~3 × 10⁻²⁸) — the presumptive screen of N60's cascade. (The soluble-alkaline-earth members hydrolyze water on the way in — the map's seam flagged, no number printed here.)

**The matrix.** Lab 9.1 runs the map as an engine: six household-relevant cations (Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Cu²⁺, Ba²⁺, Ag⁺) against six anions (NO₃⁻, Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻, CO₃²⁻, OH⁻, C₂O₄²⁻) — 36 verdicts, each with its rule citation and its Ksp anchor where the battery owns the number. Two patterns jump out of the grid, and both are design tools. First: the **nitrate row/column is clear everywhere** — currency, again. Second: the precipitates cluster on the carbonate/oxalate/hydroxide side — which is why the network's favorite insolubles are carbonates (cheap, byproduct-clean) and oxalates (the P14 lever), and why the byproduct of nearly every metathesis you *want* to fire is designed onto the soluble side (Na₂SO₄, NaCl, NaNO₃): **the target separates itself because the other pairing refuses to dissolve** — or, read the other way (N59's direction), the target is itself the refusal.

## Lecture 3 — Ksp: "insoluble" gets its number

Chapter 4 gave the constant its first three digits; this chapter works it. For a solid MX in contact with water, dissolution stalls at a fixed product: **[M⁺][X⁻] = Ksp** — the solubility product. For a 1:1 salt, each ion's saturation concentration equals s and Ksp = s²; for a 1:2 salt like Mg(OH)₂, Ksp = 4s³ (the exponents are the composition's shadow — Chapter 3's atom counts reappearing as algebra, exactly as Ch 4 printed). The four Ch 4 anchors ride unchanged (cross-pinned in Lab 9.2's battery, round-trips to machine precision): CaCO₃ 3.36 × 10⁻⁹ → s 5.797 × 10⁻⁵ M; AgCl 1.77 × 10⁻¹⁰ → 1.330 × 10⁻⁵ M; BaSO₄ 1.08 × 10⁻¹⁰ → 1.039 × 10⁻⁵ M; Mg(OH)₂ 5.61 × 10⁻¹² → 1.119 × 10⁻⁴ M. This chapter adds the network's own favorites, all web-verified this session: MgCO₃ 6.82 × 10⁻⁶, CaC₂O₄ 2.7 × 10⁻⁹, CuCO₃ 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁰ (a table-spread constant — some compilations print 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁰; the spread is printed, and it matters below), PbI₂ 7.1 × 10⁻⁹, PbCrO₄ 2.8 × 10⁻¹³, PbS ~3 × 10⁻²⁸.

**The ion product Q: the verdict before the event.** Q is [M][X] computed from what you *actually have*; Ksp is what the saturated solution *permits*. Three verdicts, the whole decision procedure of the chapter:

- **Q < Ksp:** unsaturated — nothing falls; add more and it dissolves.
- **Q = Ksp:** saturated — the edge; the last crystal added sits unchanged.
- **Q > Ksp:** supersaturated for that pairing — a precipitate forms, *and keeps forming until Q is dragged back down to Ksp.* The solid's exit is the drag.

Lab 9.2's Q battery runs the cases: the bath-cloud glass mixed at 0.05 M each way overshoots MgCO₃'s Ksp by a factor of ~370 (Q 2.5 × 10⁻³ vs 6.82 × 10⁻⁶); a single-drop silver spot test overshoots AgCl by five orders. And the verdict is quantitative, not just yes/no — because the precipitate forms *until* Q = Ksp, **what remains dissolved is priced by the constant itself**: the stoichiometric bath-cloud glass finishes with [Mg²⁺] = [CO₃²⁻] = 2.612 × 10⁻³ M — the cloud took 94.8% of the magnesium and the water is still carrying the other 5.2% (0.22 g of MgCO₃ per liter, the "insoluble" that is not tiny — Ch 4's "insoluble is a number," at its most household).

**The common-ion lever: pushing a product ion in.** Add carbonate (washing soda) to a solution already carrying carbonate ion, and MgCO₃'s permitted [Mg²⁺] falls to Ksp/[CO₃²⁻]: in 0.0100 M Na₂CO₃ the magnesium floor drops to 6.82 × 10⁻⁴ M — a 3.83× suppression, and the reason the bench says *use a modest excess of the precipitating reagent, then wash it out*. The same lever read backward explains washing soda's whole household job (softening water by pushing Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ below soap's patience), and read sideways it is why the *filter cake must be washed*: the mother liquor carries the common ion, the product's own sulfate, the reagent's excess — and cold, sparing washes (Ch 5's ledger) swap them out without redissolving the product (peptization — the colloid trap that turns a washed cake back into a cloud that runs through the paper — is named here and owned by Ch 13's colloid treatment).

**The 219,000-liter question, derived at last.** Ch 4 promised the numbers; here they are, honestly. N59's episode, after precipitating its copper as the carbonate, remarks that dissolving the batch of CuCO₃ would take on the order of two hundred thousand liters of water — a backyard swimming pool [single-source corpus datum, quoted as the episode's figure]. The battery's re-derivation: the batch is 0.401919 mol (Lab 9.1's ledger); at the tabulated Ksp spread, saturation carries s = 1.183–1.581 × 10⁻⁵ M, so the batch needs **25,400–34,000 liters** to fully dissolve — *itself* a swimming pool. The episode's 219,000 L implies a solubility of 1.835 × 10⁻⁶ M (Ksp ≈ 3.4 × 10⁻¹²) — below every tabulated figure, and the honest reading is the one the Atlas always gives: the **qualitative verdict (you cannot wash this away; it is staying out of the water) survives every table in the book**, while the exact volume is constant-table-dependent — and, plausibly, the episode priced the *basic* carbonate or a stricter convention, exactly the "which solid did you form" question of Lecture 5. The gap is printed, not laundered: [computed re-derivation; single-source original; the pool image robust across the spread].

**The gypsum seam: where the ideal model meets a "slightly."** Gypsum's measured solubility (Ch 4's pinned 0.24 g/100 g; the standard table row runs 0.223–0.265 g/100 mL across 0–30 °C) works out to s = 1.394 × 10⁻² M — an *apparent* ion product of ~1.9 × 10⁻⁴. The ideal-model Ksp tabulated for CaSO₄ is not that number: tables print 9.1 × 10⁻⁶ to 2.4 × 10⁻⁵, implying s = 3.0–4.9 × 10⁻³ M. The ideal free-ion model underpredicts gypsum by roughly **3–5×** — because at gypsum's (for an "insoluble") rather generous solubility, ion pairing (the dissolved neutral CaSO⁰₄ pair is a real species) and activity corrections are no longer small. This is the model's honest seam, priced at the scale where it lives: **the ideal Ksp engine owns the far-insoluble regime (nanomolar and below, every constant in the battery); the *measured* solubility owns process design for the "slightly soluble" middle** — and N64's plaster route worked from the measured solid, as any route must. The lab prints both numbers with both labels; no verdict is claimed that the model cannot carry. (The corpus's own solubility self-correction belongs here too: the vanadate episode caught its tabulated NaVO₃ solubility figure coming out "way low" in the run and corrected on the record — N70's second in-video self-correction. Solubility is a measured property with temperature and form dependence, not a law of nature; even the tables can be wrong at your working scale, and the P12 culture is to catch it in your own ledger.)

## Lecture 4 — The precipitation network: the reagent factory

Here is the chapter's thesis, and the Atlas's Vol II in miniature: **the ions you can buy are not the reagents you need — but every missing reagent is one swap away, if you know which pairings refuse to dissolve.** The network's nodes are reagents; its edges are metatheses; its edge costs are P10's scorecard (availability, apparatus, hazard, separability — with separability decided by this chapter's rules). Run the flagships:

**The carbonate hub (N30, performed by N59→N58).** Thompson's "Chemicals on the Cheap" names the design: carbonates are *byproduct-clean building blocks* — a carbonate plus any acid gives the salt plus water plus CO₂ and nothing else, so the product never inherits a byproduct it must then shed. The hub's canonical node is copper carbonate, made from root killer plus baking soda (CuSO₄ + 2 NaHCO₃ → CuCO₃↓ + Na₂SO₄ + H₂O + CO₂ — N59's episode, its foam and hiss audible in Lecture 1). From that one green solid, every acid in the cabinet drags out a different copper salt: CuCO₃ + 2 HCl → CuCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂; + acetic → the acetate; + nitric → the nitrate (N30's hub: acetate/chloride/nitrate from one cheap intermediate). The insoluble solid is the network's *storage form* — immune to dust, water, and time, un-spoilable precisely because it refuses to dissolve — and N58's copper-compound session dissolves it onward. The hub's ledger runs in Lab 9.1 at the episode's own scale: 100.35 g of root-killer pentahydrate (0.401919 mol) plus 67.99 g of baking soda — which the episode booked as the stoichiometric equivalent, and which the ledger prices more exactly: stoichiometry asks 67.53 g, so the soda carries a 0.68% planning margin (copper limiting either way; the verdicts unchanged — the P12 ledger exists precisely to catch margins of this size, and the register's planning figure is recorded here alongside the ledger's own).

**P14 performed: the acid that precipitation makes.** Two chapters of forward flags cash here. The pattern: *want a strong acid; have its salt, but no stronger-acid precursor* — so metathesize with a weak acid whose metal-salt byproduct is INSOLUBLE; the byproduct's precipitation pulls the otherwise-unfavorable proton transfer, and the strong acid stays free in the filtrate. The corpus performed it twice, independently: the nitrate face (calcium ammonium nitrate + oxalic acid → HNO₃, N16) and the sulfate face (**CuSO₄·5H₂O + H₂C₂O₄·2H₂O → CuC₂O₄↓ + H₂SO₄ + 7 H₂O**, N28 plus a community replication). The measured planning: 22.5 g of the pentahydrate + 11.5 g of oxalic acid dihydrate — copper limiting, oxalic acid 1.2% in excess (the reagent you wash *out* of the precipitate should be the cheap one), 13.66 g of copper oxalate to filter, and **8.84 g of sulfuric acid — 0.72 M in a 125-mL filtrate** — made with no distillation and no stronger acid anywhere in the house. The design rules are the register's own: the byproduct must be insoluble enough (sodium fails — sodium oxalate is a Group-1 salt and stays dissolved, no lever: *the cation must be the one whose oxalate refuses*); it must filter clean (Fe(II) oxalate passes fritted funnels — **Cu(II) is the clean choice**, the register's datum); and it must survive the target acid (copper oxalate does). The full equilibrium story — Ksp and Ka coupled, why the acid wakes up strong — is Chapter 24's tools, forward-flagged as this chapter's one deliberate deferral.

**The thrift ledger.** The network does not waste its byproducts: N57's acid episode *saves its Na₂SO₄ for reuse* — and N59's own filtrate carries 57.09 g of exactly that salt (Lab 9.1's ledger), one metathesis's "waste" priced as another session's stock. P12's cumulative-yield culture and P10's separability scoring both live on this thrift: a route whose byproduct is a clean soluble salt is a route whose bookkeeping stays honest.

**The recognition cascade (N60, the network run backwards).** To *identify* an ion, choose precipitations whose colors and Ksp's differ: the presumptive screen (PbS, black, the blackest Ksp in the battery), then confirmatory falls (PbI₂ golden, PbCrO₄ yellow). At a 0.0100 M dosing concentration the three tests leave, respectively, [Pb²⁺] = 3 × 10⁻²⁶ M (the free-sulfide idealization flagged), 2.80 × 10⁻¹¹ M, and 7.10 × 10⁻⁵ M — a **twenty-one order-of-magnitude span in one spot plate**: each anion chooses a different depth of "gone." That is Chapter 13's wet qualitative scheme previewed in arithmetic, and Chapter 28's hazard-recognition logic besides. And the geochemical version of the same cascade is Chapter 5's evaporite sequence (carbonates → gypsum → halite → bitterns): even two "insolubles" swap partners if the product is *more* insoluble — gypsum standing in washing-soda water slowly turns to calcite, K = Ksp(CaSO₄)/Ksp(CaCO₃) ≈ 7 × 10³ (ideal basis) to ~6 × 10⁴ (measured basis) — forward by three to four orders either way, the network's thermodynamic staircase in one beaker. (Ch 33's reagent-network data model — nodes, edges, edge costs — is this lecture formalized; the forward flag stands as written in the map.)

## Lecture 5 — Working a precipitate: the bench discipline

A precipitate is not an answer until it has been *worked*, and the working is where honest chemistry lives or dies. The bench sequence, with the corpus's own lessons pinned at each step:

**Drive it to completion, and prove it.** A stoichiometric mix is never complete (the bath cloud left 5.2% of its magnesium in solution); the bench answer is a modest excess of the precipitating reagent (the 20%-excess case: 98.72% down), and the proof is a *test of the filtrate* — a fresh drop of reagent must make no new cloud (P9: the reaction's own signal read as its endpoint). Book the excess in the wash plan.

**Choose the filter for the cake, and the cake for the filter.** Gravity for coarse fast clouds; the Büchner/vacuum bench for fine ones — N59's episode runs exactly this hardware (funnel + vacuum flask + hand pump, deionized rinse), and its honest failures are this lecture's own text: the filter paper tears on transfer, the crystals come off "gummy" — mechanical loss, the P12 ledger's first row, and the reason N58's 95% yield bookkeeping is a *culture* and not a formality. (Ch 5 owns the wash/settle/decant arithmetic; its wash-cost ledger is re-pinned here at the precipitation scale — cold, portioned, settle-between washes.)

**Wash without losing.** The mother liquor is not water — it carries the common ion, the excess reagent, the byproduct. Unwashed product is mislabeled product. The wash must not redissolve the cake (solvent saturated with the product is the lab-pro trick; here, *cold* and *portioned* is the household version) and must not peptize it (the colloid trap of Lecture 3 — if the wash water runs cloudy *through* the paper, you have made a colloid, not a wash; salt the wash lightly and re-settle).

**Pin the form, then weigh: the gravimetric rule.** "Which solid did you actually make?" is the question every yield claim silently answers. The model compound in this chapter's equations is CuCO₃ (M 123.554); the solid from a bicarbonate run is often a *basic* carbonate (malachite-family, M 221.114 for Cu₂(OH)₂CO₃ — the green of weathered bronze), and hydrates are always possible. Drying "to constant mass" is not a ritual: it is the only way the balance knows you are done; and before any yield is claimed, the form must be pinned (color, behavior on acid, and — at Atlas scope — the honest admission that polymorph/hydrate ID without instruments is inference from evidence, P9's convergence, never certainty). The same rule read at the grocery store is Ch 2's hydrate trap; read at the pharmacy it is why "magnesia" and "milk of magnesia" are different products.

**Weigh the answer: the gravimetric assay.** The instrumentless gold standard (N57's cross-assay culture): precipitate the ion completely, filter, wash, dry to constant mass, weigh — and the mass *is* the assay, because the ledger between the solid's formula and the parent solution is exact arithmetic. Lab 9.2's summit exercise runs it: a 1.0000 g retail root-killer sample precipitated as the carbonate, 0.4702 g dried solid — and the two calculation routes (mass route and mole route, agreeing to the digit) return 95.0% — the sample is 95.0% CuSO₄·5H₂O and retail filler the rest, and the recovery number is the exact class of N58's own 95% bookkeeping. This is the home lab's mass spectrometer: a balance, a filter, and this chapter's ledger.

**Solubility is safety.** The lecture closes where the contracts opened, because the property is protective: BaSO₄ is swallowed by the gram in X-ray studies *because* its Ksp is 10⁻¹⁰; barium's soluble salts are dangerous poisons *because* they dissolve; and N30's $3 pottery-shelf BaCO₃ is the rodenticide-grade hazard that must never meet acid (the CO₂ fizz that frees soluble barium is Lecture 1's driver running the wrong direction). The same argument prices every disposal decision: convert soluble heavy-metal waste to its insoluble carbonate, filter, and route the solid to hazardous collection per local rules — never the drain, never the pond. The anion decides the fate; the Ksp is the fence.
