# Vol I · Chapter 8 — The Household pH World

Chapter 7 opened the ledger of fire and priced it in enthalpies; this chapter opens the cabinet under the sink and the shelf above it, where acid–base chemistry lives at retail strength — vinegar and lemon, muriatic acid, household ammonia, bleach, baking soda and washing soda, antacids and aspirin, and the two drain openers that must never meet. **An acid and a base are not vibes of sour and slippery: they are bookkeeping categories — proton donors and proton acceptors — and every household bottle in this chapter can be priced in the same arithmetic.** The corpus supplies this chapter's practitioners whole: the Rooto drain-opener sulfuric assayed by titration *and* by gravimetric cross-assay (N57), the copper-compound session with its own yield bookkeeping self-correction (N58), the root-killer-and-baking-soda carbonate hub with its 219,000-L solubility datum (N59), the serial-dilution sensitivity ladder (N60), the Crystal drain-opener NaOH substitution (N66), the two-reagent cross-verification rule (N71), the turmeric/curcumin colorimetry with its 7-ppm ladder floor (N72), the Dragendorff episode with its quinine-in-tonic subject (N73), the salicylate visual colorimetry with its serial-halving ladder (N74), and the chlorine generator — pool pucks plus muriatic acid — whose explicit do-not-attempt disclaimer is this chapter's Lecture 5 in one video (N77).

The chapter's honest contracts, stated up front: **the Ka and Kb values are tabulated constants** (every one web-verified this session, and quoted as constants, never bench-measured here — the labs compute, they do not titrate the constants); **an indicator's color change is a band, not a point** — roughly pKa(indicator) ± 1, and titration discipline is the discipline of matching bands to endpoints; **strong-vs-weak and concentrated-vs-dilute are two different axes** — the household conflates them constantly, and this chapter's most-repeated computation exists to un-conflate them (a concentrated weak acid like vinegar is 216× gentler in hydronium than the same-molarity strong acid); and **the never-combinations are binding safety language, not homework** — bleach never meets acid, bleach never meets ammonia, the two drain openers never meet each other, and acid goes into water, never the reverse. The tasting rule runs through all of it: **nothing at the bench is ever tasted** — the food side of the kitchen is where taste may meet acid; the bench side never — and no food-grade identity makes a bench reagent food.

## Lecture 0 — The cabinet, before the theory

Sit at the kitchen table with the notebook and the household inventory list, and pour nothing yet — this is the pencil-and-paper lecture. Run the cabinet through the mind's eye: vinegar meeting baking soda in a bowl (the fizz that lifts the batter); vinegar dripped on the kettle's limescale (the same fizz, slower); the muriatic bottle's fume when the cap comes off (sharp, at arm's length, adult hands, goggles); the ammonia bottle's bite; bleach's smell, which is not a "base smell" at all but an oxidizer's; and the hardware-store shelf where the two drain openers sit side by side — one bottle stamped *acid*, the other stamped *caustic*, each carrying a label that forbids the other. The cabinet is not a chemistry set; it is a chemistry *archive*, and every bottle in it is a label to be read (P1, Chapter 2's OTC map reread through a pH lens).

The observations sort themselves. Some liquids make carbonates fizz (they push out CO₂ — the acid family). Some feel slippery and eat the fizz (they receive what the acids push — the base family). Some are dangerous in tiny amounts because they are *concentrated*; some never bite much at any strength because they are *weak* — and the two facts are different facts, which is the chapter's headline confusion, priced in Lecture 2. The kitchen's own sour map — lemon, vinegar, yogurt, the coffee's edge — is acid behavior met at the table, and the Atlas's tasting rule runs one way only: the food side may inform the chemist, the bench side never feeds the taster.

The chapter's four questions, asked at the cabinet and answered in arithmetic: **what is an acid, and what is a base?** — the proton ledger, Lecture 1; **how much acid is actually in the bottle?** — the equilibrium engine and the pH ladder, Lecture 2; **where does a neutralization actually end?** — indicators and the assay chain, Lectures 3 and 4; and **what must never meet what?** — the household pH map and the never-combinations, Lecture 5. ## Lecture 1 — From Arrhenius to Brønsted: the proton ledger

The first definition is Arrhenius's (1884): an acid releases H⁺ in water, a base releases OH⁻. It is honest as far as it goes — the cabinet's stars behave exactly so, HCl and NaOH splitting apart to the last ion — and then it meets the ammonia bottle, which contains no hydroxide anywhere on its label and yet cuts grease, neutralizes acid, and fumes like a base. The definition has to grow.

The grown definition is Brønsted–Lowry (1923): **an acid is a proton donor; a base is a proton acceptor.** The growth is small and it changes everything, because it makes every acid–base reaction a *transfer* — a proton moved from one species to another — and every transfer writes two ledger entries: the donor becomes its **conjugate base** (HA → A⁻), the acceptor becomes its **conjugate acid** (B → BH⁺). The cabinet's conjugate pairs, read straight off the bottles: (HCl, Cl⁻), (CH₃COOH, CH₃COO⁻), (NH₄⁺, NH₃) — ammonia *is* a base, and the Arrhenius problem dissolves: the OH⁻ appears after the transfer, not before. Carbonic acid is a pair of pairs — (H₂CO₃, HCO₃⁻) and (HCO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻) — the polyprotic face. And water appears on both sides of the ledger: (H₃O⁺, H₂O) and (H₂O, OH⁻). A species that can both donate and accept — water, bicarbonate — is **amphiprotic**, and Lecture 2 will compute exactly what that buys baking soda.

Hydronium honesty, stated once and carried through: the proton never swims free. In water, "H⁺" is H₃O⁺, and H₃O⁺ is itself a bookkeeping stand-in for protonated-water clusters (H₅O₂⁺, H₉O₄⁺) flickering through the hydrogen-bond network. The Atlas books H₃O⁺ and says so — the shorthand is exact for the ledger, approximate for the physics, and knowing which is which is part of the education (OpenStax 14.2 makes the same point in the same words).

Not all donors donate equally. The equilibrium HA + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + A⁻ has an equilibrium constant, and it is the donor's report card: **Ka = [H₃O⁺][A⁻]/[HA]** — big Ka, donates freely (strong acid); small Ka, holds its proton (weak acid); and **pKa = −log₁₀ Ka**, the same inversion the pH scale will make in Lecture 2: *small* pKa means *stronger* acid. This chapter's working constants, tabulated once and used everywhere ([convergent]; the derived entries [computed]):

| Equilibrium | Constant | pK | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ | Kw = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴ | 14.00 | the water ledger's floor |
| CH₃COOH ⇌ H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻ | Ka = 1.8×10⁻⁵ | 4.75 | vinegar's acid; reported pKa 4.75 |
| C₉H₈O₄ ⇌ H⁺ + C₉H₇O₄⁻ | Ka = 3.3×10⁻⁴ | 3.48 | aspirin; reported pKa 3.5 |
| H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ | Ka1 = 4.3×10⁻⁷ | 6.37 | carbonic, first proton |
| HCO₃⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + CO₃²⁻ | Ka2 = 4.7×10⁻¹¹ | 10.33 | carbonic, second proton |
| NH₄⁺ ⇌ H⁺ + NH₃ | Ka = 5.556×10⁻¹⁰ | 9.26 | [computed] Kw/Kb(NH₃) |
| NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ | Kb = 1.8×10⁻⁵ | 4.75 | ammonia's report card |
| CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + OH⁻ | Kb = 5.556×10⁻¹⁰ | 9.26 | [computed] the conjugate's mirror |
| CO₃²⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + OH⁻ | Kb = 2.128×10⁻⁴ | 3.67 | [computed] washing soda's engine |

The ledger's conservation law, visible in the table's [computed] rows: **for a conjugate pair, Ka × Kb = Kw** — one proton transfer, two entries, and the books balance every time (machine-shown in Lab 8.1 for all three pairs). A strong acid has a weak conjugate base and vice versa; vinegar's acid holds its proton (Ka 1.8×10⁻⁵), so acetate grabs one back only barely (Kb 5.556×10⁻¹⁰) — which is why Lecture 4's vinegar flask ends basic, but only *barely* basic, at its equivalence point.

And the definition the chapter will not let slip: **strong means dissociation essentially complete in water** (a statement about the solvent's leveling, paid in full at Chapter 24), and it is a different axis from concentration. A 0.010 M HCl solution is strong and gentle; household vinegar is, for a household acid, concentrated — and weak. Lecture 2 computes the cost of confusing the two, and the answer is a factor of 216.

## Lecture 2 — Kw, pH, and the two ladders

Water keeps its own books: [H₃O⁺][OH⁻] = Kw = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C. Define **pH = −log₁₀[H₃O⁺]** and **pOH = −log₁₀[OH⁻]**, and the ledger's floor becomes a scale: pH + pOH = 14.00, and pure water sits at the middle, 7.00. The scale's ends are bookkeeping conventions, not walls — and the first honest surprise of the lecture is that the household crosses both.

**The strong ladder.** A strong acid dissociates completely, so its pH is one division: 0.10 M HCl → pH 1.00; 0.10 M NaOH → pOH 1.00 → pH 13.00; and muriatic acid at the hardware-store grade Chapter 4 priced (31.45% w/w at ρ 1.160 → 10.0066 M) books **pH -1.00** — *below zero*, which is not a paradox but an honest entry: it says [H₃O⁺] > 1 M, nothing more. The bookkeeping does not stop at the scale's painted ends; it stops at the arithmetic's.

**The weak-acid engine.** A weak acid holds most of its protons: HA + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + A⁻, with x ionized out of an analytical concentration C, gives Ka = x²/(C − x). The **5% rule** is a test, not a formality: *after* the shortcut x ≈ √(Ka·C), check x < 5% of C — if the test passes, the shortcut's error is under a tenth of a percent; if it fails, run the quadratic. Worked, on vinegar, at Chapter 4's canonical basis (5% w/w at ρ 1.006 → 0.8376 M — the RA-1 discipline; this chapter's first engine run used a 5% w/v basis, produced 0.8326 M, and was re-derived on the book's basis — cross-chapter consistency is part of the verification ladder): the shortcut gives [H₃O⁺] = 3.883e-3 (0.46% ionized — the 5% test passes comfortably); the quadratic gives 3.874e-3 → **pH 2.41**, either way. Measured reports for 5% vinegar cluster at 2.42 [convergent] — the 0.01 gap is the activity-coefficient honesty flag: the engine is ideal, the bottle is real; the flag is named, not chased.

**The dilution fingerprint.** Dilute a weak acid tenfold and the square-root engine moves its pH by +0.50; dilute a strong acid tenfold and it moves +1.00. Compute it: acetic at 0.10 M → pH 2.87 (1.34% ionized); at 0.010 M → pH 3.37 (4.24% ionized — the 5% test's honest edge, and the quadratic's 3.38 is the correction printed with its shortcut). The fingerprint — **+0.50 per decade for weak, +1.00 for strong** — is P9's instrumentless strong/weak test, and Exercise 8.6 runs it on two unlabeled bottles.

**The aspirin showpiece** — the case that breaks the shortcut. One 325 mg tablet in 250 mL of water: C = 7.2158e-3 M. The shortcut gives x = 1.543e-3 = 21.4% ionized — the 5% test **fails**, and the quadratic is the only honest engine: x = 1.387e-3 → **pH 2.86**, 19.2% ionized. Cross-check the same answer a third way by rearranging the Henderson–Hasselbalch balance (Lecture 4's tool): pH = pKa + log(x/(C−x)) = 2.86 — two engines and a cross-check, one digit, P9's convergence discipline.

**The base side**, same engine, conjugate constants. Ammonia at 0.10 M: Kb 1.8×10⁻⁵ → [OH⁻] = 1.342×10⁻³ (1.34% — the mirror of acetic's number, because their constants are equal) → **pH 11.13**. Household ammonia (5% w/w, ρ ≈ 0.98 → 2.88 M): → **pH 11.86** — the web-reported household band 11–12 [convergent] catches it. Washing soda: carbonate is the conjugate base of a weak acid's *second* step, Kb = Kw/Ka2 = 2.128e-4 → at 0.10 M, [OH⁻] = 4.613e-3 (4.61% — the 5% edge again, printed with its engine) → **pH 11.66**, and the web-reported value for 0.1 M sodium carbonate is 11.66 [convergent] — the ideal model landing on the measured digit. Baking soda: bicarbonate is **amphiprotic**, donating through Ka2 and accepting through Ka1, and the two equilibria bracket it: **pH = (pKa1 + pKa2)/2 = (6.367 + 10.328)/2 = 8.35** (the web band 8.3–8.4 [convergent]; the derivation of the mean formula belongs to Chapter 24, which owns amphiprotic arithmetic in full).

**The two-axis lesson, priced.** Suppose the vinegar bottle's acid had been strong: 0.8376 M of a fully dissociating acid books pH 0.08. The real vinegar books 2.41. The gap is 2.33 pH units — a factor of **216** in hydronium concentration. Concentration and strength are different axes, and every titration in the next chapter of your education depends on never confusing them: the assay of Lecture 4 works *because* the analyst knows which axis each bottle lives on.

## Lecture 3 — Indicators: bands, not points

The household's instrumentless pH meter (P9's home game): an indicator is itself a weak acid — HIn ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻ — whose two forms wear different colors. The color you see reports the ratio [In⁻]/[HIn], and that ratio swings through roughly pKa(In) ± 1: **a band, not a point**. This is the first honest contract of titration work, and the bench set (all [convergent]) is three bands wide: **methyl orange 3.1–4.4** (red → yellow), **methyl red 4.4–6.2** (red → yellow), **phenolphthalein 8.2–10.0** (colorless → pink).

The kitchen's own indicators, used honestly. **Turmeric**: curcumin on paper turns red-brown above about 7.4–8.6 [convergent] — the pantry's phenolphthalein-class tool. The corpus's own episode (N72) is the honesty lesson here: its rosocyanine chemistry is *boron-specific* colorimetry — the 1000-ppm borax standard ladder run down to a 7-ppm detection floor [single-source] is a boron datum, not an acid–base one — and the same yellow root wears two different chemistry hats, which the Atlas keeps labeled. **Red cabbage**: the anthocyanin ladder runs red → purple → blue → green → yellow across the whole scale — canon bench practice in every home-chemistry text of the last century, and labeled here exactly that way: **canon, not corpus** (no face in the survey register; used, priced, never laundered into a citation). **Quinine** (N73): tonic water fluoresces under a cheap UV LED and the fluorescence reads the pH — a demonstration, not a quantitative indicator (the episode's 83-ppm quinine subject is Dragendorff-reagent territory, [single-source]).

The ladder discipline, straight from the corpus's method: N60's serial-dilution sensitivity ladder, N74's serial-halving standard curve (150 → 9.5 mg/dL), N72's 1000 ppm → 7 ppm — the working habit is the same in all three: *when a reading matters, you do not trust one spot; you build a ladder of knowns and read the rung where the answer changes.* Indicator work is that discipline in a test tube: bracket the endpoint with knowns, then read the unknown.

**Choosing the indicator is endpoint arithmetic, not aesthetics**: the band must bracket the expected equivalence pH — Lecture 4's battery makes this exact, and the chapter's assay chain chooses methyl orange for one endpoint, phenolphthalein for another, and methyl red for a third, each by the band that brackets the computed equivalence, never by the prettiest color. And the **two-reagent rule** (N71, the corpus's own cross-verification discipline): one reagent's word is never enough — confirm by a second, independent method. The gold standard in the register is N57's Rooto assay: the drain-opener's acid content closed by *titration* and cross-checked by a *gravimetric* byproduct assay (the Na₂SO₄ saved and weighed) — two independent ledgers, one answer. Lab 8.2's standardization chain borrows exactly that discipline: every concentration in the chain is anchored to a weighed solid, and the final assay closes on the bottle's own label.

One boundary, restated for the indicator bench: papers and solutions are reagents. Turmeric paper is a spice until it enters the bench jar, and a reagent after; phenolphthalein's history as a laxative is a chemistry-curiosity datum, not an invitation; and nothing bench-side is ever tasted — the tasting rule of the intro, standing at the indicator bench where it is most tempting to break it.

## Lecture 4 — Neutralization and the salt ledger

The neutralization equation is a bill of materials like any other: acid + base → salt + water (and CO₂, when the base is a carbonate). What makes acid–base bookkeeping special is that the *salt* — the flask's content at equivalence — has a pH personality of its own, inherited from its parents' strengths: NaCl is neutral (7.00); acetate is *barely* basic (its Kb is acetic's mirror); ammonium is *barely* acidic (its Ka is ammonia's mirror). Salts are not spectators, and Chapter 24 owns that theory in full — this chapter books the numbers and forward-flags the derivation.

**Vinegar + baking soda, the full ledger** (Lab 8.2's headliner, run at open-bench scale): 500 mL of vinegar at 0.8376 M holds 418.8 mmol of acid; 14.0 g of soda is 166.7 mmol — the soda is limiting (the acid would need 35.2 g of it). Products: CO₂ 7.334 g = 4.08 L at 24.5 L/mol (never in a sealed vessel — Chapter 4's never-latch rule, restated at the fizz), water, sodium acetate — and *leftover acid*: 252.15 mmol of acetic beside 166.7 mmol of acetate. That pair is an **accidental buffer**, and its pH is the Henderson–Hasselbalch ledger's one-line identity: **pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]) = 4.56**. The striking property is what the equation says by construction: the pH depends only on the *ratio*, not the volume — dilute the bowl tenfold and the pH holds (the solution is ~504 mL of ~0.50 M acid + 0.33 M acetate; the pH does not care). Chapter 24 owns buffers; this chapter simply cannot avoid meeting one, because half-neutralization *is* one.

**The antacid row.** Calcium carbonate meets hydrochloric acid two-for-one: CaCO₃ + 2 HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂. A 500 mg tablet is 4.996 mmol of carbonate → 9.99 mmol of acid neutralized — 99.9% of the model stomach (100 mL of 0.10 M HCl, 10.00 mmol; the real stomach's band is 1–3 [convergent]), with 0.220 g of CO₂ as the burp. The honest remainder — 9.99 vs 10.00 mmol — is not noise to be rounded away; the never-round-intermediates discipline keeps the books true (Chapter 1's lesson, repriced in moles).

**The standardization chain — why an assay needs a foundation.** You cannot titrate vinegar against a bottle labeled "NaOH" and call the result a measurement: the bottle's concentration is itself unverified until someone anchors it. The chain runs from a **primary standard** — anhydrous Na₂CO₃, oven-dried to constant mass (Chapter 5's discipline; Chapter 9's carbonate hub supplies it): 0.2500 g = 2.3588 mmol = 4.718 mmol of HCl demand; the titration consumes 47.18 mL → **M(HCl) = 0.09999 ≈ 0.1000**; the equivalence flask holds carbonic acid at pH 3.93 → **methyl orange** (3.1–4.4 brackets it). Then NaOH by parity against the standardized HCl — titrated to the *same* phenolphthalein endpoint the assays will use, so the band's bias cancels to first order (the indicator-coupled trick: standardization and assay share one endpoint, and the shared error walks out of the subtraction). Only now is anything else assayable.

**The vinegar assay, closing on the label.** Dilute the vinegar 1:10 (Chapter 4's dilution discipline); take a 25.00 mL aliquot — 2.094 mmol of acetic; titrate with the 0.1000 M NaOH to V(eq) = 20.94 mL, endpoint pH 8.70 → **phenolphthalein** (8.2–10.0 brackets it); back-calculate: 2.094 × 10 / 25.00 mL = **0.8376 M = 50.30 g/L = 5.00% w/w at ρ 1.006** — and the bottle's label ("diluted to 5% acidity") closes within the assay's honest error. **The label check is the assay's proof**: when a measurement and a manufacturer's declaration agree, both ledgers earned it — N57's cross-assay discipline, run all the way to the shelf (P9's convergence at its cleanest).

**The ammonia summit** — a weak base meets a strong acid, and the equivalence flask tells the truth about salts. Household ammonia at 2.88 M, diluted 1:20 → 0.144 M; a 25.00 mL aliquot is 3.596 mmol → V(eq) = 35.96 mL of 0.1000 M HCl. At equivalence the flask holds no ammonia — it holds **ammonium**, 0.0590 M of a weak acid (Ka 5.556×10⁻¹⁰): [H₃O⁺] = 5.725e-6 → **pH 5.24** → **methyl red** (4.4–6.2 brackets it). A weak base's titration ends *acidic*, and the analyst who reached for phenolphthalein by habit would have rung the wrong bell.

**The equivalence battery** — the chapter's indicator honesty in four rows (Lab 8.2 runs each):

| Titration (to equivalence) | The flask holds | pH | The honest indicator |
|---|---|---|---|
| strong acid + strong base (equal volumes) | NaCl, a spectator | 7.00 | any near-neutral band |
| acetic + NaOH | 0.050 M acetate — barely basic (Kb 5.556×10⁻¹⁰, [OH⁻] 5.270e-6) | 8.72 | phenolphthalein (8.2–10.0) |
| NH₃ + HCl | 0.050 M ammonium — barely acidic | 5.28 | methyl red (4.4–6.2) |
| Na₂CO₃ + HCl (to carbonic) | ~0.033 M H₂CO₃ | 3.93 | methyl orange (3.1–4.4) |

The endpoint is where the *stoichiometry* completed — the indicator is chosen to bracket that arithmetic, never to flatter the eye. Every row of the battery is a computed number before it is a color.

The map spans the whole aqueous scale, and the dangerous part of the map is not the ends — it is the *meetings*. **The never-combinations are binding safety language**, and each row is chemistry, not caution-label boilerplate:

**Bleach never meets acid.** Hypochlorite plus acid displaces chlorine: the pool-puck generators run exactly this chemistry *deliberately* — TCCA plus muriatic acid, gas-drying train, scrubbing, and an explicit do-not-attempt disclaimer (N77) — and the household's "just a splash while cleaning" runs it with none of the apparatus. Chlorine gas is the product; recognition, avoidance, and response knowledge belong to Chapter 28, the chemistry to Chapters 15/17, and the Atlas's bench has no version of it at any scale.

**Bleach never meets ammonia.** The products are chloramines — toxic gases one shelf over from harmless cleaning. The "extra-strength cleaning" instinct is the failure mode; there is no safe proportion, and the same rule covers bleach-plus-anything-nitrogen-containing.

**The two drain openers never meet — and neither meets the other's residue.** The Rooto-class bottle (sulfuric, N57) and the Crystal-class bottle (NaOH, N66) are an acid and a base at industrial strength: their meeting is a violent exothermic neutralization with boiling spatter, in a pipe, in the dark. A second product goes in only after a full flush and an honest re-assessment — and never on the same day's impulse.

**Acid into water, always** — Chapter 4's rule restated at strength: the dilution of anything this chapter labels strong is exothermic, and the order of pouring decides whether the heat has mass to absorb it.

**Fume discipline.** Muriatic and household ammonia both fume at the bench: ventilate, work at arm's length, goggles — and the Charter's rule that smell is never a test method (waft, if at all, and only when a procedure genuinely requires it).

**The fizz is a pressure source.** Vinegar-and-soda chemistry in a closed vessel is Chapter 4's never-latch rule with a household face: CO₂ has nowhere to go, and the vessel chooses where.

And the boundary that closes every chapter of this Atlas: **the bench/food line**. Food-grade identity never makes a bench reagent food — the vinegar that assays at 5% w/w is the pantry's bottle until it enters the titration flask, and after that it is a reagent, labeled and shelved as one (Chapter 2's relabel rule, restated at the cabinet).

**Handoffs, forward-flagged by name.** The carbonate hub — root killer plus baking soda, N59's pairing — is Chapter 9's opening move, and the standardization chain's Na₂CO₃ is its first customer. The accidental buffer, the amphiprotic mean, and salt hydrolysis are Chapter 24's, previewed here as computed numbers. Ester hydrolysis — the fats-and-soaps ledger — runs on this chapter's grammar and is Chapter 23's to price. The chlorine chemistry behind Lecture 5's first never-combination belongs to Chapters 15 and 17, with its recognition face in Chapter 28. And P14's forward flag: precipitation-driven acid liberation is, at heart, *this chapter's proton ledger pulled by an insoluble byproduct* — the pattern's grammar is acid–base even when its payload is a mineral acid, and it fires from this page.

**8.2 ●○○ — The strong impostor.** Suppose a rival label claimed vinegar's acid was fully strong. (a) Compute the pH of a 0.8376 M *strong* acid. (b) Compute the real vinegar's pH (the quadratic). **8.5 ●●● — The assay chain (chapter summit).** Run Lab 8.2's chain end to end *as the analyst who must defend every number*: from the dried primary standard to the vinegar bottle's label, choose the indicator at each endpoint with your reason, and state every place the chain could lie to you, with the size of each lie. *Hints: the chain is only as good as its weakest reference; the drop volume is a datum from Chapter 1.* **Answer:** the chain: 0.2500 g Na₂CO₃ → 2.3588 mmol → 4.718 mmol HCl demand → 47.18 mL → M(HCl) = 0.09999 ≈ 0.1000 M, endpoint 3.93 → **methyl orange** (the band brackets carbonic's equivalence); NaOH by parity to the shared phenolphthalein endpoint (the band-bias cancellation); the assay: 2.094 mmol in the aliquot → V(eq) 20.94 mL → 0.8376 M = 50.30 g/L = **5.00% w/w** — the label closes. **8.6 ◆ — The two unknown bottles (no hints; the bench diagnoses, then decides).** Two unlabeled clear liquids, A and B, both sour to a taste that is *never taken* (the label says "acid" how? — say what you would actually run), both roughly 0.1 M in acid by an initial neutralization. **8.7 ◐ — The household pH census (open-ended).** Take the indicator set and turmeric paper on a census of the house: at least twelve items, each assigned a band, an estimated pH, and a class (acid / base / salt-of-which-parent / neither).
