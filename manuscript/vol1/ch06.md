# Vol I · Chapter 6 — Separation Train II: Distillation

Chapter 5 ended where the mechanical and evaporative rungs run out of differences: two dissolved liquids, mixed down to the molecular scale, immune to gravity and to holes. This chapter takes the difference they cannot hide — **volatility** — and builds the machine that exploits it: the still. The premise's discipline applies to it exactly as it applied to the settling jar and the filter paper: **distillation is a machine built on a vapor-pressure difference — the more volatile component leaves first, and the still is just the plumbing that lets it leave and then changes its mind.** The rung is P2's most expensive and most powerful one, and the corpus supplies its practitioners' faces whole: drain-cleaner sulfuric distilled to reagent grade on a DIY heating mantle (N02), the same run rebuilt with a 540 °C ceramic hotplate and PTFE threading-tape joint seals (N82), hardware-store hydrochloric acid distilled against its azeotrope cap (N83, with N46 the Home Scientist's version of the same trade), and the azeotrope turned from a wall into a worker — the Dean-Stark dryer (N87) and the vacuum drying pistol (N97).

The chapter's honest contracts, stated up front: **the vapor-pressure arithmetic runs on empirical Antoine fits with stated temperature ranges, and the fits' own slope errors are printed where they matter** (a fit is honest only at what it earns); **the ethanol-water ideal mixture model cannot produce the azeotrope it is famous for** — the model is a shape argument, the azeotrope a datum, and Lab 6.2 shows the model sailing past the real wall while reality stalls at it, the same honest-seam discipline as Stokes' Re = 0.2 boundary in Chapter 5 and the Ksp ideal battery in Chapter 4; and **the sulfuric-acid rungs are corpus knowledge, not bench prescriptions** — the Charter's kitchen ceiling and the acid's 337 °C boiling point agree that the Atlas computes that wall and does not climb it at the bench.

## Lecture 0 — The lid and the window (before any notation)

Put a pot of water on the stove and watch the lid rattle. Take the lid off and hold a cold plate above the pot: it fogs, and then it drips. You have just built a still, and your kitchen builds one every time it boils — the question is only whether the water that leaves is *lost to the room* or *caught on the way*. Draw it pencil-and-paper: the pot (where the liquid is), the space above the pot (where the vapor goes), the cold plate (where the vapor becomes liquid again). Three stations, one journey, and the whole chapter is the engineering of that journey.

The lid rattles because the vapor pushes: even below the boil, the surface of any liquid leaks molecules into the air — a glass of water left open disappears over days (Chapter 5's evaporative rung was this same leak, unsupervised). The rattle is the leak turning into a shove: at the boil the leak's pressure reaches the atmosphere's pressure, and bubbles form *in the body of the liquid*, not just at the surface.

And the window tells the third fact for free: the fog on the kitchen window over a boiling pot is **distillate** — water that left, traveled as vapor, and condensed where it met cold. The pot's mineral ring (Chapter 4's limescale) is what the water left *behind*: the still's two products, residue and distillate, in every kitchen that has ever boiled water. Add the fourth jar to Chapter 5's three: two liquids mixed to invisibility — jar four — the mixture that gravity and holes cannot sort. But if one of them leaves as vapor more eagerly than the other, the journey through the lid sorts them after all. That is the whole chapter, and everything after this lecture is bookkeeping for it.

## Lecture 1 — Vapor pressure: why liquids leave, and when they boil

The leak at the surface has a pressure: the **vapor pressure** of the liquid, the push of its own escaping molecules, and it climbs with temperature — slowly at first, then steeply. The climb is captured empirically by the **Antoine equation**, log₁₀ P = A − B/(C + T), a fitted curve with three constants per liquid and a *stated temperature range* that is part of the data, not a footnote: water's low branch holds 1–100 °C and a second branch takes over from 99 °C; ethanol's fit ends at 78 °C, and this chapter prints what happens when a chain needs it past that ceiling (the extrapolation is flagged, and the flag is honest — Lab 6.1 shows the fit's slope error growing exactly at its range edges).

**Boiling is not a temperature of the liquid; it is a match of pressures**: the liquid boils when its vapor pressure reaches the pressure of the atmosphere above it. At sea level water boils at 100 °C because its vapor pressure *is* 760 mmHg there — the temperature is the atmosphere's choice, not the water's. Change the atmosphere and the boiling point moves, and the moves are not small:

- At 255 mmHg — the pressure on Everest's summit, about a third of sea level — water boils at ~72 °C (Lab 6.1's bisection; the tea never does get properly hot).
- In the pressure cooker at 15 psig — 2.02 atmospheres — water boils at 121 °C (Lab 6.1, branch 2 of the fit), which is the entire point of the cooker: the atmosphere the lid builds makes the food's chemistry run faster.
- At 100 mmHg — a modest hand-pumped vacuum — water boils at 52 °C: the drying-pistol's lever (N97), boiling a liquid *cool* by taking the atmosphere away from it instead of adding heat.

The energy ledger: boiling is paid for in **latent heat** — water costs 40.65 kJ/mol to vaporize at its boil (2.26 MJ/kg), ethanol only 38.6 kJ/mol (0.84 MJ/kg — a third of water's per kilogram). The still's fuel line (P10) is written directly in those numbers: distilling water-rich mixtures means shipping megajoules to make vapor, which is why Chapter 5's cheap rungs come first and the still is the expensive, precise, *last* rung of the mechanical half of the train.

The honest label, stated where it belongs: the Antoine fits are empirical curves, not laws. Their *slopes* — the quantities the Clausius-Clapeyron equation turns into latent heats — carry the fit's own errors, and the errors grow at the ranges' edges: the water branch is about 2% steep at 100 °C, the ethanol fit about 6% steep at its 78 °C edge. Lab 6.1 prints these gaps deliberately, because a fit trusted past what it earns is how numbers go wrong quietly.

## Lecture 2 — The still: apparatus improvisation's flagship (P11)

The still is Chapter 1's glassware hierarchy and Chapter 5's filtration discipline assembled into one machine, and its home-lab form is older than the glassware: a boiler, a head that carries vapor up and over, a condenser that cools it back to liquid, and a receiver that catches the condensate. The premise's version (P11) rebuilds each station from the kitchen: a kettle or saucepan as the boiler, tubing as the head, a coil of tubing in a bucket of cold water as the condenser (or a metal bowl of ice over the pot, Lecture 0's cold plate made useful), a jar as the receiver. Thompson's curriculum gives the store-bought form — the proper distilling flask, the Liebig condenser with its counter-current water jacket — and the make-or-buy verdict is Chapter 2's: the improvised still works with a stated accuracy penalty, and the penalty is mostly *control*, not chemistry.

Three bench disciplines make the still honest:

1. **Never sealed.** A still must have an open pressure path from boiler to receiver, always. A closed vessel over evolving vapor is not a still; it is a bomb with a schedule (the Charter's line, and the row this chapter's safety table exists to enforce). The kettle's whistle is a pressure relief doing its job; your still needs the same courtesy, built in before the heat goes on.
2. **Cold water first.** The condenser's water runs before the burner lights — a dry condenser means vapor meeting room air, and for a flammable vapor that is a fire looking for its ignition source (the safety table's row 2). Counter-current flow (cold water entering at the receiver end) keeps the coldest surface where the last vapor needs it.
3. **Cuts, not a single stream.** The receiver is changed by temperature and by sequence: the first fraction (the "foreshots" — the most volatile tail of everything that was ever in the boiler, including the fingernail of solvent smell in a reused pot) is discarded or kept aside by rule; the middle fraction is the product's heart; the late fraction is the pot running out of the volatile and starting to carry water. The thermometer at the head is the still's own notebook: a **cut** is taken by temperature reading, and the pot temperature's slow climb tells the operator what is leaving. The notebook habit is Chapter 1's six-line entry with a column added: head temperature, and fraction number.

The corpus's own faces for this lecture are the acid stills: NurdRage's drain-cleaner sulfuric distilled to reagent grade over a DIY heating mantle (N02), rebuilt later with a 540 °C ceramic hotplate and PTFE threading-tape joint seals — the seized-joint honest failure recorded with the fix (N82); the Scrap Science hardware-store hydrochloric run (N83) and The Home Scientist's purify-the-cheap-gallon (N46) — the same still pointed at the same cap; and Thy Labs' drying pistol (N97), the still's vacuum-cousin. The acid faces carry the chapter's boundary lesson, stated now: **the acid runs are corpus knowledge, not bench prescriptions** — the Charter's kitchen ceiling stops where concentrated hot acids begin, and the Atlas reads those runs the way it reads a thermite demonstration: as verified knowledge of what the tool does, executed by practitioners with the right gear, priced and understood from the armchair.

## Lecture 3 — Mixtures: which vapor leaves, and by how much

For a pure liquid, vapor pressure is a property of the substance. For a *mixture*, each component pushes its own share — **Raoult's law**: the partial pressure of each component is its mole fraction times its pure vapor pressure, P_i = x_i · P_i*. Add them and you get the bubble point of the ideal mixture, and the share of each in the vapor, y_i = x_i P_i*/P_total. One line of algebra, and the whole separation in it: **the more volatile component is a bigger fraction of the vapor than of the liquid** — y > x for the eager leaver, always, in the ideal model. The still is the machine that repeats that one inequality: vaporize, condense, vaporize again — each cycle the liquid's composition walks one step toward the laggard and the distillate walks one step toward the leaver.

Lab 6.1 runs the ideal ethanol-water chain at 760 mmHg and it reads like the still's specification: a liquid at 10 mol% ethanol boils at 96.8 °C and its first vapor is 20 mol%; a liquid at 30 mol% boils at 91.3 °C and its vapor is 49 mol%. Every liquid-vapor handshake multiplies the leaver's share — the machine's gain per handshake, and the reason a *fractional* column (a tall tube packed with surface area — glass beads, scrubber copper, marbles) beats a pot still: the column is many small handshakes stacked, so one slow pass through it does the work of many pot passes. The count of handshakes is the **theoretical plate** count — a model concept, flagged as one (a real column's packing is messy hydrodynamics, not clean stages) — and Lab 6.2 runs the ideal plate ladder from fermentation strength to the wall in twelve printed steps: 23.2 wt% after one plate, 60.4 after three, 94.7 after six. The pot still, by contrast, gets roughly *one* plate per pass — Lab 6.2's three passes (20.1 → 32.2 → 47.5 wt% early cuts) show why the column earned its place in every serious lab.

The honest flags on the model, stated in place: the real ethanol-water pair **deviates positively** from Raoult — its molecules prefer their own company less than the ideal model assumes, its total vapor pressure runs higher than predicted, and its vapor is *richer* in ethanol at every step than the ideal chain says (the real pot still's early cut beats the ideal 20.1 wt% comfortably). The same deviation is exactly what builds the azeotrope — the next lecture's wall — so the ideal chain is doubly honest here: a conservative *lower bound* on enrichment (it under-promises the cuts) and a false *upper bound* on the destination (it has no ceiling at all where reality stops dead). A model that errs in both directions, with both errors named, is the Atlas's standard instrument.

## Lecture 4 — The walls: azeotropes, the train's honest boundaries

Climb the plate ladder far enough, the naive story says, and any mixture separates completely. Reality has two walls, and both are called **azeotropes**: mixture compositions where liquid and vapor are the *same* — the handshake hands back exactly what it was given, y = x — so no number of plates moves the composition at all. At a wall, the still's one inequality fails, and the failure is a datum, not a derivable surprise: Lab 6.2 shows the ideal model *sailing past* the real ethanol-water wall (x = 0.89465) between plates 6 and 7 without a flicker, while the real still stalls at it forever. **The model cannot build the wall; the wall is measured.**

- **The ethanol-water wall (minimum-boiling):** 95.6 wt% ethanol boils at 78.2 °C — *below* both pure components (ethanol's own 78.4 °C; the azeotrope is the pair's most eager leaver as a team). The spirits still stops there; "absolute" ethanol past the wall is made by *chemistry* (drying agents that break the handshake — Chapter 15's territory), not by more plates.
- **The HCl-water wall (maximum-boiling):** hydrochloric acid boils at its *highest* at 20.2% — 108.6 °C, above water's own 100 °C — and the cap works in reverse: distill hardware-store 32% muriatic and the *distillate* comes over pinned near 20.2% while the *pot* climbs richer (Lab 6.2's cuts: the pot passes 35, 40, 44, 50% as the clean distillate keeps coming off at the cap). N83's honest datum is the tradeoff itself: the clean fraction is the distillate, capped at ~20% and never better; the pot's climb buys concentration but carries what the acid carried. (Chapter 4 met this wall as a ceiling on label arithmetic; here it is the machine.)
- **The H2SO4-water wall (maximum-boiling):** sulfuric acid's ceiling is 98.3% at ~337 °C — the corpus's own datum (N02's drain-cleaner run; N82's rebuild). The concentration ladder (P6) rides this wall: boil battery acid and water leaves until the pot *is* the azeotrope (Lab 6.2: from 35% acid, 64.4 g of water out of 100 g brings the pot to 98.3%); past the wall the still *delivers* the azeotrope itself — NurdRage's run is exactly that, distilling at ~337 °C to *collect* 98.3% acid — and past *that* is drying-agent chemistry (Ch 15). The 337 °C number is also the chapter's boundary marker: past the kitchen ceiling, corpus knowledge only.
- **The azeotrope as worker, not wall (N87):** the Dean-Stark dryer *uses* the azeotrope. The drying entrainer carries water out of a pot as a two-phase distillate — the water trapped in the trap, weighed against theory (the corpus's own bookkeeping: 17.1 mL theoretical against 16.75 g collected, the honest-excess discussion). The wall that stops a separation is, with the right plumbing, a pump: the Atlas's recurring lesson that a boundary understood is a boundary *usable*.
- **Steam distillation (named, promised):** two *immiscible* liquids each push their own full vapor pressure — the mixture boils below either pure component, and the volatile oils ride out with the steam. The trick that distills what would otherwise decompose: named here, run at the bench when its reagent appears.

## Lecture 5 — The ledger extended: when to pay for the still

The still is the train's expensive rung and it is priced like one. The fuel line first (P10): every gram of distillate is paid for in latent heat — water at 2.26 MJ/kg, ethanol at 0.84 — and a water-rich charge spends most of its fuel moving *water*, the component you did not want. Chapter 5's cheap rungs come first precisely so that the still is only ever asked to separate what nothing cheaper could: settle, decant, filter, crystallize first; distill the remainder. The cuts ledger is the still's P12 column: fraction number, head temperature, mass, and the cumulative product mass — the same fractions-multiply honesty as Chapter 5's train ledger, now with a thermometer in the bookkeeping.

**When to distill is decided by the next step (P10's rule, unchanged):** the pot still's 20 wt% early cut was *good enough* for the end-use that needed "some ethanol, mostly ethanol"; the column and its many plates are bought only when the end-use demands the wall's neighborhood; and past the wall, when the end-use truly needs absolute dryness, the handoff is honest and forward: drying agents and chemistry (Chapter 15), not more plates. N62's good-enough rule from Chapter 5 is the same decision wearing different glass.

**And the train's honest endings, extended:** distillation cannot cross an azeotrope (the wall is a datum); it cannot split two components of nearly equal volatility (the handshake hands back nearly nothing per stage — the fractional column grows toward absurdity, and the resolution is Chapter 13's chromatography, a thousand tiny differences instead of one big one, or Chapter 9's chemistry — *change the substance so the difference exists*: the P14 precipitation engines of the corpus turn an unstillable salt problem into a filterable one). Naming what the rung cannot do is the last lecture's habit, and it is why the Atlas's separation chapters end in handoffs, not in silence.
