# Vol V · Chapter 36 — The Tech Tree: Which Shelf Feeds Which

*Every reagent on the home shelf was once another reagent. Vinegar was sugar; the sugar was CO2 and water and sunlight nobody had to pay for; the drain cleaner in the bottle next to it is the vinegar's own salt, run in reverse. This chapter — the capstone of Volume V, and the last chapter of the Atlas — stops adding nodes and instead reads the whole book at once: thirty-five chapters of kitchen chemistry, redrawn as a single graph of feeds and products. Which shelf feeds which. Where the trunk is. What breaks if one bottle goes empty. The Tech Tree.*

**Chapter status: drafted 2026-10-19 (session 105, parts 1–2). Engine 25 (the call-stack descent evaluator) run 4 verified 60/60. Zero new masses printed (session-100 line). Awaiting the session-106 adversarial review; not yet canon.**

## Lecture 0 — The one-page map

Draw it yourself; it costs one sheet and a pencil.

Across the top of the page, write the trunk: **C6H12O6 -> C2H5OH -> CH3COOH -> CH3COONa**, with **CO2** hanging off the first arrow and **NaOH** feeding the last. Under the trunk, on the left, add the Haber-fed loop: **CO2 + 2 NH3 -> CO(NH2)2** (with water on the product side), and its reverse edge — read the corridor both ways, because the tree is not a one-way street. On the right, add the seam node: **CH3COONa.3H2O**, the trihydrate, wired between the salt and the water bottle. In the lower right corner, add the binder wing's gateway: **C4H6O2 + NaOH -> C2H4O + CH3COONa** — vinyl acetate walked straight into the trunk's hub. In the lower left, the polymer pair: **C6H7NO2 — C6H10O5**, connected to each other and to nothing else. And floating alone, the acetone island: **C3H6O**, pinned since Chapter 21, edgeless on this map — no certified route in this chapter's battery touches it.

That is the whole map: **fifteen nodes, twelve certified edges, three components** — the trunk, the polymer pair, and the acetone island. Every node's mass was pinned in an earlier chapter; every edge is certified by a balance row in this chapter's battery (Lab 36.1, rows E1–E8). The one edge the map refuses to draw is the slipped one: row E9 is a deliberately wrong fermentation written to prove the gate still bites — it is caught, and excluded.

The point of drawing it by hand is the point of the whole Atlas: the map is a supermarket receipt turned sideways. Nothing on it is exotic. The tree is what the kitchen already knew.

## Lecture 1 — Nodes, edges, depth

The vocabulary is small. A **node** is a species with a held mass — fifteen of them on this map. An **edge** is a certified feed: a balance row that turns some set of nodes into some other set. **Depth** is the longest chain of edges from any raw input to any final product; on this map the trunk is the deep path, and it is four nodes long (glucose -> ethanol -> acetic acid -> sodium acetate), with the seam and the binder wing hanging off its ends.

The Atlas has drawn pieces of this graph before, and this chapter owes both of them an ancestor credit. Chapter 20 drew the **B6 cycle map** — the first time the book closed a loop instead of a line. Chapter 21 drew the **network map** — twenty-two ethanol appearances reconciled into one node that many routes pass through. What those chapters drew as local sketches, this chapter draws as the whole book. The P-index labels ride along as edge annotations: each edge carries the pattern number of the route it instantiates (P10 for the economics reading, P12 for the cumulative-yield reading, P14 and P15 for the network placements — Lecture 2).

One reading rule, borrowed from the review chapters: the map is only as good as its certifications. An edge exists on this map if and only if a balance row in this chapter's battery certifies it at displayed precision, and a node exists if and only if its mass is pinned in a prior chapter's page. Nothing else gets drawn. That is why the acetone island is an island — the pin survives, but no route in this battery certifies an edge for it, so no edge is drawn. Honest maps leave islands visible.

## Lecture 2 — The trunk: routes, pins, and the two P-index homes

The trunk carries the Atlas's oldest traffic.

## Lecture 3 — Route economics: criteria, cumulative yields, and the time axis

**The four criteria (P10).** To judge a route you need four numbers, not one: the yield, the cost of the feeds, the number of steps, and the waste per pass. The Leblanc-versus-Solvay history is the canonical example — the Leblanc route turned salt and sulfuric acid into soda ash through two dirty steps at a cost that only made sense while the by-product hydrochloric and sulfur waste could be dumped for free; the Solvay route replaced it not by being chemically more elegant but by winning on the same four criteria — cheaper feeds, fewer steps, cleaner waste [convergent]. The Atlas's version of the same judgment runs on the trunk: the vinegar corridor (B1) costs one alkali addition and wastes one water; the fertilizer loop (B3) costs two industrial gases the kitchen cannot make; the binder gateway (E6) costs the drain cleaner to buy back a salt.

**Cumulative yields (P12).** A route is only as good as the product of its step yields. If fermentation passes 0.98 and the work-up passes 0.95, the route delivers 0.98 x 0.95 = 0.9310 — and the salt branch, run in the other order (work-up first, 0.95, then fermentation, 0.98), delivers 0.95 x 0.98 = the same 0.9310. That is row B4, and it is exact: both routes converge at displayed precision with no rounding gap, which is the session-104 discipline in its cleanest form — convergence claims are evaluated at displayed precision, and here the claim is exact. The engine then applies the factor honestly: the theoretical 6.830247 g of sodium acetate from the B1 corridor lands as 6.830247 x 0.9310 = 6.358960 g (B5), and the same factor walks the fermentation and fertilizer corridors down from their theoretical figures (X5).

**The time axis.** A tree that only knows feeds and products is missing the axis Chapter 25 flagged on its way out: *which reagents keep, which decay, and which to date on the label.* The pins stand as Chapter 25 printed them [convergent to Ch 25's page]: bleach at 0.97 monthly retention is 0.97^12 = 0.6938 over a year — a first-order decay with k = 0.030459 per month and a half-life of 22.76 months; the peroxide class decays at 0.5–2% per year — half-lives of 34.7 to 138.6 years. Read on the tree, the time axis says: the trunk nodes are keepers (salts and sugars do not decay on the kitchen scale), and the leaf nodes that carry reactive shelf space are the ones to date. The tree's edges have yields; its nodes have clocks.

## Lecture 5 — Reading the tree

**Bootstrap order.** If you had to rebuild the kitchen shelf from nothing, the tree tells you the order: water and a heat source first (no edges needed); then the trunk from the top down — sugar, then fermentation's ethanol, then the acid, then the salt — because each node's route is certified by a balance whose feeds are already on the shelf. The fertilizer loop and the polymer pair are not bootstrap items: their feeds (industrial gases, refined monomers) are exactly the nodes the tree marks as *not kitchen-walkable*. The tree draws the kitchen's competence boundary as a graph property, which is the whole point of a capstone.

**Articulation points — the keystones.** The engine computes them (B6): **CH3COOH, CH3COONa, CH3COONa.3H2O, CO2, CO(NH2)2, C6H12O6, and C4H6O2** — the seven nodes whose removal splits the map. They are the species that front a side-branch: lose the acetic acid bottle and the ethanol and salt sides of the trunk stop talking; lose the salt and the alkali route, the seam, and the binder gateway all detach at once. Chemically, the keystones are the book's cast: the acid, its salt, its hydrate, the exhaust gas, the fertilizer, the sugar, and the monomer. The graph says what thirty-five chapters said one at a time: this is a small kit, and it has a hub.

**Failure modes.** A tree fails three ways: a keystone goes empty (the articulation reading above), an edge's yield quietly degrades (the cumulative reading of Lecture 3 — 0.9310 is a promise that assumes both steps hold), or a node decays off the shelf (the time axis of Chapter 25 — the bleach that is 69.38% of what the label promised). Every kitchen failure the Atlas has ever recorded is one of those three, and the tree is the only diagram that shows all of them on one page.

**The charter fence.** The tree refuses energetics-class nodes, absolutely and forever: no oxidizer-fuel pairs, no energetic mixtures, no nitrates walking in as "just another salt." The gate refusal of R1 — phosphorus, P, refused at the gate, this chapter being the eleventh zero-admission chapter — is the fence drawn as a parser behavior. The capstone's job is to see the whole tree; the fence is what keeps the tree worth seeing.

## Lab 36.1 — Engine 25: the call-stack descent evaluator

**Design.** Engine 25 is a recursive-descent parser that goes DIRECT TO MASS. Every grammar production — formula, group, element — returns an atom-count map on the way up the call stack; the stack itself is the only structure the engine ever builds. No parse tree is materialized, no memoization, no registers, no RPN: evaluation is a single descent. Atom counts are BigInt; masses live in myu (1 myu = 1e-4 u) on the held-22 table; ledger grams are exact BigInt rationals rounded once at display (6 decimal places); a single integrality gate checks total mass on every balance row. The grammar: a formula is one or more groups; a group is an element with an optional count, or a parenthesized formula with an optional multiplier; a side is terms joined by '+', each with an optional leading coefficient. The gate refuses unknown symbols (against held-22), lowercase leads, unterminated and empty groups, zero counts, and trailing junk.

**Lineage.** Chapter 25's review engine was a recursive-descent TREE parser — it built a tree and then walked it. Engine 25 shares zero code with all twenty-four prior chapter engines, and with that one in particular it shares only the descent idea: this engine never builds the tree, because the whole point of the capstone is that the tree is the book, not the parse.

## Lab 36.2 — The route ledgers, corridor by corridor

**B1 — the vinegar corridor.** Start where Chapter 4 started: 5.00 g of acetic acid is 83.2612 mmol (the engine re-derives the pin mass 600520 myu and the millimoles in one descent). Neutralizing it takes 3.330197 g of NaOH and delivers 6.830247 g of sodium acetate plus 1.499950 g of water. The closure is exact on both sides at 8.330197 g — and exact it must be, because the closure IS balance row E1: the atoms that go in are the atoms that come out, and the ledger merely weighs them twice.

**B2 — the fermentation corridor.** 100.00 g of glucose is 555.0745 mmol. Fermented per E2, it yields 51.143453 g of ethanol and 48.856547 g of CO2, closing exactly at 100.000000 g. The ethanol figure is the theoretical yield — the mass-basis ceiling that Chapter 21's review appendix recorded as 2 x 46.069 / 180.156, and that the industrial fermentation literature carries as the theoretical 0.51 g of ethanol per g of glucose [convergent]. Real fermentations land below it — glycerol and cells take their cut — which is precisely why the distinction between the theoretical and the applied ledger exists.

**B3 — the urea corridor.** The tree's industrial edge: 10.00 g of urea is 166.5113 mmol; hydrolyzed per E7's reverse (2 NH3 + CO2 side), it consumes 2.999700 g of water and splits into 5.671706 g of ammonia and 7.327994 g of CO2, closing exactly at 12.999700 g. The corridor is the kitchen's read-only window onto the Haber-fed world: the kitchen can run the arithmetic but cannot make the feeds — industrial urea is the NH3 + CO2 reaction run under heat and pressure at world scale [convergent].

**B4/B5 — the cumulative reading.** Row B4 proves the product rule on the trunk: 0.98 x 0.95 and 0.95 x 0.98 both land exactly on 0.9310 — the two branches of the trunk commute, and their convergence is exact at displayed precision (the session-104 line, in its cleanest form). Row B5 then applies the factor to the B1 corridor's theoretical output: 6.830247 g becomes 6.358960 g of what actually lands. The gap between those two figures — 0.471287 g — is the price of two imperfect steps, and printing both is the difference between a recipe and a route.

**B6 — the map audit.** The engine walks the chapter's own map: fifteen nodes, each mass re-derived and matched to its pin; twelve certified edges, each endpoint checked; three components counted; and the articulation set computed by removal — the seven keystones of Lecture 5. The one slipped edge (E9) is excluded by construction: the audit counts only edges the gate passed. Zero unpinned node masses: the capstone invented nothing.
