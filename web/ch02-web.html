Vol I · Chapter 2 — Reading the World as a Chemical Inventory

Chapter 1 built the lab and the notebook. This chapter fills the shelves. The premise of the Atlas — a chemist with full access to knowledge but no access to a chemical supplier — meets its first test not at the bench but at the store: the ordinary world is already a warehouse, if you can read its labels. This is the full working chapter of P1: Read the label (the P-index's first move, seeded in Chapter 1's Lecture 0), and of P10: Route economics — the make-or-buy decision that will govern every synthesis in Volumes II through V.

Two commitments recorded at Chapter 1's review (RA-5) are honored here in full: the improvised balance (Lecture 5) and the make-or-buy scoring method with worked examples (Lecture 4 + Lab 2.2).

Lecture 0 — The census (pencil and paper, before any chemistry)

Before you own a single piece of apparatus, do this: take your notebook (Chapter 1's six-line entry template) and walk your home top to bottom. Every bottle, box, and canister that declares a chemical composition gets one line. Do not interpret yet — copy what the label says, exactly.

Lecture 1 — The label is a data structure

A consumer label is not prose; it is a record with fields. Learn the fields once and every label reads the same way.

Field 1: the product name. Chemistry-free marketing. "Rooto" tells you nothing; "drain cleaner" tells you the use, not the contents. Two bottles called "drain opener" can share a shelf while containing completely different chemicals — sulfuric acid in one (N57's Rooto), sodium hypochlorite or lye in another. Never buy by product name; buy by declared active ingredient. This is not pedantry: the Charter's known-dangerous combinations from Chapter 1 are mostly made by people who bought by name and mixed by guesswork.

Field 2: the active ingredient(s). The declared chemical, ideally with its CAS registry number — the unique identifier that disambiguates every "copper sulfate," "tartar," or "lime" you will meet. When a label says sodium tetraborate without saying decahydrate, you are holding Na2B4O7·10H2O in practice (borax is always the decahydrate at retail); the hydrate is part of the identity (Lecture 2 returns to this).

Field 3: the strength (assay), with its convention. The trap that costs the most errors, because "percent" hides four different meanings:

  • w/w (weight in total weight): the muriatic label's 31.45% HCl is 31.45 g per 100 g of solution.
  • w/v (weight in total volume): the bleach label's "6% sodium hypochlorite" is 6 g per 100 mL; vinegar's "5% acidity" is 5 g acetic acid per 100 mL.
  • v/v: alcohol "70% by volume."
  • ppm: the trace regime.

For dilute water solutions the conventions nearly coincide (density near 1). They diverge exactly where it matters most: concentrated acids. Read 32% HCl as w/w and you have 10.18 M; read the same number as w/v and you compute 8.78 M — a 16% error from one convention [computed, Lab 2.1]. Concentrated-acid labels in the United States are w/w; assume nothing, look for the words "by weight" or the SDS.

Field 4: the other/inert ingredients. Sometimes inert means inert (water). Sometimes it hides function: surfactants and thickeners in drain openers, stabilizers in peroxide, iodide-boosters in tincture iodine, anti-caking silicates in salt, dyes everywhere. The impurity taxonomy in Lecture 2 tells you when to care. A first-pass rule that never misleads: if the inert ingredients are named and few, the product is usually workable; if they are unnamed and many, assume the product is a formulation, not a reagent.

Field 5: the safety block. The GHS pictograms and signal words are themselves chemical data: the corrosion pictogram on muriatic acid is telling you the assay is real. Chapter 28 will teach you to read this block as a working document; for now, one binding habit: the safety block is part of the census line. Copy it into the notebook entry.

The fertilizer trap — a worked example of field-drift. Fertilizer labels do not declare compounds at all; they declare elemental N-P-K. A bag marked 21-0-0 is ammonium sulfate — and the check is a one-line computation: (NH4)2SO4 is 132.13 g/mol, carrying 2 N = 28.014 g, so the nitrogen fraction is 28.014/132.13 = 21.2% [computed, Lab 2.1]. The bag's "21" is not a coincidence; it is the compound's elemental nitrogen, rounded. The same trap runs on copper products: some root-killer labels state elemental copper content; CuSO4·5H2O is only 25.45% copper by mass [computed], so a "25% Cu" label is the same near-pure pentahydrate as a "99% copper sulfate pentahydrate" label — different numbers, identical bottle (Exercise 2.3 makes you convert between them both ways).

The borax datum — a convergent check. Chapter 13's boron test will need a boron standard. The borax box says nothing about boron at all, but the arithmetic is fixed: borax decahydrate (Na2B4O7·10H2O, 381.36 g/mol) carries 4 B = 43.24 g, hence 11.34% B by mass [computed]. The corpus's own colorimetry episode quotes 11.34% for exactly this reason (N72) — two independent routes, one number. When your computed value and a practitioner's quoted value agree to four figures, that is the P9 convergence habit working on a purchase.

The era and region fields. Labels drift by decade and by country. The Golden Book's 1960 sourcing assumes a drugstore that sells silver nitrate off the shelf; the 2010 corpus still finds gentian violet at Walgreens (N67), but the EU of the 2020s has pulled consumer sulfuric acid from the map entirely (N84; N51's Germany parallel). Every aisle map in this chapter is dated and regional — re-census when you move, and treat "not available here" as a P10 input, not a dead end. Chapter 15 will route around the EU hole with the make-routes.

Lecture 2 — Grades: what purity buys you

Retail products carry no ACS-grade certificates, but the grade ladder is still worth knowing, because it calibrates what you are not paying for:

  • Technical / industrial — the bulk trade grade. Your drain opener, your muriatic acid, your borax. Assay declared, impurities unspecified.
  • FCC (Food Chemicals Codex) — food-grade: your baking soda, table salt. Constrains toxic impurities (heavy metals especially), says nothing about trace organics.
  • USP / NF — pharmaceutical purity. Epsom salt bottles say "USP." Aspirin tablets are assayed to the milligram — the 325-mg tablet is a standard, not an approximation (N74 built a quantitative colorimetry ladder on exactly that).
  • ACS / Reagent — the analytical grades. Almost nothing at retail; the specialty vendors of the N29 list live here.

The impurity taxonomy — decide which class you are facing before you decide whether it matters:

  1. Declared functional additives (surfactants, thickeners, dyes, fragrances). The most common and the most separable-or-avoidable: buy the variant without them (there usually is one), or route around them in the separation train (Vol I Ch 5).
  2. Manufacturing residues (iron in muriatic acid — the yellow tint N83's distiller filtered out; organics in drain-opener acid — what N82's distiller was removing for electroplating work).
  3. Water, in two disguises: free water (which Lab 2.1's assays just subtract) and water of crystallization — the hydrate trap. CuSO4·5H2O is a different compound from CuSO4 for bookkeeping purposes: the pentahydrate is 63.9% anhydrous salt by mass, so "99% copper sulfate" on a label always means the hydrate unless it says anhydrous basis, and any recipe in moles must specify which. The corpus's flagship yield case (N58) worked its loss ledger through exactly this: 100.35 g of pentahydrate is 0.4019 mol, not 0.629 mol.
  4. Structural/isomeric trace impurities — matters in organic work (Vol II Ch 21+), nearly never in the inorganic aisles.

The decision line: pay for purity only when the experiment invoices you for it. The corpus gives the two poles. Thompson titrated retail drain opener and found it running at 18.4 M — 98%-grade sulfuric acid wearing a plumber's label (N57) — then saved the sodium sulfate byproduct and got on with the work: purity needed, purity verified, no more. At the other pole, a distiller re-distills that same retail acid at 540 °C because electroplating grades cannot tolerate the organic residues (N82): the experiment demanded the next rung. This pairing — assay what you bought (P9), upgrade only on demand (P6) — is the grade chapter in one line, and it is why the Atlas's mineral-acid chapter (15) will show you all three: buy, verify, upgrade.

Note the verification culture: Thompson did not trust the Rooto label's number; he titrated it, then cross-checked titration against a gravimetric method (N57) — two ways, the Chapter 1 rule, applied to a purchase. Adopt this: the label is a claim until your notebook has verified it.

Lecture 4 — Make or buy: the scoring method (P10, worked)

The premise's own rule is the starting point: specialty chemicals are bought only when synthesis is genuinely impractical — and then only items of regular, unrestricted retail availability. The working question, every single time you need a chemical: which route wins? The corpus's history gives the answer a scorecard shape (P10 in the P-index). Score each route on four criteria, each 1 to 5, higher is better:

  • A — starting-material availability (5: on a retail shelf; 1: specialty-only)
  • D — low apparatus demand (5: a jar; 1: a 540 °C still)
  • H — low hazard (5: nothing to it; 1: the route is the hazard)
  • Y — yield and separability (5: the product separates itself; 1: dilute, slow, gummy)

Total score = wA·A + wD·D + wH·H + wY·Y, with weights chosen by you, because weights are your priorities (the Ruined-World scenario zeroes the retail branch and re-prices everything). Before scoring, normalize the one number that makes unlike packages comparable: price per mole.

Price per mole — the normalizer. Lab 2.1 computes the corpus's headline numbers [computed]: Rooto at $8.00/quart titrated at 18.38 M holds 17.39 mol of H2SO4 — $0.46 per mole. Muriatic at ~$8.00/gallon and 10.0 M holds 37.85 mol of HCl — $0.21 per mole (per mole of H+ they are near-twins: $0.23 vs $0.21, since sulfuric is diprotonic). Charcoal at $6 per 12 lb is $0.013 per mole of carbon — the cheapest carbon you will ever price. Activated carbon at $6 per 9 oz is $0.28 per mole. Epsom salt at $3/lb, dehydrated, is $13.54 per kg of anhydrous MgSO4 versus $25 per kg for the bought anhydrous — a 1.85× gap that Exercise 2.6 makes you think about carefully, because the raw gap is not the verdict.

Worked case 1 — sulfuric acid, five routes. This is Lab 2.2's full engine; the outcome: under equal weights the buy route (R1) scores 18 and dominates; a 2,401-combination weight sweep shows R1 winning 97.1% of all weight profiles, with the in-pot metabisulfite route (R4) taking 41 profiles (when hazard dominates) and the 540 °C distillation route (R5) rising to second place only under purity-first weighting. The verdict is robust: where retail sulfuric exists, buy it; the make-routes exist for the EU hole and the Ruined World. (Lab 2.2, executed below, shows all of this.)

Worked case 2 — the $45 vs $2 reversal. Ammonium metavanadate: $45 from the specialty chemical vendors, or ~$2 of pottery-aisle V2O5 plus the V2O5→NaVO3→NH4VO3 conversion performed at the bench (N70). Here both price and availability point the same way — make — and the episode doubles as a purity lesson: its maker caught and corrected his own solubility number mid-video (the corpus's second in-video self-correction; the honest-notebook culture of Ch 1 is not optional equipment). [single-source corpus]

Worked case 3 — when the make-route wins on quality, not price. Cody's charcoal verdict: the $6 bag answers the price question, but homemade retort charcoal wins the quality axis — cleaner-burning, "a third of the way to activated" (N85). And its successor episode closed the loop with numbers: steam-activated homemade carbon measured an iodine number of 92.5 mg/g against the commercial product's 89 — commercial parity, at ~2 kWh per 2 oz, so buy unless the making is the point (N96). Read the axes: price per mole is one criterion, not the verdict; the verdict is the weighted sum.

The corpus's own classifiers. Texium's stocking list (N29) is the buy-philosophy canon: some chemicals are simply not worth making at any price — the Atlas accepts this and adds the premise's discipline (regular retail only, no specialty orders in the buyer's world). Thompson's carbonate hub (N30) is the same lesson in the inorganic aisle: buy the baking soda, make from it. The standing order of operations for every Volume II target: P1 first (is it on a shelf?), then P10 (score the routes), then synthesize — and log the verdict in the notebook, because route-economics decisions are data too.

Lecture 5 — The improvised balance (the Chapter 2 commitment)

Molar bookkeeping needs grams; grams need a balance; and in the Ruined World there is no balance. The chapter's P11 lecture delivers one.

The beam. A rigid straight edge — a ruler, a hacksaw blank, a straightened coat hanger — balanced at its center on a fulcrum (a knife edge, a nail, the rim of a bowl works at this accuracy class). Two identical pans hang from thread at equal marks (count the marks; you are calibrating arm symmetry, not trusting it).

The two-sided habit, carried over from arithmetic. Weigh by substitution, not by one-sided comparison: place the object on the left pan and tare to balance; then swap sides and re-balance. If the beam disagrees with itself, the arms are unequal — halve the discrepancy and move on, or mark the true center. This is the same discipline as Chapter 1's two-ways rule, applied to an instrument.

Calibration masses, two ways.

  1. Coins. A US nickel is 5.000 g by mint specification; a post-1982 penny is 2.500 g [convergent — US Mint specifications; readers elsewhere: your mint publishes the same table]. Five nickels = 25.00 g, a check mass anyone can reproduce. Coin sets give 2.5/5/10/… steps.
  2. Water. A measured milliliter is a gram at everyday precision (Chapter 1's Case B used 0.998 g/mL at room temperature — the difference is below this instrument's noise). A kitchen measuring cup plus a drop-counted pipette (Chapter 1's N63 calibration) gives any mass in the awkward gaps: 36.27 g ≈ 100 mL.

The tare, the drift check, the honest penalty. Always weigh by difference (container+sample, then container) — the double-weighing also cancels zero drift. Re-zero before every session; drafts and a sticky pan edge are this instrument's real error sources. The honest accuracy statement: a carefully built beam balance reads to roughly ±0.1 g in the 1–100 g range [convergent at the level of the corpus's own gravimetric episodes — N63's pipette calibration is the accuracy culture's ancestor]. A $10–25 digital jeweler's scale reads to 0.01 g, and the corpus's verdict on balances was blunt: this is the BUY branch — the improvised balance is the Ruined-World fallback and the accuracy-homework, not the daily driver (N63's balance datum).

The recursion is worth one look before you leave the lecture: Thompson calibrated a pipette with a balance (N63); you calibrate a balance with water. Every instrument in this book is checked against some other instrument, and the chain bottoms out in definitions — a liter, a gram — that you can rebuild from a kitchen tap. That is what "instrumentless" actually means: not no instruments, but no unverified instruments.

Exercises

Graded ●○○ (routine) / ●●○ (needs assembly) / ●●● (chapter summit); ◆ = diagnose the tool yourself (no hints); ◐ = open-ended. Every exercise worked at least two ways, each way tagged with its P-ID.

2.1 ●○○ — The bleach bottle. A label reads "sodium hypochlorite 6%. Other ingredients: 94%. Yield: 500 mL." (a) How many grams of NaOCl are in the bottle? (b) How many moles? Hint: what convention is "6%" here? Answer: (a) 6% w/v means 6 g per 100 mL → 30.0 g. Second way: 0.06 g/mL × 500 mL = 30 g — the proportion view. (b) 30.0 g ÷ 74.44 g/mol = 0.403 mol. Ways: direct proportion (P1 arithmetic) and unit-track (Lab 2.1's engine, P1). Both run through the same field: the convention call.

2.2 ●○○ — The 21-0-0 bag. A garden fertilizer declares 21-0-0 and nothing else chemical. (a) What compound is it? (b) Verify the 21. (c) Where does the other 78.8% of the mass live? Hint: elemental vs compound labeling. Answer: (a) Ammonium sulfate — the only common retail solid whose N content rounds to 21 (sodium nitrate is 16.5% N; urea 46.6%). (b) 2×14.007 ÷ 132.134 = 21.20% [computed]. Second way: mass bookkeeping per mole: 28.014 g N in 132.13 g of salt. (c) 24.3% S and 54.5% O — the sulfate; the percentages sum to 100, a self-check (P12's ledger habit, P1's label habit).

2.3 ●●○ — The hydrate trap, both directions. Two one-pound root-killer brands sit at the same price. Brand A: "copper sulfate pentahydrate 99.0%." Brand B: "copper sulfate 99.0% (anhydrous basis)." Which bottle holds more moles of copper, and by what factor? Hint: one of these labels is telling you the assay basis, not the bottle contents. Answer: B. Route 1 (moles): A: 453.6 g × 0.99 ÷ 249.68 = 1.798 mol; B: 453.6 g × 0.99 ÷ 159.60 = 2.814 mol. Factor 1.565. Route 2 (mass fraction): the pentahydrate is 63.9% anhydrous salt, so per equal mass A delivers 0.639× the anhydrous copper sulfate B does: 1/0.639 = 1.565 — same answer, no moles needed. (Both ways: P1 + the hydrate arithmetic of Lecture 2; the factor is the 1.5654 multiplier from Lab 2.1.) The follow-on worth a notebook line: if you buy B and later weigh out "copper sulfate pentahydrate" quantities using B's bottle, every downstream yield is off by more than half — the loss ledger starts at the shelf.

2.4 ●●○ — Acids by the proton. Using corpus prices (Rooto $8.00/qt at 18.38 M; muriatic ~$8.00/gal at 10.0 M), compare the two acids per mole of H+ and say which is cheaper per proton delivered. Then: why might the answer not decide which bottle you buy? Hint: diprotic. Answer: Rooto: 17.39 mol H2SO4 × 2 = 34.8 mol H+ → $0.23/mol H+; muriatic: 37.85 mol H+ → $0.21/mol H+. 2.6 ◆ — The drying job (no hints; diagnose the tools, then decide). A procedure needs 50 g of anhydrous magnesium sulfate. Your pharmacy sells Epsom salt, MgSO4·7H2O, USP, 100%, $3.00/lb. A hobby catalog sells "anhydrous MgSO4, 97%, $22.00 per 2-lb bag." Your oven holds 250 °C. What do you do?

Worked answer (diagnosis first, then the numbers). The tools this diagnoses: P7 (thermal decomposition — the hydrate gives up its water on heating) and P1 (the label arithmetic: what the Epsom bottle actually holds), scored by P10. The Epsom bottle: 453.6 g of heptahydrate × 48.83% anhydrous fraction = 221.5 g of anhydrous MgSO4 per pound — you need only a quarter-pound, and $3 buys four runs' worth. The catalog: 907.2 g × 0.97 = 880 g at $22.00. Per kilogram of anhydrous: $13.54 (epsom-dehydrated) vs $25.02 (bought) — dehydrate wins 1.85× on price. But the second way to score it: the oven route's real costs are the rungs (110 °C to ~250 °C, held "until it appears dry" — the corpus's own ladder, N86), the hours, the dissolve-itself hazard if you rush it, and a yield below 100% (loss ledger). At $22/bag, making wins clearly; at a $5 sale price, buying wins. The general tool you have extracted: hydrate-bearing retail products are pre-paid anhydrous salts with water attached — price them on the anhydrous basis before you pay the catalog's premium (P1+P7 under P10).
