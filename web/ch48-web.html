Lecture 0 — The Vat at Pencil and Paper (the color axis's own arithmetic)

The three states, and why the middle one is the trick. Indigo itself is the chapter's fence and its product in one: a blue solid so reluctant to dissolve that water essentially refuses it [register]. That insolubility is why it dyes — a molecule that will not go into the fiber's water will not come back out of it either — but it is also why you cannot apply it directly: there is no blue solution to dip anything into. The vat is the workaround, and it is three states in a cycle. State one, the pigment: insoluble blue, 262.268 g/mol, pinned at Chapter 22 and re-derived exactly at this chapter's Sheet 1. State two, the leuco form: two hydrogens added across the central double bond (B2, C₁₆H₁₀N₂O₂ + H₂ → C₁₆H₁₂N₂O₂, exact — mass 264.284, the second Chapter 22 pin re-derived), and with the double bond gone the molecule's crystal-building geometry is gone too: the reduced form is soluble, and its solution is not blue at all but yellow-green. State three, the developer: air. Dip the fiber in the yellow, work it under the surface, lift it out — and the oxygen of the air oxidizes the leuco form back to the insoluble blue, this time inside the fiber, one molecule at a time, permanently. The episode's own mechanism sentence is the whole vat in one line: "The oxygen first oxidizes any reducing agent that might remain and then it starts oxidizing the leuco indigo back into the insoluble blue form" [register]. Blue in, yellow through, blue forever: that is the entire technology, and it is five thousand years old.

The reducing agent, priced. The bench question is what removes the two hydrogens' worth of oxygen demand — what keeps the vat reduced while the fiber soaks — and the answer of both episodes is sodium dithionite, Na₂S₂O₄, 174.096 g/mol, Chapter 22's pin. The pencil price: the dye bath of the indigo episode charges 0.2 g of indigo (0.7626 mmol, L08) and 0.3 g of dithionite (1.7232 mmol, L09) — 2.26 equivalents of practice against the 1:1 demand (L10), where the 1:1 theory charges 0.1328 g for that bath (L12, Chapter 22's B4 convention re-derived this session). The overcharge is not carelessness; it is the oxygen scan. Dithionite burns air on contact — the balance B3 prices the sweep (Na₂S₂O₄ + O₂ + 2 NaOH → Na₂SO₃ + Na₂SO₄ + H₂O, exact) — so every gram charged must reduce indigo and stand guard against the oxygen dissolving in at every stir. The episode's pretreatment makes the same point at the bucket scale: 5 L of water with 1 g of dithionite and 1 g of drain-cleaner NaOH poured in first, a 1.1488 mM oxygen scavenging bath (L17), the fiber wetted with deoxygenated water before it ever meets the vat — because a wet fiber carries dissolved air, and dissolved air is the developer arriving too early. And the episode's own honest unknown lives exactly at this seam: "I'm not exactly sure why in both runs I needed more sodium dithionite than the recipe asked for. I think it's because my sodium dithionite is just old and not very good" [register] — stale reagent, oxygen ingress, or impatience; the record refuses to say which, and the chapter refuses to guess.

What pencil and paper refuses. Nothing in this chapter needs a new element: every species in its molar book is closed under the book's own pins — the dye axis's four at Chapter 22, acetone and its density and sodium acetate at Chapter 21, the NaOH/Na₂CO₃ pair at Chapter 8, sulfuric acid at Chapter 6, glucose at Chapter 10, sodium nitrite at Chapter 20, aniline at Chapter 47, the sulfite/sulfate/sulfide/sulfur set at Chapters 17, 5, 13, and 17 again — and the nine genuinely new admissions (2-nitrobenzaldehyde, isatin, the disulfonic acid, indigo carmine, the reduced carmine, sodium gluconate, p-nitroaniline, 2-naphthol, para red) are new only because no prior chapter had touched a dye. The chapter's arithmetic is therefore checkable by anyone, at home, with a pencil: every mass an integer sum of Chapter 3's held element table, every balance an atom-multiset identity, every yield a division someone can redo. The one place the pencil needs help is the one place the chapter says so: pH. A pH is a logarithm of a root of an equilibrium, and neither the root nor the log is rational arithmetic — so the engine brackets the root exactly (the carbonate's quadratic, bisected at 1e-7 on integers with the comparison made against the exact rational constant), and only the final log renders in the float witness, inside the exact bracket as the cage. The convention is stated here because the chapter's sharpest correction depends on it: the stoplight's carbonate bath, which the episode estimated at pH 8–9 in the video and corrected toward 11 in the description, computes at 11.8953 [computed] — and the correction, not the estimate, is what the arithmetic supports.
