Chapter 34 — Plant and Medicinal Extractions: The Kitchen's Solvent Library

A ruined world's medicine cabinet is not a pharmacy — it is a garden that nobody remembers how to read. Every leaf the household ever brewed, every spice that burned and every root that numbed, is a plant keeping its chemistry folded inside a cell, and the kitchen has always been the instrument that pulls it out: hot water, alcohol, steam, patience. This is the chapter where the Atlas reads the spice rack and the tea tin the way it has read every other shelf — as chemistry, weighed, priced, and entered in the ledger — with one fence the other shelves never needed: the dose that heals and the dose that harms are the same extraction, run longer.

Chapter status: reviewed (session 102 — VERDICT: PASS WITH CORRECTIONS; Revision Appendix RA-1..RA-8 appended this session, binding over the body where they disagree). Drafted session 101 — Engine 21's battery clean at run 5; every number on this page was machine-verified before the page was written: Engine 21, the interval-table compositor (Lab 34.1), and the extraction ledgers (Lab 34.2), both executed in-drafting with verbatim outputs — and re-verified at review by Engine 22, the shunting-yard skeptic (Revision Appendix), sharing zero code with all twenty-one prior engines. Nothing in this chapter should be read as bench-verified beyond the labels shown; the labels are the contract — and on this shelf, more than any other, the contract includes a fence no arithmetic can replace: this chapter prices medicines' chemistry; it does not practice medicine.

Lecture 0 — The Mortar at Midnight

Start with the phenomenon, before any formula. A clove of garlic sits whole on the board and smells of almost nothing. You crush it — and within a breath the whole room knows. Nothing was added; something was assembled: the crushing opened compartments, an enzyme met its substrate, and a chain of sulfur reactions built allicin (162.265 [computed]), a molecule that did not exist one second before the knife. Wait ten minutes and the smell changes again — allicin is unstable, folding toward the softer diallyl disulfide family (146.266 [computed]). The garlic is not a container of a chemical; it is a reaction vessel that fires on damage.

That is the first lesson of this shelf, and no other shelf in the Atlas teaches it the same way:

  1. The plant keeps its chemistry folded. The molecules live in compartments — oil glands, vacuoles, cell walls — and the extraction is the unfolding. Heat, alcohol, crushing, and steam are the four household unfolding instruments.
  2. The defense is the flavor. Allicin is the garlic's antibiotic, aimed at the fungi that attack wounded bulbs; allyl isothiocyanate (87.140 [computed]) is the mustard seed's weapon, stored harmless as a glycoside and detonated by an enzyme when the seed is crushed — the wasabi bite is a chemical defense, borrowed by the tongue. The spices humans prize are, almost without exception, plants' arms.
  3. Extraction is a count, not an event. Tea steeps gold, then amber, then black — the caffeine (194.194 [computed]) and the tannins cross into the water at a rate the clock sets (Chapter 25 owns the rate laws; this chapter owns the inventory). A cup of black tea carries about 47 mg of caffeine in 240 mL [convergent]; a cup of drip coffee carries about 96 mg [convergent] — the same molecule, two plants, two doses, one ledger.

And one more phenomenon, the most domestic of all: vanilla. The bean's own molecule, vanillin (152.149 [computed]), is barely 2 % of the bean's dry weight [recognition-only] — the rest of the flavor is the extraction itself: the bean steeped in alcohol and water for months, which is why the bottle on the shelf reads like a bar inventory and a garden at once. Extraction is not the preface to the chemistry; on this shelf, extraction is the chemistry.

Lecture 1 — The Solvent Window

The kitchen owns exactly two extraction solvents in bulk — water and ethanol (46.069 [pin, Ch 21]) — and the entire craft of the shelf is knowing which plant door each one opens.

Water opens the polar doors. Salts, sugars, acids, alkaloid salts, glycosides, tannins — the molecules that carry oxygen and nitrogen in load-bearing positions — cross into hot water. And heat widens the window dramatically: caffeine's solubility climbs from 22 mg/mL at 25 °C to 670 mg/mL at 100 °C [convergent] — a 30.45× ladder (Ledger B1) — which is why tea is steeped hot and why the same leaf yields almost nothing to cold water in a hurry. A 240 mL brew held at the boiling ceiling could, in principle, carry 160.8 g of caffeine (B1's absurd row) — the leaf, not the water, is what limits the cup: the entire caffeine in the cup is ~47 mg, a rounding error of the solvent's capacity. The extraction is inventory-limited, not solubility-limited — the solvent window is open far wider than the plant ever fills it.

Ethanol opens the middle doors. The molecules that water refuses but gasoline would dissolve — the fragrant oils, the resins — cross into drinkable alcohol at 40–95 %. Chapter 21 owns the ethanol bench; this chapter borrows its fences wholesale (the flame line above all: an ethanol extraction is a flammable-liquid handling event, Safety row 1). Vanilla, tinctures, bitters, and the perfume trade's predecessors are all this one window.

And water refuses the terpenes outright. Limonene (136.238 [computed]) is 88.16 % carbon by mass (B3's row) — a pure hydrocarbon, the oil of orange peel at > 90 % of the cold-pressed oil [convergent], floating on water because it cannot hydrogen-bond to it. This is the family that needs the third instrument:

Steam. Chapter 6 owns distillation theory; this chapter is one of its oldest customers. A boiling mixture of two immiscible liquids carries them both at a combined vapor pressure — the oil distills far below its own boiling point, out of leaves and peels and buds that would scorch if heated dry. Clove oil, mint oil, citrus oil: the essential-oil shelf is Chapter 6's still pointed at a garden. The method's honesty: steam extraction does not make the oil — it only moves it; the plant made it, and no arithmetic raises the yield past what the leaf holds.

The window, closed where it must be: the lab-classic caffeine extraction runs on dichloromethane, a superb water-immiscible solvent for the alkaloid [convergent, the undergraduate canon] — and not a household liquid. The Atlas's solvent fence is absolute (Safety row 4): the bench door for the DCM route is closed, named as closed, and the chapter prices the chemistry of the closed door (the partition constant, below) without ever opening it. What the household keeps is the hot-water door, the ethanol door, and the steam door — three windows, every one older than chemistry itself.

Lecture 2 — The Partition Principle

What happens when a solute meets two solvents that do not mix? It partitions: the molecule divides between the layers in a fixed ratio, the partition coefficient K — the equilibrium constant of "where do I prefer to sit" (Chapter 24 owns the equilibrium formalism; this is its purest household face).

The principle prices the craft's oldest piece of bench wisdom — why many small extractions beat one big one. Take a model system [model-only]: K = 5, a 240 mL water layer, 30 mL of extracting layer. One pull of the full 30 mL lifts 150/390 = 0.3846 of the solute. Three pulls of 10 mL each lift 1 − (240/290)³ = 0.4332. Five pulls of 6 mL lift 0.4451. Same total solvent, more fractions won — and Ledger B6 prints the ladder. The math is Chapter 24's, the lesson is every grandmother's: rinse the leaves three times, don't drown them once.

Two honesty gates on the model: (a) the K of a real caffeine system is a property of the solvent pair, measured, not assumed — the chapter's K = 5 is a teaching number, labeled [model-only] and never printed as a caffeine datum; (b) the household's actual extraction — tea in a cup — is a one-layer system: the "second layer" is the leaf itself, a solid matrix the hot water drains. Partition still rules it: the caffeine crosses the cell wall down a concentration gradient, and the same multiple-contact logic says fresh hot water over spent leaves out-performs one long drown — the refill is a second extraction, and everyone who has ever re-steeped tea has run Lecture 2's arithmetic without knowing its name.

Lecture 3 — The Plant-Principles Ledger

The shelf's roster, by family. Four families carry most of the kitchen's plant chemistry, and one sulfur militia carries the rest:

The alkaloids — nitrogen with a price. Caffeine (C₈H₁₀N₄O₂, 194.194 [computed] — this chapter's first NEW composition, the number Chapter 21's N11 face forward-flagged here and no earlier page prints), theobromine (C₇H₈N₄O₂, 180.167 [computed] — the cacao alkaloid, "god-brothers' food" [recognition-only]), quinine (324.424 [pin, Ch 22 — the tonic bottle's own mass, first printed on the dye shelf's page]), and piperine (C₁₇H₁₉NO₃, 285.343 [computed] — black pepper's bite). Piperine's composition carries the chapter's quietest lesson: C₁₇H₁₉NO₃ is also morphine's formula [convergent] — the same atom count, worlds apart in effect; composition is not destiny, architecture is. And the alkaloid's salt face: caffeine hydrochloride (C₈H₁₁N₄O₂Cl, 230.655 [computed], E2) — how the alkaloid actually travels in acidic company, the same door quinine enters in the tonic bottle (E7, 3,608,850 myu). The family's defining habit: bitter taste, nitrogen in a ring, physiological force at small doses — which is the fence in its purest form (Lecture 4).

The glycosides — chemistry held behind a sugar. Salicin (C₁₃H₁₈O₇, 286.280 [computed]) is the willow's storage form: the active phenol tied to a glucose (180.156 [pin, Ch 21]), inert until hydrolysis cuts the tie (E1: salicin + H₂O → salicyl alcohol + glucose, 3,042,950 myu exact). The glycoside is the plant's own controlled-release chemistry — and the mustard seed's isothiocyanate glycoside is the same trick with a detonator. Note the near-twin: salicin 286.280 stands 1,420 myu — 0.142 g/mol — from Chapter 24's washing-soda crystal 286.138, two molecules with no chemistry in common at nearly the same mass (Probe 3; the seam named, the numbers never confused).

The terpenes — the oil family. Limonene (C₁₀H₁₆, 136.238 [computed], the citrus face), menthol (C₁₀H₂₀O, 156.269 [computed], the mint), thymol (C₁₀H₁₄O, 150.221 [computed], the thyme shelf's own antiseptic [recognition-only]). Built from five-carbon isoprene units, water-refusing, steam-carried — the family that is the essential-oil shelf. Chapter 33's fragrance flag is honored here: the cleaning label's "fragrance" line is this family's household second job — limonene dissolves grease, which is why citrus cleaners smell the way they clean (the Ch 33 join, priced there as a surfactant adjunct and here as an extraction product).

The phenols — the ring with an –OH. Eugenol (C₁₀H₁₂O₂, 164.204 [computed] — clove bud oil at 80–90 % eugenol [convergent], the toothache anesthetic of three centuries [recognition-only]), vanillin (C₈H₈O₃, 152.149 [computed — as a number, an ISOMER claim, not a NEW one: Chapter 23 already prints 152.149 for methyl salicylate, the wintergreen ester; same formula, same mass, different architecture — the isomer seam this chapter inherits and names]), cinnamaldehyde (C₉H₈O, 132.162 [computed] — with the honesty event printed: the drafting plan carried 148.16 for cinnamaldehyde, which is cinnamic acid's mass, C₉H₈O₂; the web battery caught the slip before the engine ran — one oxygen, 160 myu, the difference between an aldehyde and an acid). The phenol's acid face is eugenol's own arithmetic: E3 prices the saponification — eugenol + NaOH (39.997 [pin, Ch 24]) → sodium eugenolate (186.186 [computed]) + H₂O, 2,042,010 myu exact — the reaction that makes "clove oil plus soap" a wasting of both, and the reason the dental trade compounds its eugenol with zinc oxide, never with lye [recognition-only].

The sulfur militia — the defense chemistry. The garlic chain: crushing fires alliinase → allylsulfenic acid (C₃H₆OS, 90.140 [computed]) → two of them condense to allicin + H₂O (E5, 1,802,800 myu exact — the chapter's one true formation reaction, and it is an extraction-time reaction: the molecule forms while the cook works). Allicin, unstable, relaxes toward diallyl disulfide (146.266 [computed]) and its siblings — the cooked-garlic smell is the defense chemistry aging. The mustard chain: the glycoside's enzyme releases allyl isothiocyanate (C₃H₅NS, 87.140 [computed]) — wasabi, horseradish, the bite that arrives after the grate, because the reaction has to run. Sulfur, held in Chapter 25's table since the beginning, here earns its keep: the plant-defense family is the Atlas's clearest case of chemistry that happens because the household processed the plant — the extract is not a sample of the plant's chemistry; it is the plant's chemistry, in progress.

Lecture 4 — The Medicine Door

The oldest extraction the household owns is medicine. This lecture prices the chemistry of that history — with the fence printed before the chemistry, because this is the shelf where the fence and the chemistry are the same sentence.

The fence (absolute): an extract is not a dose. Willow bark tea, clove-oil toothache drops, quinine water — the historical doses lived in an era whose alternative was no medicine at all. The modern bottle carries the compound, the assay, and the count; the extract carries a plant and a hope. Never self-medicate from an extraction; never dose by steeping. The salicylate fence adds its own warning: salicylate chemistry is dose-toxic — the difference between therapy and poisoning is a factor of ten, not a hundred [convergent] — and aspirin must never be given to children with viral illness (Reye's syndrome) [convergent]. The chapter prices the molecules; the medicine is not for sale here.

The willow's chemistry, priced end to end. The willow stores salicin; hydrolysis (E1) frees salicyl alcohol (124.139 [computed]); the body and the plant oxidize it to salicylic acid (138.122 [pin, Ch 13 — first printed on the wet-analysis shelf's own page]); and in 1897 the acetyl door opened: salicylic acid + acetic anhydride (C₄H₆O₃, 102.089 [computed]) → aspirin (180.159 [pin, Ch 3 — the Atlas's own first-quoted composition, the N74 standard]) + acetic acid (60.052 [pin, Ch 4]), E4 at 2,402,110 myu exact. Three doors — hydrolysis, oxidation, acetylation — and Exercise 34.5 walks the whole corridor from 5 g of bark sugar to 3.15 g of theoretical aspirin, every closure exact (the summit, Ledger B4b's arithmetic scaled up). The corridor's honesty: no household bench should run the acetylation door — acetic anhydride is a corrosive, lachrymatory reagent with no place in a kitchen [recognition-only, the door named closed] — the corridor is priced, not practiced.

The tonic bottle, honored from two shelves. Quinine's mass belongs to Chapter 22 (the fluorescence-is-not-indication fence), its 83 ppm to Chapter 13's Dragendorff episode (N73, register-verified, [single-source] there) — this chapter adds only the salt: quinine + HCl → quinine hydrochloride (E7, 3,608,850 myu exact), the form the antimalarial history actually dosed. The bitter taste of tonic water is the alkaloid fence made potable — 83 ppm is a flavor, not a dose.

Caffeine, the chapter's own alkaloid, fenced. A cup carries ~47 mg [convergent]; the pure powder is lethal at gram scale [convergent] — the extract is safe because the leaf did the dosing. The fence has a modern edge: concentrated caffeine powders and "energy" formulations move the arithmetic from the leaf's ledger to the consumer's, and the margin narrows by three orders of magnitude. The pure-compound fence: pure caffeine is a drug; the extract is a beverage; and the difference is the whole of Safety row 3.

The pet fence (the same lecture, one species over). Theobromine, the cacao alkaloid this chapter prices at 180.167, is the dog's poison: dogs clear it many times slower than humans, and chocolate carries it in load — cocoa powder and plain chocolate at 15–20 mg/g, milk chocolate at ~2 mg/g [convergent], with the lethal band reported at 100–500 mg/kg of the dog's weight [convergent]. The molecule that is a bitter note in a human's dessert is an unmetabolized drug in a dog — metabolism, not chemistry, draws the fence line. The kitchen's extraction shelf is the dog's hazard shelf; the chapter's arithmetic is why.

Lecture 5 — Reading the Label

The extraction shelf is the label-reading shelf at its most deceptive, because the label's vocabulary is legal, not chemical — and the law's words point at processes this chapter has just priced.

"Extract" and "flavor." Vanilla extract is the label's honest word: the bean, the alcohol, the months — roughly a third of the bottle is ethanol by the standard of identity [recognition-only, order-of-magnitude]. "Natural flavor" is the legal face of the same chemistry run at industrial scale — the vanillin (152.149) in a "natural vanilla flavor" may come from a bean or from a fermentation [recognition-only]; the molecule is identical, the extraction is not. "Imitation vanilla" is the same molecule run from guaiacol or lignin [recognition-only] — the label distinguishes provenance, the mass spectrometer does not. This is the isomer lesson of Lecture 3 standing on the supermarket shelf: chemistry prices identity; the law prices history.

The essential-oil label. "100 % pure" is an extraction claim, not a composition claim — clove oil is 80–90 % eugenol [convergent], orange oil > 90 % limonene [convergent], and the rest is dozens of molecules at trace doses that carry most of the scent's character [recognition-only]. The oil is a mixture wearing a pure label. The aromatherapy trade's medical claims are not priced here at all — they are fenced at Lecture 4's line: an extract is not a dose, and an oil on skin is Lecture 4's fence plus a solvent hazard (Safety row 2: essential oils are concentrated chemical defenses, never applied neat, never taken internally).

The supplement label. "Standardized extract" is the supplement trade's attempt to cross the fence this chapter refuses: standardization is the claim that the extract carries a counted dose of one marker molecule. Where the count is real, the arithmetic of this chapter applies (the salicin content of a willow preparation is a solubility-and-yield problem, B4b's arithmetic); where the count is not real, the label is a steeped plant in a capsule. The chapter's reading: ask for the number, distrust the story.

The garden's honest salt. The Epsom box in the garden aisle is this chapter's one inorganic neighbor: magnesium sulfate heptahydrate (MgSO₄·7H₂O, 246.466 [pin, Ch 24]) — a plant nutrient sold as a crystal, dosed by the tablespoon, and extractable from nothing: the salt shelf and the extract shelf share the garden, not the method.

The join to the sink (Chapter 33's flag, honored). The cleaning aisle's "fragrance" line and the citrus-cleaner smell are this chapter's limonene family, extracted by the ton from the peels the juice trade discards — orange oil is a by-product stream [convergent] — the largest extraction industry the household touches daily, printed on every label as one word that is actually a garden.

Lab 34.1 — Engine 21: The Interval-Table Compositor (design, honesty record, battery)

Design. A fresh per-chapter drafting engine, sharing zero code with all twenty prior engines. Where Engine 19 compiled formulas to arithmetic and Engine 20 split seams recursively, Engine 21 memoizes the parse itself: a formula's token stream is parsed by a dynamic-programming interval table — F[i][j] holds the exact composition of the token span [i,j), filled by increasing span length with no recursion at all at parse time, paren-pairs precomputed by a stack scan, and every interval either a symbol leaf, a subscript binding to the immediately preceding unit, a paren group's inner span, or a first-valid top-level split (with the split-boundary rule: a number token may never begin a half, because a subscript always binds leftward). Masses are carried in deci-myria-units (1 dmyu = 1e-5 g/mol) so that decimal hydrate/segment coefficients (0.5, 0.05) ride exact integer arithmetic, and the only integrality gate is on the total mass — non-integer myu refuses as NON-EXACT; atom counts stay rational. The held-22 element table was re-transcribed independently before any computation (Ch 25's own page, the held twenty plus Mn 54.938, plus Ch 30's Si 28.085 — twenty-two entries, all integral at the myria scale). P is not among them, and this shelf never asks: the plant roster needs only C, H, O, N, S plus the salts' Na, Mg, Ca — the ninth zero-admission chapter, confirmed by a battery that never once had to reach past the held table.

Provenance census (the session-100 binding line, applied BEFORE the page froze). A provenance claim is itself a printed claim: every NEW-composition row below was grepped against every prior chapter's page — the local manuscript set (Ch 3–Ch 30) and the Ch 31/32/33 canvases — before the battery ran. The census: aspirin 180.159 first printed on Ch 3's own page (the N74 standard, later re-pinned by Ch 8 and Ch 13) — a PIN here; salicylic acid 138.122 on Ch 13's page (and Ch 23's) — PIN; methyl salicylate 152.149 on Ch 23's page — and vanillin, sharing that exact formula and mass, is printed here as an ISOMER row, never as a NEW number; quinine 324.424 on Ch 22's page (Ch 8 and Ch 13 own the 83-ppm face, not the mass) — PIN; the word caffeine appears on Ch 21's and Ch 31's pages with no mass printed (Ch 21's N11 face, forward-flagged here and honored); the word limonene appears once, on Ch 33's own forward-flag line, with no mass; and all eighteen NEW rows returned zero hits at their printed precision. The 180-family seam is named in the census itself: no prior page prints any 180.16x value (glucose's 180.156 is the family's resident), so theobromine's 180.167 is a true first print — three molecules, one leading number, 30 and 80 myu apart (Probe 2).

Honesty record. Run 1: crashed — the interval table's trailing-number rule multiplied the entire prefix interval instead of binding to the immediately preceding unit (MgSO₄ read as (MgSO)₄, caffeine doubled to 356.390), plus a harness expectation-scaling slip, the missing mass-integrality gate, and unhandled whitespace in the species syntax. Run 2: the binding rule repaired — and the battery caught a real hand-pin slip: the drafter's 74.122 for isobutanol is the database basis (H 1.00794, O 15.9994); the held-22 basis gives 74.123 — the engine right, the pin re-based, the seam named; six further rows failed only on a float-comparison artifact (values exact underneath); and the run exposed a second engine hole: REF 12 was still wrongly ACCEPTED because a split could begin at a number token (H₂O mis-parsed through a 0.5 coefficient). Run 3: split-boundary rule added; the engine core fully clean — every composition, atom map, balance, closure, and the must-fail CAUGHT — but the run carried three harness defects of its own: the REF-12 probe was badly chosen (CuSO₄·0.5H₂O is mass-exact on the held table, 1,686,095 myu — the engine right to accept it; the probe re-chosen at 0.05, whose 9,007.5-myu water of crystallization is genuinely non-integral), the balance comparator used raw object key order (two PASS rows printed FAIL), and the ledger displays wore mol/mmol scaling slips.
