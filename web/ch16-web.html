Vol II · Chapter 16 — Oxidizers of the Household: The Upper End of the Redox Ladder

Lecture 0 — The shelf's other half, read before anything is poured

Chapter 11 built the electron court and let the metals sue each other for their compounds; Chapter 12 put a price on the electron and showed the cell paying it; Chapter 15 climbed the acid ladder to the strong-acid bench. This chapter walks to the other end of the activity series — the end that takes electrons rather than gives them — because the ruined world sells that end ready-made, and it is the most dangerous thing on any household shelf. The thesis, and it is the volume's hinge stated a second time: an oxidizer is a reagent that has already been paid for — the world's shelf sells concentrated electron demand by the bottle, and the discipline this chapter teaches is not how to make that demand stronger but how to read what strength the bottle actually holds, keep it from spending itself on the wrong shelf-mate, and step it up only where the chemistry is honest and the door is licensed.

Sit at the kitchen table with the notebook and pour nothing. The pencil-and-paper census, before any apparatus:

  • The brown bottle — drugstore hydrogen peroxide, 3% w/w, the dark plastic bottle with the venting cap; the 12% "40-volume" hair developer at the beauty supply; and behind the pharmacy counter in some countries, the 35% class this book never schedules.
  • The white jug — household bleach, sodium hypochlorite at 5.25% (the traditional regular class) or 8.25% (the concentrated class), always sold basic, always sold unstable, always sold with an expiration nobody prints.
  • The pool shelf — calcium hypochlorite granules at 65–70% available chlorine; the trichloroisocyanuric acid pucks at ~90%; the "non-chlorine shock" bucket, which is potassium peroxymonosulfate — an oxidizer so polite the label says spa.
  • The purple crystal — potassium permanganate, sold for iron removal in well water and stump treatment at the farm store, gram-for-gram the strongest oxidizer an ordinary person can buy without a question asked.
  • The dry box — sodium percarbonate (the "oxygen bleach" laundry booster, a cocrystal that is one-third hydrogen peroxide by mass), the free-flowing powder that makes the liquid peroxide bottle look like what it is: a bottle of water with a reagent dissolved in it.

That is five families and four shelves, and not one of them is sold as a chemical — every one is sold as a product, with its assay hidden inside a marketing convention. The chapter's five questions, in bench order: what does each label's number actually mean (the assay conventions), which bottle is strongest (the redox meter at the top), how does the shelf's weakest family get stepped up (hypochlorite's two upgrades: the boiling pot and the cell), what does one purple reagent do in three different pots (permanganate's three faces), and how do you keep any of them — stored, dated, and separated from everything that burns?

One boundary before any apparatus is unpacked, and it is the book's binding line for this entire chapter, inherited from Chapter 15's nitric line and the durable energetics resolution: every oxidizer in this Atlas is benched as a reagent — an oxidant, a titrant, an oxygen source, an assay tool — and nothing else. No oxidizer-plus-fuel mixture procedure appears anywhere in this book: no compositions, no ignition recipes, no energetic demonstrations; the corpus's one energetic-material face (N75, the guncotton video) remains cited at metadata level only, through the hazard-recognition lens of Chapter 28, and the lens itself is the lesson. The chapter prints the arithmetic of the strongest things on the household shelf because a complete book prices the doors; it opens none of them.

Lecture 1 — The oxidizer census: reading labels that were written to hide the assay

How you could have found it. Buy one bottle of each family, read the label, and try to answer the only question that matters at the bench: how many moles am I holding? The label will not say. The oxidizer shelf is sold in four mutually incompatible conventions, and the first skill of this chapter is translating all four into the one currency the bench speaks — molarity, by way of the three-way arithmetic of Chapter 4 and Lab 15.1: C = 10·wt%·ρ/M.

Convention one: weight percent. The peroxide bottle (3% w/w) and the bleach jug (5.25% or 8.25% sodium hypochlorite) sell by mass of active ingredient per mass of product. Straightforward — except the bleach jug's ρ is its own quiet number (tabled: 1.093 g/mL at 5% solution, rising to 1.21 at 14%), and the label prints neither the density nor the date that would make the number true.

Convention two: "available chlorine." The pool shelf sells hypochlorite products by the mass of Cl₂ that would do the same oxidizing — a convention from the water-treatment industry, where what matters is oxidizing equivalents, not chemical identity. The conversion is one honest multiplication: a mole of NaOCl carries the oxidizing work of a mole of Cl₂, so the factor is 70.906/74.442 = 0.9525 — and the elegant accident of the regular jug is that **5.25% sodium hypochlorite is 5.00% available chlorine, which is why both numbers appear on both labels and generations of homeowners have assumed they were the same thing**. The trap runs the other way at the pool store: calcium hypochlorite products quote available chlorine directly (65–70% for the granular shock class) — and the pure salt's own ceiling is 2×70.906/142.982 = 99.2% available chlorine, so the pool label's number is a product assay, not a conversion, and the label's remainder is water, calcium chloride, and the salt's own slow decay (Lecture 5).

Convention three: "volumes." The beauty-supply developer sells peroxide in volumes of oxygen — the volume of O₂ (at STP, the 22.414 L/mol of Chapter 10's table, not the 24.5 working pin) released per volume of reagent, a convention from the bleaching trade. The honest arithmetic runs 10×wt%·ρ/M, halved for the O₂, times 22.414: the 3% bottle computes to 9.98 ≈ 10 volume, the 6% developer to 20.2 ≈ 20 volume, the 12% to 41.1 ≈ 40 volume — the label classes are the STP convention rounded, and the drafting session's own engine initially ran the chain on the book's RTP pin and read 10.9 for the 10-volume bottle: the catch is printed as found, because the conventions are the lesson (Lab 16.1 Run 5).

Convention four: the purple crystal's none at all. Potassium permanganate is sold by the pound with no assay convention whatsoever — 99% class crystals, and the bench makes its own solutions and standardizes them itself (Lecture 4: the self-indicating titration). The strongest bottle on the household shelf is also the only one sold without a number.

The census itself, in the bench's currency. Run every label through the composition engine (the same three-way arithmetic, all masses from the held element table — Lab 16.1, executed and verbatim below): the regular jug is 0.769 M sodium hypochlorite; the concentrated jug 1.258 M (on the two tabled density datums, interpolated); the 3% peroxide bottle 0.891 M; the 40-volume developer 3.669 M; and the quietest label in the house — the laundry booster's cocrystal 2Na₂CO₃·3H₂O₂ — computes to 32.50% hydrogen peroxide by mass, ten times the drugstore bottle, sold as a powder you store next to the detergent.

Three census lessons, each load-bearing. First, the strongest number on the shelf is the one nobody prints. The percarbonate box and the permanganate jar both outrank the liquid bottles by an order of magnitude; the shelf's danger ranking and its label ranking are unrelated, which is Chapter 28's recognition lesson stated as a shopping observation. Second, every liquid oxidizer is a decomposition waiting to be dated. Peroxide decays to water and oxygen on a shelf-life clock; hypochlorite decays on a faster one — and the assay discipline of Chapter 15 transfers whole: the dated titration is the only sentence in the system that knows what the jug holds today. Third, the two solid "polite" oxidizers are the network's future reagents. The percarbonate is the peroxide source that never needs concentrating (dissolve, and the soda ash is a spectator Chapter 14 already owns); the oxone bucket (the triple salt 2KHSO₅·KHSO₄·K₂SO₄, 49.5% peroxymonosulfate by mass, computed) is the solid acid oxidizer — and its peroxymonosulfate is exactly the species Chapter 15's audit-only ceiling (9.26% H₂SO₅, the Caro door) priced from the other side: the shelf sells, pre-made and stable, what the from-scratch route walls at 9.26% — the oxidizer chapters' first inherited flag, honored here by buying instead of making, and the door itself is priced at audit level in Lab 16.2's B6.

Lecture 2 — The meter at the top: reading oxidizer strength the way Chapter 12 read batteries

The activity series of Chapter 11 ranked the metals by willingness to give electrons; its table stopped at the noble couple (Au³⁺/Au at +1.498 V) and hinted at more above. The oxidizer bench lives in that hinted-at region, and the meter is the same one subtraction Chapter 11 printed: E°cell = E°(reduced side) − E°(donating side) — here with the reagent on the reduced side and whatever the reagent attacks as the donor.

Read the table the way Lecture 0's census ranked the shelf. Permanganate in acid (+1.512, against water's own +1.229) will oxidize the solvent itself if given a chance and a day — ΔG° = −2×96485×0.283 = −54.6 kJ per couple (Lab 16.1 Run 4) — which is why the purple solution is stable only in neutral-to-basic water and why the strong-acid faces of Chapter 15 are exactly the wrong place to casually pour it. Hypochlorous acid (+1.482) outranks chlorine's own couple (+1.358) by +0.124 V — the quiet thermodynamic push behind the never-combination Chapter 8 pinned: bleach + acid does not merely release the chlorine the jug was made from, the acidified oxidizer is the stronger species, and ΔG° = −23.9 kJ per couple points the way. And hydrogen peroxide appears twice.

That double appearance is the lecture's one idea, and it deserves its own paragraph. Peroxide is the shelf's only common reagent that is both a strong oxidizer and a moderate reducing agent: its peroxide–water couple sits at +1.776 (above every other species here — peroxide the oxidizer), while its oxygen–peroxide couple sits at +0.682 (below most of them — peroxide the donor). Which face it shows depends entirely on the partner: against bleach it donates (E°cell = 1.482 − 0.682 = +0.800 V, ΔG° = −154.4 kJ — the oxygen handoff of Chapter 10's wet route, priced at last), against iron(II) or sulfurous acid it takes (Chapter 15's N01: the peroxide that oxidized SO₂ to sulfuric — the same bottle, the other face). Even alone it disproportionates — +1.094 V of self-push, −211.1 kJ — the number behind the brown bottle's slow decay and the reason the cap vents. The meter's lesson: "oxidizer" and "reducer" are not substances; they are roles, assigned by the subtraction, and the reagent's role flips with its partner.

Two readings close the lecture. First, the numbers are acid-standard, and the pH moves every one of them — by the Nernst drift Chapter 12 printed (0.0592 V per decade, per electron) and by species change: hypochlorite's couple falls from +1.482 (HOCl) to +0.890 (OCl⁻) as pH crosses the pKa at 7.5 [convergent], which is why the jug is sold basic and stays basic — the shelf stores its oxidizer as the weaker species and lets the acid face exist only at the point of use. Second, the meter explains a titration rule every manual states and never derives: permanganate runs in dilute sulfuric, never hydrochloric — because the permanganate couple sits a full +0.154 V above the chlorine couple, and in hydrochloric acid the titrant oxidizes the acid itself (−29.7 kJ per couple, willingly) and eats its own molarity. Lecture 4 turns exactly that danger into the bench's most honest assay.

Lecture 3 — Hypochlorite: the shelf's workhorse, and its two honest upgrades

The bleach jug is the household's biggest single chemical holding of oxidizing power, and it is also the shelf's most degraded product — sold already decomposing (Lecture 5's ledger), sold in a convention that hides its strength, and made by a chemistry Chapter 12 already built. The jug's own ancestry is the brine cell: chlorine from the anode, hydroxide from the cathode, and the cold, dilute meeting that makes the hypochlorite — Cl₂ + 2 NaOH → NaOCl + NaCl + H₂O (BALANCED, Lab 16.1 Run 2) — the industry runs it at a pH the lab would call strongly basic, because the product's own acid face is the never-combination: bleach + acid pushes HOCl out of its equilibrium (pKa 7.5), and HOCl at +1.482 V sits above Cl₂'s own couple — the jug un-makes itself into the gas it was made from, ΔG° −23.9 kJ per couple, the thermodynamic sentence behind Chapter 8's rule and N77's do-not-attempt boundary. Ammonia is the same lesson one shelf over (chloramines, the toxic-gas never-combination Chapter 8 pinned; the corpus's own hypochlorite-ammonia chemistry is benched at the hydrazine chapters' boundary, N07/N14 cited at register level as the reason the jug is labeled what it is labeled).

But this chapter's question is the volume's question: can the shelf's weakest oxidizing family be stepped up? The answer is yes — twice, by two different patterns, and the corpus built both.

Upgrade one: the boiling pot (pattern CAND-2, the disproportionation upgrade). Heat hypochlorite and it argues with itself: three hypochlorite ions shuttle electrons among their own chlorines — 3 NaOCl → 2 NaCl + NaClO₃ (BALANCED) — two chlorines step down to chloride, one steps up to chlorate, and the oxidizing power of the jug is converted from the fragile, decaying hypochlorite into the stable, crystalline chlorate. This is the corpus's N05 (NurdRage, Make Potassium Chlorate from Bleach, original 2010-08-26, the register's first confirmed takedown-and-reupload case — the current video is the 2023 reupload, and the original's date survives only in the mirror's filename): boil the jug, and the practitioner's own process-control cue is the register's verbatim teaching — boil until you just start to crystallize sodium chloride crystals, then note the volume and continue boiling off half — the NaCl is the reaction's own byproduct crystallizing out as the thermometer of the pot's progress. Then the potassium handoff: NaClO₃ + KCl → KClO₃↓ + NaCl (BALANCED) — and the product crystallizes because potassium chlorate is sparingly soluble where both of its parents are freely soluble (Lab 16.2's B4 prints the curve that makes the whole separation work: 7.3 g/100 g at 20 °C against sodium chlorate's ~101). The ledger, run honest (Lab 16.2's B1): a full liter of regular bleach prices at 31.40 g of theoretical potassium chlorate — and the route's honest name is theoretical: the video states no yield, the boiling drives the equilibrium but not to completion, and the register's process cue is process control, not a yield claim. The route-class honesty of Chapter 15's nitre still (N21's 60–80% band) is the precedent: the Atlas prints the ceiling and the honest band, never the hopeful number.

Upgrade two: the cell (pattern P5, electricity as reagent). The disproportionation pot burns fuel to push a self-redox; the chlorate cell pays in electrons instead — and it is the corpus's N10 (NurdRage, Make Potassium Chlorate by Electrolysis — The Basic Guide, 2019-01-15): a saturated chloride solution, an anode that survives (carbon, platinized or MMO class), current, and days. The composite chemistry is the brine cell of Chapter 12 pointed at its own products: chlorine at the anode, hydroxide at the cathode, hypochlorite where they meet — and then the same disproportionation, this time run by the pot's own heating and pH, climbing the chlorate. The composite equation gates it (Lab 16.1 Run 2): NaCl + 3 H₂O → NaClO₃ + 3 H₂, six electrons per chlorine lifted from −1 to +5, and the register's mechanism datum is the chemistry's own honesty: the two competing paths (anodic chlorate formation at the boundary layer; bulk autoxidation of the hypochlorite intermediate) are both real, both run in parallel, and the cell's efficiency is the bookkeeping of their competition. The Faraday ledger prices it exactly (Lab 16.2's B2): 6×96485 C = 160.8 Ah per mole, hence 1.3122 Ah per gram of potassium chlorate theoretical — a 5-ampere day-batch (120 Ah) buys 91.4 g at 100% current efficiency, and the honest bench band is 50–80% (the industrial cells' own modern class runs 93–96% [convergent, the process literature's central bookkeeping, re-pinned in the session-58 review]) — the losses are oxygen at the anode and the cell's own back-reactions, the same current thieves Chapter 12's zinc bath met. The energy price: 0.54 kWh per day-batch at 4.5 V — electricity is the cheapest reagent on the oxidizer bench, and the cathode's hydrogen (54.8 L per day-batch) is the ventilation bill the Charter prices.

The binding line, restated where the temptation would live. Both upgrades end at the same white solid, and the solid is the classic oxidizer of the energetic-materials world — matches, in the register's own production face, and worse, in the world's accident record. The Atlas's line is the same line Chapter 15 drew for nitric, and it binds: chlorate is benched here as a reagent — an oxidant for the wet bench, a source of oxygen (its thermal decomposition face, 2 KClO₃ → 2 KCl + 3 O₂, printed as knowledge at the recognition level with Chapter 28's lens, not scheduled as a bench procedure), and nothing else. No composition, no mixture with any fuel, no ignition demonstration appears anywhere in this book. The separation that isolates the product is a wet, cold, patient crystallization — and the product is stored in its own labeled tray, away from everything that burns, exactly as the safety table says.

Lecture 4 — Permanganate: one purple reagent, three honest faces

The purple crystal is the chapter's finest reagent and its most instructive: the same anion, MnO₄⁻, is three different oxidizers in three different pots, and the products wear the change as color — purple in, colorless, brown, or green out, the self-announcing titration the analytical chapters inherit.

The acid face: purple to colorless, +1.512 V. In dilute sulfuric acid, permanganate takes five electrons per manganese and lands as the nearly colorless Mn²⁺: 2 KMnO₄ + 5 H₂O₂ + 3 H₂SO₄ → 2 MnSO₄ + K₂SO₄ + 5 O₂ + 8 H₂O (BALANCED) — the electron court's version of Chapter 10's catalytic decomposition, here run as stoichiometry: five peroxides donate their electrons to two permanganates, and every atom of the peroxide's oxygen exits as gas. This is the permanganometric titration of the peroxide bottle — the assay this chapter adds to the bench's toolkit, run in the acid that will not eat the titrant (Lecture 2's +0.154 V meter reading is the derivation of the "dilute H₂SO₄ only" rule every titration manual states as folklore). The titration is self-indicating: the first drop of permanganate that outlives its peroxide stays purple, and the endpoint is the color that persists (Lab 16.2's B3 prices the drugstore bottle honestly: 17.8 mL of 0.02000 M titrant on a standard aliquot chain — and the titrant itself is the chapter's cleanest cross-pin, potassium permanganate at the Chapter 13 parser's own 158.032 g/mol, 3.161 g to the liter).

The neutral face: purple to brown, the water-face. In neutral or faintly basic water the manganese stops at MnO₂ — two electrons taken, and the brown-black solid that is Chapter 10's own catalyst re-pinned from the other side: the dead battery's black paste was this product once, running the decomposition it had just been. 2 KMnO₄ + 3 H₂O₂ → 2 MnO₂↓ + 2 KOH + 3 O₂ + 2 H₂O (BALANCED). The face matters to the water-treatment census: the well-water jar of potassium permanganate (the farm-store sourcing, P1) is dosed to oxidize dissolved iron and manganese to their insoluble dioxides — the register's water-treatment class runs 3.8–7.6 g per gallon for iron bacteria [convergent, the treatment-lab literature] — and the filter catches the brown. The brown precipitate is the assay's own endpoint in the household's oldest water test: the purple that clears is iron being priced.

The basic face: purple to green, +0.558 V. In strong hydroxide the permanganate takes a single electron and stays in solution as the manganate, MnO₄²⁻ — green, and reversible: 2 KMnO₄ + H₂O₂ + 2 KOH → 2 K₂MnO₄ + 2 H₂O + O₂ (BALANCED) — the only face that runs down the ladder and can be walked back up by acid (the green solution acidifying back through brown toward purple is the demonstration the analytical chapter will inherit as a redox seesaw). Three faces, one reagent, and the pattern is the meter's again: the solvent and its pH choose the product; the reagent only brings the demand.

The honest corpus note, printed where the register would want it. The survey register holds no permanganate face — no episode in the corpus works the purple crystal, and the census in Lecture 0 rests on the label class and the water-treatment literature, not on a practitioner's own footage. The chapter says so plainly, the way Chapter 15 said which faces were single-source: the permanganate bench is canon-plus-web, not corpus, and if the survey later gathers a face, it inherits a chapter that left the slot honestly open.

Lecture 5 — The keepers' network: decay, dating, and the tray that saves the house

Every oxidizer on the census is already spending itself. The keepers' discipline of Chapter 15 transfers whole — the label is a prediction about the past; the dated assay is the only sentence that knows the present — and this shelf's decay chemistry is the recognition course Chapter 28 will teach at hazard level. The ledger prices it (Lab 16.2, B5): hypochlorite decays at a few percent per month at room temperature (3%/month, the storage class [convergent]), and the 5.25% jug computes to 3.64% after a year — a jug at under seven-tenths of its label. The decay's two doors are the lesson: the oxygen door (2 OCl⁻ → 2 Cl⁻ + O₂, the reason old jug caps pop and the top of the bottle fizzes on opening) and the chlorate door (3 OCl⁻ → 2 Cl⁻ + ClO₃⁻, the same disproportionation as the boiling pot, running cold and slow) — which is the recognition datum this chapter contributes to Chapter 28: **old bleach is doubly dangerous — weaker than its label says, and quietly carrying the chlorate the label never mentioned.** The old jug gets dated, diluted, used for laundry or down the drain with plenty of water; it never gets "re-strengthened," and it never migrates toward anything organic.

The peroxide bottle decays the same way, slower (its self-push is Lecture 2's −211 kJ, spent through a kinetic door — the dark bottle, the venting cap, the cool shelf, and the ppm-level stabilizer are the packaging that buys the years; Chapter 25 inherits the decay as kinetics homework). The purple crystal and the dry boxes barely decay at all — and that is the storage hierarchy the census ranks: the solid oxidizers keep; the liquids spend; the strongest shelf is the driest one.

The separation that protects the product also protects the house, and it is pure solubility arithmetic (Lab 16.2's B4): potassium chlorate crystallizes because its curve is a cliff — 7.3 g/100 g cold against 56.7 hot, an 87.1% recovery on any cooling run — while potassium chloride (34.2 g/100 g cold) and sodium chlorate (~101 g/100 g, 13.8× the product) stay behind in the mother liquor. The lesson the ledger writes for the network: never evaporate chlorate liquor to dryness — the last crop on the evaporating dish is the one that dries into the state the binding line refuses; the wet, cold, filtered crystal is the only state this book schedules. And the final audit-only wall, inherited from Chapter 15 and re-derived in this session's own engine (Lab 16.2's B6): the peroxide-plus-sulfuric door — Caro's acid — walls its product at 9.26% H₂SO₅, 0.86 M, and the ceiling is the reason the oxone bucket exists: the shelf sells the peroxymonosulfate class pre-made, dry, stable, and polite, and the Atlas buys it there — the from-scratch door stays shut, priced and labeled audit-only.
