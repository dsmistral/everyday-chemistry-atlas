Vol I · Chapter 4 — Water and Solutions

Chapter 1 built the lab, Chapter 2 filled its shelves and taught you to read every label, Chapter 3 explained what the chemicals are. This chapter is about what happens next, at the bench, almost every single time: you put something in water. The teaspoon of salt vanishes; the vinegar bottle's 5% becomes a number you can use; the kettle's scale refuses to leave until the vinegar arrives; the ice-cream bag freezes cream on a hot day using nothing but salt. Solutions are where the atom-counting of Chapter 3 turns into working arithmetic — and the household is one long shelf of solutions, declared by percentage, priced by the gallon, and behaving exactly as this chapter's ledger says they must.

The Atlas treats solution chemistry the only way the premise allows: every concentration claim is machine-verified in the labs (the same honest-number contract as Chapters 1–3), the kitchen's own calibrated drop is a volumetric instrument (the corpus's gravimetric calibration, N63), and the model's limits are stated where they sit — the azeotrope ceiling, the ion-pairing discount, the eutectic where the dilute law simply ends. The corpus's practitioners supply the phenomena the theory must explain: the titration culture (N57), the drop-count calibration (N63), the purification that meets its ceiling (N46, N83), the hydrate ladder (N86), and the precipitation tricks that Volume II will perform on purpose (N16, N28).

Lecture 0 — The disappearing teaspoon (before any notation)

Fill a drinking glass to the brim with water. Now, slowly, add a level teaspoon of table salt. The pile breaks the surface — and the glass does not overflow. Stir, and the crystals vanish: the water is clear again, and tastes of nothing you may check, because the Charter forbids bench tasting — the spoon goes to the sink, not the tongue. Two measurements, both pencil-and-paper, and both are the lecture:

First: weigh everything. Glass + water + salt before stirring: write the mass. After the salt vanishes: weigh again. The mass is exactly conserved — the teaspoon's worth of matter went nowhere at all. "Dissolved" does not mean "disappeared"; it means mixed down to the molecular scale (Chapter 3's ions and molecules are still there, every last one — the balance vouches for them).

Second: look at the water line. The full glass held ~1 mm of proud tension above the rim; a level teaspoon of salt is ~6 g ≈ 2.6 mL of crystal volume — yet the overflow was a film, not a flood. The dissolving salt's particles pack into the water's own open structure; the volume of a solution is not the sum of its parts' volumes. Mass conserved, volume not additive: a solution is a single phase with its own bookkeeping, not "water plus stuff."

Now read the under-sink shelf with this chapter's eyes. Vinegar: a solution — acetic acid in water, declared 5%. Hydrogen peroxide: a solution, 3%. Bleach: a solution, 5.25% sodium hypochlorite. Muriatic acid: a solution, 31.45%. Iodine tincture (the corpus's drugstore substitution, N61): iodine dissolved in alcohol–water. Maple syrup, soda, the brine jar: all solutions. The solvent is the phase that does the dissolving — water in almost every Atlas case, and water is what this chapter is about; the solute is what got dissolved; and concentration — how much solute per how much mixture — is the single number that decides what the bottle can do. The whole chapter is one long answer to two questions the teaspoon asked: where did it go (nowhere — it is counted), and how much of it is here (that number, computed, every time).

Lecture 1 — Concentration: one conversion web

Chapter 2's labels were percent by mass (w/w) — grams of solute per 100 g of solution. This chapter adds the three units the rest of chemistry uses, and the point of this lecture is that they are one conversion web, not four systems:

  • Molarity (M): moles of solute per liter of solution. The reaction unit — stoichiometry runs on moles, and the bench works in liters.
  • Molality (m): moles of solute per kilogram of solvent. The temperature-stable unit — the kilogram of solvent does not expand when heated; the liter of solution does (Lecture 5's colligative work runs on molality for exactly this reason).
  • Mole fraction (x): moles of solute divided by total moles of everything. The particle-counter's unit — Lecture 5 will show that nature counts particles, and x is the count written as a fraction.

A label's w/w becomes any of them through two pieces of data: the molar mass (Chapter 3's engine) and the density (Chapter 2's other label line). The chain, run on 1.000 L of solution: mass of solution = 1000ρ; solute mass = w × 1000ρ; solvent mass = the rest; divide by molar masses; done. Dilution is the web's one moving part. The count of solute is fixed; concentration trades against volume: C1V1 = C2V2, always (Chapter 1's Lab 1.2 verified the bijection at 50,820 cases; Lab 4.1 restates the working rules). The 10× rule — 1 part stock + 9 parts water — and the 100× rule are the same equation in kitchen form. Two disciplines make the operation safe and the number honest:

  1. Acid into water, ALWAYS — never water into acid. Dilution releases heat; the water-filled vessel absorbs it, but a drop of water landing on concentrated acid flashes to steam at the surface and spatters acid. The Charter's dilution line is absolute (the safety table at this chapter's end).
  2. Know your drop. The corpus calibrated the disposable pipette gravimetrically (N63: 100 drops = 3.62 g of water → 0.0363 mL/drop, 27.5 drops/mL [computed and matched to Ch 1's re-derivation at 27.57]). Ten milliliters is 275.5 counted drops; one drop of 10.00 M muriatic stock into 100.0 mL of water makes 0.00363 M [computed, Lab 4.1] — the drop-count kitchen range, honest to its own precision, with the graduated cylinder taking over beyond it. The field's other concentration practice is the serial-halving ladder (N74's colorimetry methodology, matched to Chapter 1): 150 → 75 → 37.5 → 18.75 → 9.375 mg/dL by successive halving — concentration control by nothing but a container and a plan.

The w/w trap, generalized. Chapter 2 computed the cost of reading 32% HCl as w/w when the label meant w/v (10.18 M vs 8.78 M). The general rule this chapter owns: a percentage without its basis is not a number yet. w/w needs the density; w/v needs no density but hides a rounding; "5% acetic acid" on a vinegar label is w/w by convention [convergent — the US retail convention] — and the corpus's own copper-compounds episode priced it as "≈ 0.87 M" (N58's rounded figure; the label arithmetic gives 0.838 M [computed] — the gap is the source's rounding, recorded here so no reader inherits it silently).

The ceiling. Concentration is not unbounded, and one ceiling is chemistry's own: the azeotrope. Distilling a hydrochloric-acid solution cannot pass ~20% HCl [convergent] — at that composition the vapor has the same composition as the liquid, and the still returns the same mixture forever. The corpus's hardware-store purification episode (N46) meets exactly this wall, and the Australian 32% bottle (N83) documents the purity-at-concentration tradeoff: you may keep the concentration or purify past the ceiling, not both. The 31.45% muriatic bottle survives at full strength only because it is bottled as manufactured, never distilled. Water the solvent is generous up to a point; past the azeotrope it refuses to let its partner go.

Lecture 2 — Why water dissolves things (and why it refuses)

Chapter 3 built the molecule: water is bent, with two polar O–H bonds whose pulls do not cancel — a strongly polar molecule, its oxygen end negative, its hydrogen ends positive. In the liquid, each water molecule hydrogen-bonds to its neighbors in a flickering, constantly re-forming network [convergent — the structural picture of liquid water]. That polarity is the engine of this lecture: dissolution is a tug-of-war, and water pulls with its dipoles.

Salt, ledgered. A sodium chloride crystal is Chapter 3's ionic lattice: Na+ and Cl− locked in all directions. Drop it in water and the dipoles crowd in — oxygens facing the sodiums, hydrogens facing the chlorines. Each ion that gets fully surrounded (a hydration shell) is dressed in water and cut loose from the lattice; the LED glass of Chapter 3's sorter lights because those dressed ions are mobile charge. The ledger has two competing accounts — the lattice's ion-ion attraction versus the hydration's ion-water attraction — and dissolution happens when hydration wins. The tally is measurable (thermochemistry owns the full ledger in Volume II); the Atlas states the outcome and its price: dissolving salts absorbs or releases heat depending on which account wins, which is why the dilution rule of Lecture 1 has a thermal face.

Sugar, embraced. Sucrose makes no ions — but its molecule is studded with polar O–H and C–O groups (Chapter 3's Δχ ledger computed them). Water's dipoles embrace those groups exactly as they embrace each other, and whole molecules lift off the crystal into solution — no ions, no LED, the sorter's silent row. The distinction that matters at the bench: salt water conducts because the solute changed identity in solution (ions); sugar water does not because it did not (molecules, intact).

Grease, refused. A nonpolar solute offers water's dipoles nothing to hold. Squeezing nonpolar molecules in costs the water network hydrogen bonds it would rather keep with itself — so the oil floats as a separate layer and the pan needs soap (Chapter 5's surfactants are polar-and-nonpolar janitors, engineered around exactly this refusal). "Like dissolves like" is Chapter 3's polarity ledger read at the sink.

Electrolytes: the sorter reread. Chapter 3's sorter sorted the household into conducts-always / conducts-when-dissolved / never-conducts. Solution chemistry gives the classes their working names:

  • Strong electrolytes — solutes that come apart completely into ions in water: NaCl, and the muriatic bottle's HCl (10 M of acid is 10 M of H+ and 10 M of Cl−, nothing else). For colligative counting they contribute their full ion count: i = 2 per formula unit for both.
  • Weak electrolytes — solutes that come apart only partially: acetic acid, the vinegar bottle, is mostly intact molecules at any moment, with a small fraction dissociated. This is why vinegar's LED glows faintly where muriatic's blazes (Chapter 3's sorter saw it; the equilibrium that sets the fraction is Chapter 8's to compute — a flagged forward reference, no number printed here).
  • Nonelectrolytes — solutes that never make ions: sugar, peroxide (their solutions conduct only at the impurity level).

The class label is not decoration; it is the multiplier i — the van 't Hoff factor — that Lecture 5 will cash: a mole of dissolved strong electrolyte counts as ~2 moles of particles for every physical effect that counts particles. The honest zero of this lecture: at real concentrations the count runs a little below the ideal (ions pair up briefly — the ion-pairing discount, quantified in Lab 4.2's seawater case), and the chapter says so every time i appears.

Lecture 3 — How much dissolves: saturation, the curves, and the hydrate trap

Saturation is a rate standoff. Put more salt in than water can hold, and two processes run at once: ions leaving the crystal, and ions landing back on it. When the two rates match, the crystal sits unchanged in a saturated solution — not inert, but balanced. The standoff's position is the solubility, and for crystalline solids in water it rises with temperature (hot water jiggles the lattice harder), by an amount that is wildly different from solute to solute — the fact of this lecture:

  • NaCl: the flat curve. 35.9 g per 100 g of water at 25 °C; 35.7 g at 0 °C; 39.1 g at 100 °C [convergent]. From ice-cold to boiling, table salt's solubility barely moves. (Saturated brine at Lab 4.2's conversions: 359 g/L of water, 26.42% w/w, 6.143 mol/kg [computed].)
  • KNO3: the steep curve. 13.3 g at 0 °C; 31.6 g at 25 °C; 247 g at 100 °C [convergent]. A 19-fold climb — and the stumper-remover aisle's gift to the home chemist (Ex 4.4's purification).
  • Sucrose: the enormous curve. ~200 g per 100 g at room temperature, headed past 500 g hot [convergent]. Candy-making is applied solubility-temperature chemistry; the sugar chemist and the salt maker work the same curves at different altitudes.
  • The nearly-insoluble end. Gypsum (CaSO4·2H2O, the drywall mineral of N64's route) holds at 0.24 g per 100 g of water [convergent] — and the corpus's copper-carbonate datum names the territory: the carbonate that Episode 004 precipitated from root killer would need on the order of two hundred thousand liters of water to dissolve its batch [single-source corpus, N59; qualitative name-check only — Chapter 9 derives numbers of this kind from Ksp]. "Insoluble" is a region of this curve, not a different world — Lecture 4 gives it its numbers.

Recrystallization is the steep curve weaponized. Dissolve KNO3 hot (where water holds 247 g per 100 g), cool (where it holds 31.6 g): the excess must leave solution, and it leaves as clean crystals — the impurities stay behind in the mother liquor, the residual saturated solution. Lab 4.2's mass balances: 247 g dissolved at 100 °C gives up 215.4 g of crystals (87.2%) on cooling to 20 °C, 233.7 g (94.6%) to 0 °C [computed]; the same recipe run on NaCl gives up only 3.4 g (8.7%) — the flat curve refuses the method. This is the P2 separation train at its cleanest: the curve's steepness IS the method.

The hydrate trap, priced again. Chapter 2 taught the anhydrous-versus-hydrate weighing trap for prices; solubility tables restate it for recipes: tables quote the anhydrous salt (MgSO4: 35.7 g per 100 g [convergent, table range 35.1–36.4 flagged — the 20 °C sources vary]), but the store jar holds the heptahydrate (Epsom salt, the dehydration ladder of N86's drying-agent episode). The same dissolution weighed with its waters: 35.7 g of MgSO4 = 73.1 g of Epsom salt per 100 g of water [computed, Lab 4.2 — the 2.0478 mass ratio doing Chapter 2's work at the recipe scale]. The notebook rule: every solubility number carries its form's name, because the crystal's waters ride along into solution.

Lecture 4 — "Insoluble" is a number

The lecture's one-sentence program: there are no insoluble substances, only small solubilities — and the small number is on the ledger.

A saturated solution in contact with its undissolved solid is Lecture 3's rate standoff, and standoffs have an arithmetic. For calcium carbonate:

CaCO3(s) ⇌ Ca2+ + CO3^2-

At 25 °C, the equilibrium fixes the product of the ion concentrations: [Ca2+][CO3^2−] = 3.36 × 10⁻⁹ — the solubility product, Ksp [convergent]. The ideal-model engine of Lab 4.2 turns constants into solubilities: with one Ca2+ per CaCO3 and one CO3^2−, each equals s, so s² = Ksp and s = 5.797 × 10⁻⁵ M = 5.802 × 10⁻³ g/L [computed] — about six milligrams in a liter of pure water. The battery's other faces: AgCl (1.330 × 10⁻⁵ M), BaSO4 (1.039 × 10⁻⁵ M) — the chemist's classic "insolubles" all carry three- or four-digit nanomolar-to-micromolar numbers; and Mg(OH)2, where the stoichiometry enters the constant's shape — two hydroxides per magnesium, so Ksp = 4s³ and s = 1.119 × 10⁻⁴ M [computed]. Same lens, different algebra, and the algebra is the composition's shadow — Chapter 3's atom counts reappearing as exponents.

The honest label on the model. Ksp arithmetic assumes the dissolved ions float free. At real concentrations they pair up briefly (Lecture 2's discount), so measured solubilities run a little above the ideal s — the model's signature carries the caveat [convergent + flagged: ideal model, ion pairing ignored]. The chapter uses the model the way the premise uses every model: stated, bounded, and honest about the seam.

Equilibrium shifts are the working tool. The Ksp equation is not a verdict, it is a lever:

  • Pull a product ion out, and more solid dissolves. Acid converts CO3^2− to HCO3− and onward to CO2 gas — the carbonate ion is consumed on the solution side, the product [Ca2+][CO3^2−] drops below Ksp, and the solid must dissolve to try to restore it. Repeat until the scale or the acid runs out: this is the kettle descale of Ex 4.5, computed to the milliliter (Lab 4.2's capstone chain), with the CO2 bubbling away removing product and driving the shift further (the shift-lever has a name, Le Chatelier's principle, and Chapter 8 owns its full treatment).
  • Push a product ion in, and solid comes out. Add carbonate to a copper solution and CuCO3 drops from the clear liquid (the corpus's carbonate hub, N30 — root killer plus baking soda, the precipitation the copper-compounds chain ran on). The corpus's presumptive-testing cluster (Chapter 13's forensic panel) is this lever run at the spot-plate scale: each confirmatory precipitate is a Ksp verdict in color.
  • The synthesis engine. The corpus's strongest trick is the lever run deliberately to make acid: oxalic acid plus a nitrate salt drops the calcium out as insoluble calcium oxalate, and the acid stays in solution — an acid made by precipitation, no distillation (N16's nitric acid, N28's sulfuric version — the P14 pattern, Volume II/Ch 9 performs it at the bench). "Insoluble" is not the end of chemistry; it is one of its best motors.

This lecture's bridge: Chapter 9 (the carbonate hub and the metathesis network) runs entirely on this lever, forward and backward. The Atlas flags the handoff and keeps only the lens here.

Lecture 5 — What the solute does to the solvent (the colligative effects)

The surprise of this lecture is old news to anyone who has salted a driveway: dissolved particles change the solvent's own physical properties — and the change counts particles, not mass or identity. Freeze a sugar solution and a salt solution of equal molarity and the salt (two ions per formula) depresses the freezing point twice as far as the sugar (one molecule). Count particles, change water: the family of effects — freezing-point depression, boiling-point elevation, vapor-pressure lowering (flagged qualitative here; its full law, Raoult's, belongs to the formal track), and osmosis — are called colligative, "depending on the collection."

The working law, in this chapter's three units at once:

ΔTf = i · Kf · m

  • m — Lecture 1's molality, chosen because the solvent's kilogram does not expand when heated;
  • i — Lecture 2's sorter multiplier: the particle count per formula unit (NaCl ≈ 2, sugar exactly 1, the strong/weak electrolyte classes deciding);
  • Kf = 1.86 °C·kg/mol for water [convergent], with its boiling-side partner Kb = 0.512 °C·kg/mol.

The cashing of Exercise 3.5. Chapter 3's summit weighed 4.00 g of salt against 4.00 g of sugar and found a 5.857× particle-count gap. Run both through the freezing-point ledger at 10.0 g per 500 g of water: NaCl gives ΔTf = 1.273 °C, sucrose 0.109 °C — 11.7× per gram [computed, Lab 4.2: the 5.857× gap doubled by i]. The promised arithmetic lands: per gram, an electrolyte is worth its molar-mass discount times its ion count, and the salt marches.

The applications, each a computed case in Lab 4.2:

  • The ice-cream bag. Ice plus coarse salt makes a brine that cannot stay frozen at 0 °C — it melts the ice at the cost of its own heat, and the mixture falls to the NaCl/ice eutectic, −21.1 °C at 23.3% w/w [convergent]. The bag of cream, surrounded by a medium 21 degrees below ice's melt, freezes solid while the outside stays liquid. The ideal dilute law would have predicted −22.9 °C for saturated brine [computed] — it overshoots, and past the eutectic it simply ends: extra salt sits as a second solid phase and no dilute-law reading exists. The model states where it stops; the ice-cream bag lives at the edge.
  • Seawater. 35 g of salt per kg: ideal i = 2 predicts a freezing point of −2.23 °C; the observed is −1.9 °C [convergent] — implying i_eff = 1.71: at that concentration ~15% of the ion count is discounted by pairing [computed]. The oceans are a full-scale nonideality experiment, and the number is honest about the model's reach.
  • The pasta myth. A generous pot: 17 g of salt in 4.0 L. The boiling point rises 0.074 °C [computed] — one-hundredth of the kitchen thermometer's resolution. To buy a full +1.00 °C you would need 57.1 g per kg of water — ~228 g in the pot, inedible [computed, Ex 4.3]. Salt the water for the taste, never for the temperature: the same ledger that runs the driveway and the ice-cream bag refuses the folklore, politely and to the third decimal place.
  • Osmosis, named. A membrane that passes water but not solute makes the particle count itself a pump: π = iMRT (qualitative in the Atlas; one ideal illustration: seawater's ~0.6 M at i = 2 is ~29 atm of pull [computed as the ideal bound] — why seawater cannot rehydrate you: the membrane fights the particle count, not the mass). The formal track owns the derivation; the household owns the phenomenon.

The honest close (flagged model). The colligative laws are dilute laws: their accuracy decays as concentration climbs (seawater's 15% discount is the decay measured), they end at saturation, and the eutectic ends them with a phase boundary. This is the same verification-ladder discipline as Chapter 3's quantum sketch: the Atlas says what the model is, what it misses, and where the bench number and the model part ways — every time they do.

4.1 ●○○ — A cup of vinegar, counted. One US measuring cup of distilled white vinegar: 237 mL, 5% w/w acetic acid, ρ 1.006 g/mL. (a) Grams of acetic acid in the cup; (b) moles; (c) baking soda is stirred in until the fizzing stops — grams of NaHCO3 consumed, and the volume of CO2 released at RTP (24.5 L/mol). Hint: the label is w/w, so the cup must be weighed before it can be counted. Answer: (a) 237 × 1.006 = 238.4 g of solution × 0.05 = 11.92 g [computed, Lab 4.1's chain at cup scale]; (b) 11.92 / 60.052 = 0.1985 mol [computed]; (c) CH3COOH + NaHCO3 → CH3COONa + H2O + CO2 is 1:1, so 0.1985 × 84.006 = 16.68 g of baking soda, and 0.1985 × 24.5 = 4.86 L of CO2 at RTP [computed, Lab 4.2's exercise block] — measure the soda by mass, not by spoon: the fizzing stop is the reaction's own end-point reading (the N57 titration culture's endpoint at kitchen scale). Second way: the mass-factor route, no moles at all — 11.92 × (84.006/60.052) = 16.68 g, identical [computed] — the label arithmetic (P1) run through the reaction's mass ratio, cross-checking the mole ledger (P12). The reaction itself is the acid-on-carbonate hub (P4's family, N30's metathesis — Chapter 9 performs this chemistry forward).

4.2 ●○○ — Dilution from the stock bottle. The under-sink muriatic is 31.45% w/w (the Ch 2 label: 10.00 M at label grade). (a) The recipe for 250.0 mL of 1.000 M dilute acid; (b) at drop scale — one drop of stock into 100.0 mL of water gives what molarity, and how many drops is 10.0 mL; (c) the two safety rules that govern the pour. Hints: the solute count is the invariant; N63's calibrated drop is 0.0363 mL. Answer: (a) V1 = C2V2/C1 = (1.000 × 250.0)/10.00 = 25.0 mL of stock, poured into ~200 mL of water, then topped to 250.0 mL [computed, Lab 4.1's ladder]; (b) one drop → 10.00 × 0.0363/(100.0 + 0.0363) = 0.00363 M [computed], and 10.0 mL = 275.5 drops (0.0363 mL/drop, N63) — at the drop scale the solution is honest to its own digit, and beyond ~10 mL the graduated cylinder takes over [computed]; (c) acid into water, never water into acid (the dilution's heat must meet the water's heat capacity, not the acid's surface — the splash rule is absolute), and never a sealed or stoppered vessel (no gas, no pressure, but thermal expansion and bumping argue the same rule). Ways: the C1V1 = C2V2 invariant (P12) and the 10× dilution-factor rule read straight off the label pair (P1: 250.0/25.0 = the 10× rule in disguise); the drop calibration is N63's gravimetric face (P9/P11 — the pipette calibrated by mass to a stated precision).

4.3 ●●○ — The pasta-salt myth, computed. The folklore: salted water boils "faster" or "hotter." A generous cook salts 4.0 L of pasta water with 17 g of coarse salt. (a) The boiling-point change; (b) invert the ledger — grams of salt needed to raise the boiling point a full 1.00 °C in that pot; (c) the same brine's freezing-point change, and where that number is visible. Hints: i counts; Kb is 0.512; the kitchen thermometer is ±1 °C. Answer: (a) m = (17/58.440)/4.0 = 0.0727 mol/kg; ΔTb = i·Kb·m = 2 × 0.512 × 0.0727 = 0.074 °C [computed, Lab 4.2] — one-hundredth of the thermometer's resolution: invisible, uncookable; (b) m_needed = 1.00/(2 × 0.512) = 0.9766 mol/kg → 0.9766 × 58.440 = 57.1 g per kg of water ≈ 228 g in the 4-L pot — inedible several times over [computed]; (c) ΔTf = 2 × 1.86 × 0.0727 = 0.27 °C [computed] — the same arithmetic that is hopeless in the pot is the working physics of the salted driveway and the ice-cream bag (Lecture 5's eutectic, at dilute scale). Ways: the colligative ledger run forward and inverted (P12 both directions — the inversion is the P12 habit); the instrument-resolution argument is P9 (the honest limit of the measuring device is part of the verdict, not a footnote to it).

4.4 ●●○ — Saltpetre refined (the gardener's purification). Stump-remover KNO3 needs recrystallization. In 100 g of boiling water, 247 g dissolve. (a) Grams of clean crystals recovered on cooling to 20 °C, and to 0 °C — the recovery percentages; (b) the identical recipe run on table salt (39.1 g dissolve at 100 °C); (c) why the method serves one solute and refuses the other, and what to do with the mother liquor. Hints: what does the water still hold at each temperature? The curve's steepness is the yield. Answer: (a) 20 °C holds 31.6 g → crystals 215.4 g = 87.2%; 0 °C holds 13.3 g → 233.7 g = 94.6% [computed, Lab 4.2]; (b) 0 °C still holds 35.7 of the 39.1 g → 3.4 g = 8.7% — salt is barely recrystallizable at any kitchen temperature; (c) recrystallization is the solubility curve's steepness weaponized — a temperature-controlled separation (P2, the separation train) whose yield is the curve's slope (KNO3's 19-fold climb vs NaCl's 10% wiggle), and the mother liquor (the saturated solution that holds the retained salt plus the impurities) is poured off, never chased — chasing it re-dissolves your product (the second way to see the yield: the mass balance crystals = dissolved − retained, checked both directions [P12]). Safety: KNO3 is an oxidizer — the hot concentrated solution keeps away from paper, sugar, and charcoal scraps, and the recovered crystals are capped, labeled, and stored away from organics (the safety table's row).

4.5 ●●● — The kettle descale (the chapter's summit). Electric-kettle scale is CaCO3. The bench: distilled white vinegar, 5% w/w (Lab 4.1's 0.8377 M). A 5.00 g scale deposit. (a) The milliliters of vinegar needed; (b) why "insoluble" CaCO3 dissolves in weak acid at all — the equilibrium argument, from Ksp; (c) the CO2 check; (d) the vessel rule. Hints: two acids per carbonate; Lecture 4's lever; the gas must go somewhere. Answer: (a) 5.00/100.086 = 0.0500 mol CaCO3 → needs 2 × 0.0500 = 0.0999 mol HAc → 0.0999/0.8377 = 119 mL of vinegar, about half a cup [computed, Lab 4.2's capstone chain]; the cup check: a full cup (237 mL = 0.199 mol HAc) covers 9.9 g of scale — a whole cup is the safe over-pour that still cannot hurt; (b) in pure water, Ksp caps CaCO3 at 5.80 × 10⁻³ g/L [computed, Lab 4.2] — but acid converts CO3^2− to HCO3− and on to CO2 + H2O, consuming the carbonate ion on the solution side; the product [Ca2+][CO3^2−] drops below Ksp and the solid must dissolve to restore it; each restored bit is stripped again — the reaction runs until scale or acid is exhausted, with the escaping CO2 removing product the whole way (Le Chatelier's lever, Lecture 4). Run the lever in reverse — add carbonate ions — and CuCO3-style solids drop from clear solutions (N30's carbonate hub, the precipitation the corpus performed); and run it sideways — remove a different metal ion as an insoluble salt — and acid can be made (P14's precipitation-driven liberation, N16/N28 — the Volume II/Ch 9 motor, flagged here, performed there); (c) 0.0500 mol CO2 = 2.20 g = 1.22 L at RTP [computed] — a kettle-sized balloon of gas from a 5-gram deposit: proof in volume that the "insoluble" scale truly left; (d) never latched or closed — 1.22 L of gas must vent (the safety table's row; the Charter's sealed-vessel line). Ways: the stoichiometric ledger (P12) and the Ksp equilibrium lens (Lecture 4's lever, the P14 pattern's logic); the fizz itself is the in-progress verification (P9 — the reaction's own signal read as its endpoint, the N57 culture).

4.6 ◆ — The unidentified white crystal (no hints; diagnose the tool, then decide). From the back of the pantry: a jar of white crystals, label gone. Tasting is forbidden (the Charter's absolute rule — the pantry jar goes to the bench, not the tongue). 10.0 g dissolve completely in 500 g of water. The improvised thermometer reads the solution's freezing point at −1.27 °C. Identify the solute, and name the bench tests that confirm it.

Worked answer (diagnosis first). The tool is the colligative ledger with one unknown too many: ΔTf = i·Kf·m gives i/M as a pair, never either alone. m_eff = 1.27/1.86 = 0.6828 mol/kg [computed]. Fork one: a nonelectrolyte (i = 1) would have M = 10.0/(0.6828 × 0.5) = 29.3 g/mol — no white household nonelectrolyte fits (sucrose 342.297; there is no 29 g/mol kitchen crystal). Fork two: a two-ion strong electrolyte (i = 2) would have M = 2 × 29.3 = 58.6 g/mol ≈ NaCl at 58.440 — agreement to the method's own rounding (the improvised thermometer's last digit covers the 0.3% gap) → table salt. The honest fork rule: freezing point alone cannot break the i/M tie — the confirmations are orthogonal and take seconds: the Ch 3 sorter (a level teaspoon in water, battery + LED — a strong electrolyte lights it; a nonelectrolyte cannot), and the flame test (a few grains at the flame's edge — the intense sodium yellow of Ex 3.5's cross-contamination warning, run deliberately this once). Salt, by convergence. Ways: the colligative ledger with its i-fork (P12) and P9's orthogonal convergence (two independent bench observations settling one verdict — and the honest zero stated: the fp reading alone underdetermines the answer, which is itself a datum).
