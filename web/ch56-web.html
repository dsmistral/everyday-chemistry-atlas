Lecture 0 — The Exotic-Metals Pencil Case (three metals that misbehave)

The annex frame. "Exotic" on this book's terms is not a price tag or a cleanroom — it is a material whose bulk behavior breaks the pattern the first forty-nine chapters taught you to expect. Metals melt and shrink into the mold; bismuth swells. Liquids wet and level; a ferrofluid stands spikes against gravity. Gold is gold-colored; AuAl2 is purple. Each axis in this annex is a single anomaly, followed from the shelf to the arithmetic that explains it, with the register's own reads as the spine and the exact-fraction engine as the witness. Nothing here requires a supply house beyond eBay pellets, a welding shop, and a pharmacy — that is the annex's claim, and its limits are named as we go.

The pencil case. Three specimens, three lessons. Bismuth — the metal you can melt on a kitchen flame that then freezes bigger than the mold, the only chapter in the book where the cast swells; its lecture is the solidification anomaly and the oxide rainbow. Magnetite — not the lodestone of Chapter 22's pigment shelf but a ten-nanometer colloid, the only chapter where the metal is the particle; its lecture is the surfactant triad and why a 5.36% yield is a lesson, not a failure. And the gold-aluminum intermetallic — the only chapter where the prize is a color, purple, that no pure-metal shelf produces; its lecture is the patent, the stoichiometry hidden inside the recipe, and the anneal. The three share one moral the annex will state plainly at the end: the exotic part is never the material — it is the discipline.

Exercises

56.1 (●) The derived datum. From gold's molar mass 196.97 g/mol and aluminum's 26.98 g/mol, show that the AuAl2 intermetallic's gold mass fraction is the patent's "typical 78.5%" — and that the aluminum complement sums with it to the whole. Hint: one gold atom, two aluminum atoms, and a fraction that must be reduced with a witness. Worked: the denominator 196.97 + 2×26.98 = 250.93; the gold fraction 196.97/250.93 expands to exactly 78.5 and the aluminum fraction 53.96/250.93 to exactly 21.5, and 78.5 + 21.5 = 100 — the patent recipe and the intermetallic stoichiometry are the same numbers (computed: Lab 56.1, B2-1..B2-3). The patent's authors did not pick a compromise; they picked the compound.

56.2 (●) The karat ladder. Convert the patent's 78.5% composition and the video's 81% recipe to karats, and place 18K, 19K, and 24K on the mass-fraction line. Hint: karat is 24 × mass fraction — a multiplication, not a lookup. Worked: 24 × 0.785 = 18.84 ("18 karat" in the literature's round phrase) and 24 × 0.81 = 19.44 ("about 19 karat" in the video's own); 18K = 0.750 exactly, 19K = 19/24 = 0.790 at two places, 24K = 1.000 (computed: Lab 56.1, B1-1..B1-5). Note what the ladder teaches: the two recipes sit one karat apart on a scale whose steps are 1/24 = 4.17% of the mass.

56.3 (●) The digit-transposition arbiter. The video's caption says "exactly 1.61 g" of aluminum against 4.95 g of gold; the recipe-consistent charge is 4.95 × 19/81. Decide what each reading implies, and state what the register does with the discrepancy. Hint: compute both mass fractions — and remember which one the video's own recipe wants. Worked: the recipe-consistent charge is 1.16 g (4.95 × 19/81), giving 4.95/(4.95+1.16) = 4.95/6.11 = 81% gold — the "about 19 karat" recipe; the caption's 1.61 g gives 4.95/(4.95+1.61) = 4.95/6.56 = 75.46% at two places (computed: Lab 56.1, B2-4..B2-7). The register records both and flags the tension; the chapter does not resolve it — a caption is evidence of what was weighed, the recipe is evidence of what was intended, and only the maker knows which sat on the scale.

56.4 (◐) The charge-ratio failure. The first ferrofluid batch charged 54 g FeCl3 (162.20 g/mol) against 27.8 g FeSO4·7H2O (278.02 g/mol). Compute the ferric-to-ferrous mole ratio, compare it with the 2:1 the atom ledger requires, and carry the arithmetic through to the batch's 5.36% yield. Hint: two mole amounts, one ratio, and a theoretical mass that must be computed from the limiting side — and watched for which batch size it describes. Worked: 54/162.20 = 0.3329 mol Fe3+ and 27.8/278.02 = 0.1000 mol Fe2+; the ratio is 3.33:1 against the required 2.00:1 — a two-thirds ferric overdose (computed: Lab 56.1, B3-1..B3-4). The theoretical magnetite is 0.1000 × 231.53 = 23.15 g full-batch, 11.58 g half-batch, and the 0.62 g recovered is 5.36% of the half-batch (computed: Lab 56.1, B3-5..B3-7). The half-versus-full distinction is the exercise's real trap: the yield is honest only against the batch that was actually run — the rev-1 harness slipped on exactly this literal, and the disclosure is the lesson.

56.5 (●) The swelling cast. A 1,000-g bismuth melt is poured at the liquid density 9.78 g/cm³ and freezes with the solidification expansion of 3.32%. Compute both volumes and the molar volume, and state what the knife-maker must do differently because of the answer. Hint: the liquid and the solid have different densities — start by dividing, then multiply by the expansion. Worked: liquid 1000/9.78 = 102.25 cm³; solid 102.25 × 1.0332 = 105.64 cm³ — the cast swells by 3.39 cm³ (computed: Lab 56.1, B4-4/B4-5); the molar volume is 208.98/9.78 = 21.37 cm³/mol (computed: Lab 56.1, B4-3). The knife-maker's consequence: the mold fills tighter than the pour, the spine grows after the gate freezes, and the sprue must be cut where the swell will push, not where the pour left it.

56.6 (◆) The silver-surface mystery — diagnose the model. The purple bar comes out silver on top. Sanding does not restore the color; breaking the bar open shows purple inside; an HCl dip returns the surface to plain gold without touching the interior; and a 600 °C anneal for thirty minutes restores the purple surface. Using only these four observations, propose a mechanism the anneal is consistent with, and name what each control rules out. Hint: separate "the color is absent" from "the color is covered" — the controls do it for you. *Worked (model, not fact — the exercise's own grade): the break-open control shows the purple phase exists undisturbed below the surface — so the color was never destroyed, only masked; the HCl dip strips the mask (an acid-soluble surface layer, gold-rich or aluminum-depleted) back to plain gold without reaching the body — so the mask is on the intermetallic, not part of it; sanding fails because it abrades into the mask unevenly and leaves the depleted layer, or re-depletes as it heats — mechanical work does not re-equilibrate a surface; the 600 °C anneal restores the color, which is the signature of surface diffusion re-equilibrating the depleted layer into the bulk composition (the patent's own annealing instruction, the auto-caption's "on a kneeling" garble, the register's first annealing face [register]). The same-recipe-different-colors datum is then honest evidence of cooling-path sensitivity, not noise — and the gamma-phase unknown stays unknown.*
