Vol VI · Chapter 41 — Potassium: The Na+KCl Displacement

The household owns this element by the bag — the salt substitute on the spice shelf is potassium chloride, the potash chain of Chapter 14 runs from fireplace ash to caustic potash, and every banana and every fertilizer bag carries it — and the metal is the campaign's genuinely hard one, the map said so before this chapter was written. The corpus has faced it three times, and the three faces are the chapter: a displacement still that ran at potassium's own boiling point and produced something its maker would only call technically potassium; a KOH electrolysis cell that made the metal and then watched it vanish into its own melt; and a two-hundred-degree sidestep — magnesium and a tertiary-alcohol catalyst in a high-boiling solvent — that ends in shining spheres nobody weighed. Three benches, three honest verdicts, not one weighed gram: the campaign's first chapter with an empty yield column for the metal it is about, and the chapter that has to say why. The title reaction is the one the industry itself runs — sodium displacing potassium out of its own chloride at eight hundred and fifty degrees, uphill on paper at room temperature and profitable only because potassium leaves as vapor — and the chapter prices it at both temperatures, both routes, and both honest verdicts.

The metal: absent, exactly as the campaign expects. The alkali ladder, walked once at the book's own pins because the campaign's running figure demands it: lithium melts at 180.5 °C, sodium at 97.8, potassium at 63.5 — each step down the group melts lower, boils lower (1,342 °C, 882.94, 759.9), softens easier, and reacts with water faster. The two properties this chapter runs on are both ladder properties. First the melting point: potassium metal softens in a clenched hand and melts in hot tap water's territory — the metal is the easiest of the three to make molten and the hardest to keep. Second the density inversion: sodium at 0.968 barely floats on water; potassium at 0.862 floats high — and that same inversion, run against the right solvent instead of water, is the design principle of the NurdRage sidestep (Lecture 4): tetralin at 0.970 g/cm³ and 207 °C boiling [convergent] is dense enough to float sodium at its own working temperature but not dense enough to hold potassium down — the product rises, gathers, and coalesces into spheres at the interface, while the magnesium by-product (MgO 40.304, Sheet 1's pin) stays behind in the pot. The float design is one row of the chapter's battery (C29), and it is the whole reason the two-hundred-degree route belongs to potassium and not to sodium: Na 0.968 against tetralin 0.970 is a coin toss, K 0.862 against it is a verdict. [convergent: both densities and the solvent's; computed: the comparison]

The flame face, P9, worn once at recognition distance: potassium's flame line is 766 nm, the lilac of every schoolroom demo [convergent] — and the schoolroom's own complication is the honest lesson: the line is masked by sodium's 589 nm yellow at the first trace of contamination, and the demo-grade answer is the cobalt glass that blacks out the sodium line and passes the lilac (Chapter 13's flame scheme, Chapter 39's mask family — 589.0/589.6 worn as the campaign's own pins). Lecture 2 — The Displacement You Cannot Force (the uphill reaction, the volatility lever, and the temperature that reverses the verdict)

The campaign's route logic, P8, has run the same sentence four times: find the metal's ion in something the household owns, find a stronger metal to push it out. Potassium breaks the sentence in half. The pusher exists — sodium, the campaign's own first product, one rung lower on the E° ladder (−2.71 V against potassium's −2.93 [convergent]) — but the push is uphill at room temperature, and the bookkeeping that proves it is this section's spine. The reaction is one line: Na + KCl ⇌ NaCl + K. The two salts' formation free energies at the book's own reconstruction (JANAF/NIST at 298 K, Sheet 2's rows C04-C05) are ΔGf°(NaCl, s) = −384.07 kJ and ΔGf°(KCl, s) = −408.75 kJ — potassium's chloride is the more stable lattice by 24.69 kJ — so the reaction's ΔG°298 is +24.69 kJ, uphill (C06, formation route). The chapter's engine derives the same figure a second way from the same table's entropies and enthalpies (C07, the ΔH − TΔS route: ΔH +25.56 kJ at C09, ΔS +2.93 J/K over the condensed phases at C10 — the flat reaction, entropy nearly silent) and the two routes agree to the joule: +24,686 J both ways. A third route through the aqueous potentials (C08) prices the same verdict at +21.23 kJ — and the gap between 24.69 and 21.23 is not error but a named seam: the hydration free energies of the two aqueous ions, folded into the standard potentials, are not the salts' lattice energies, and the chapter prints both figures with the seam named rather than pretending one route. [computed, Engine 57, Sheet 2; convergent at the formation pins]

So the household's own strongest pusher cannot take potassium out of its chloride on a cold bench — and yet the industry does exactly this reaction at scale, and the corpus's own bench ran it in a pipe still. The lever is volatility, and it is the chapter's one big piece of equilibrium reasoning, P10. Potassium boils at 759.9 °C and sodium at 882.94 °C — the two metals are 123 degrees apart in their eagerness to leave as vapor, and the reaction's products and reactants include metals only as solids at room temperature, so the entropy column that was flat at 298 K (+2.93 J/K, C10) climbs steeply once the potassium product leaves as gas: with K(g) as product the entropy is +98.59 J/K (C11, the volatility term), and the same ΔH that was uphill at room temperature tips downhill with T. The engine prices the tip at the two setpoints the corpus's own bench used: at 760.00 °C (1,033.15 K) ΔG = −76.30 kJ (C12) and at 843.33 °C (1,116.48 K) ΔG = −84.51 kJ (C13). The industry's countercurrent towers run the same lever hotter still — around 850 °C, sodium metal trickling down against potassium vapor coming off, Le Chatelier run as architecture [convergent, three independent sources; the sources' own counterintuitive framing — potassium is the better reducing agent of the two, yet the reaction runs because the product metal leaves — is quoted in Sources]. And the chapter's Ellingham sketch is exactly this arithmetic plotted: a nearly-flat enthalpy line and a steep entropy line crossing near 1,090 K, labeled what it is — a sketch, two straight lines over a two-hundred-degree window, with the approximation named at both ends (ΔH and ΔS held at their 298 K values; no phase-change corrections past the potassium boiling point; the vapor treated as ideal). [computed; the sketch-disclosure is the P10 discipline worn honestly]

The lever has a cost, and the cost is the chapter's quietest trap. A displacement still does not deliver pure potassium vapor, because sodium is volatile too — at the 760 °C setpoint the bp-anchored Clausius-Clapeyron ladder (C24-C27, both ΔHvap pairs printed as a bracket: K 79.1/76.9, Na 97.4/97.7 kJ/mol [convergent bracket]) puts potassium's vapor pressure at about one atmosphere and sodium's at 0.30 — a ratio of roughly 3.3 — and at 843 °C about 2.0 against 0.70, a ratio of roughly 2.9. Three-to-one selectivity in the vapor phase is a fortune at industrial column count and a contamination sentence at bench scale: the distilled potassium carries sodium, the sodium carry-over is the campaign's first alloy problem, and Lecture 3's purity iteration is exactly what a 3:1 vapor ratio predicts. The vapor-pressure arithmetic is Exercise 41.4's gate, and the answer it forces is the honest one the corpus's bench reached by trial: distill twice, accept the alloy verdict, and say the word "technically" when the run is over. [computed, Engine 57, C24-C27; single-source: N173's three-run purity arc]

Lecture 4 — The Wall and the Sidestep (Davy's first metal, the melt that eats its product, and the two-hundred-degree route)

Potassium was the first metal electrolysis ever gave mankind — Davy, October 6, 1807, potash on a platinum electrode, weeks before the same apparatus gave him sodium [convergent; the campaign's own history face] — and the element's modern irony is that its melt is where the home route dies. The wall is one sentence, and the corpus stated it in 2016 before Scrap Science's 2023 run priced it on camera: potassium metal is soluble in molten potassium hydroxide. For sodium, the same solubility was Chapter 37's Castner tax — the Down-cell caveat that a cell whose product dissolves in its own electrolyte "cannot exceed half efficiency"; for potassium the tax is not a discount but a wall, and the wall's height is what this lecture walks. [convergent: the 2016 statement and the solubility datum; single-source: N173's 2016 wall statement at transcript level]

The KOH cell, at its own figures (N161). The melt was KOH at its own 360 °C melting point [convergent], doped across the run's iterations with K₂CO₃ and KCl in "varying mixtures" the transcript itself never quantifies — the register prices this honestly as an unmeasured melt, composition never pinned. The cell made metal: the cathode birthed potassium that floated on the melt's surface — the density ladder of Lecture 1 predicts exactly this (K 0.862 against the melt) — and the floating metal "goes pop," skittering and flaming at the melt-air line as it was born. And then the wall collected its fee, in the transcript's own frozen sequence: "we did actually make potassium metal... we just made it, and then it disappeared" — the float never gathered, the beads never coalesced into anything a spatula could lift, and the run's final ledger, stated in the register's cleanest honest zero since lithium's fence, closed at "I haven't got a single crumb of potassium out of this cell." The sidestep, at 200 °C (N274). 1The liquid-alloy line (new, this chapter)NaK is liquid at room temperature — the pumpable fire: every bead from a sodium-and-potassium bench is handled as possible alloy, never pipetted, never flushed, sand and isolation first, and a bead that stays liquid at room temperature is a diagnosis, not a curiosityN173's pipette-sucked NaK, "more vigorous than the sodium control"
2The furnace lineThe 760-843 °C operations are kiln-class heat plus a liquid-metal still: outdoor or open bench, face shield, long tools, and the still treated as expendable — the run's own thermal budget had 130 degrees of margin and no moreN173's brazed-joint budget (brass 1,652 °F vs sodium's bp 1,621 °F); N161's furnace casualty
3The molten-salt line (inherited, Ch 40)The KOH melt is bone-dry discipline at recognition distance: every melt in this chapter is corpus-demonstrated recognition-class, never a reader benchN161's KOH/K₂CO₃/KCl melt, composition never quantified — the honesty the line is written on
4The caustic-melt rowMolten KOH at 360 °C is the most caustic substance in this book: the corrosion and spatter casualty class is priced by the run that paid it, and the crucible choice is part of the safety, not a footnoteN161's furnace casualty and the stainless-steel apology
5The alkali-water rowPotassium reacts with cold water faster than sodium — recognition-class only: single-bead, at distance, over a dish that can take the splash; never a bulk test, never shavings, and the alloy row tightens this oneThe floating "goes pop" datum; the pipette bead's vigor
6The oil-storage rowAlkali metal is stored under mineral oil short-term — and the crust row below prices the clock: storage is a waiting room, not a homeN274's mineral-oil short-term storage; use-or-destroy
7The crust row (the peroxide discipline's hardest face)A yellow crust on stored potassium is KO₂, a shock-sensitive oxidizer: never scrape crusted metal, never drill it, never cap a vessel with crusted metal under the lid — use or destroy within months, the crust is a loaded springThe KO₂ lecture (N274, transcript level); the UNR EHS and NaK handling sheets
8The never-sealed retort lineA retort running alkali metal vents or it is a bomb: hydrogen must leave, oxygen must stay out, and the vent is the safety feature — foil and loose caps are the engineering, hermetic seals are the accidentN274's foil-vented apparatus; the sealed-vessel reasoning priced row by row
9The flame lineThe lilac line is 766 nm and sodium's 589 nm masks it at the first trace: cobalt glass to see, never lean over a burning alkali metal to look, and a yellow-orange flame in a potassium run is information, not a failure to clean the wireThe diagnosis arc (Lecture 3); Ch 13/39's mask family
10The solvent lineTetralin (bp 207 °C) and the toluene wash are flammable organics at their own reflux temperatures: no ignition sources, ventilation, and the wash is done cold — the Ch 21 solvent discipline worn at the campaign's benchN274's reflux and toluene wash; Ch 21's inheritance
11The bench/food separationThe salt-substitute jar never returns to the kitchen once it has entered the lab: potassium chloride is a food item at the table and a reagent at the bench, and the dose is the fence — the hyperkalemia face is the medical name for the line's reasonThe KCl jar's two lives; Ch 1's standing line, worn at its campaign sharpest
