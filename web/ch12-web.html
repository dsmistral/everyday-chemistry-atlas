Vol I · Chapter 12 — Electrochemistry: Electrolysis, Cells, Nernst

Chapter 11 moved electrons and priced them in grams. This chapter prices them in coulombs. The step is small on paper — multiply by 96,485 — and enormous at the bench: a redox reaction with its two halves physically separated is no longer just a reaction, it is a cell, and a cell is four things at once — a power source, a meter, a factory, and a bomb. The household is already an electrochemical museum: the AA in the drawer, the phone in the pocket, the car in the driveway, the water heater's sacrificial rod, and — in the ruined-world premise — the only portable electricity you will ever own again, all of it chemistry wearing labels. Voltage is free energy per coulomb; current is the mole made electrical; a battery is Chapter 11's activity series wound into a can and told to behave. The corpus supplies the practitioners one last time before the synthesis chapters: the zinc-powder cell whose hydrogen by-current is priced as a thief (N76), the tin crystals grown on a wire (N99), the lithium episode that considered electrolysis and refused it on a combustion risk — the honest zero (N100) — the chlorine generator whose do-not-attempt disclaimer is the chapter's own boundary line (N77), and the arc-furnace finishes that mark where the home lab's electricity ends (N79/N98, knowledge-only). The book canon closes the loop: Thompson's Electrochemistry chapter with its water-splitting signature experiment (N20), Dunn's Volta and Dow — the pile that started everything and the brine cells that industrialized it (N23), the Golden Book's aluminum story — a metal made only by electrochemistry (N24), and OpenStax chapter 17's second half, which this chapter now owns in full (N25). The chapter's four questions, in bench order: how do you wire a redox pair into a cell, what does the voltage tell you, how do you make the electrons work for you — and what happens as the cell spends itself into equilibrium?

Lecture 0 — The house is wired (every voltage is a price tag)

Start in the junk drawer. The AA battery: 1.5 V on the label. The 9-volt: nine... no — six 1.5-V cells stacked [convergent]. The lithium coin cell: 3 V. The phone: 3.7 V nominal, and the manufacturer forbids you from ever seeing its chemistry. The car battery: six lead-acid cells at ~2 V each, 12 V on the terminal. Every one of those numbers is a thermodynamic price: the voltage is the free energy the reaction inside delivers per coulomb of charge it moves. This is the chapter's master equation before it is ever written with symbols:

volts = joules per coulomb = the chemical bargain, unit-priced.

And the converse, which is the ruined-world premise in one line: any reaction that pushes electrons can be asked to do work, and any work you need done can be paid for in electrons. Want hydrogen? Split water with electrons. Want chlorine? Split brine with electrons. Want copper plated on a spoon, zinc ground to powder, a black oxide stripped back to metal? All of it is the same transaction: charge in, chemistry out, and the arithmetic is Faraday's — one mole of electrons is 96,485 coulombs [convergent], a fixed exchange rate between the gram and the ampere that no market can move.

The third reframe belongs to the meter. A voltmeter is the cheapest, most honest instrument in the home lab — the $5 multimeter reads potential differences the way Chapter 8's pH strips read proton activity, but with three digits and no calibration anxiety. And unlike nearly every other observable in this book, voltage is quantitative on contact: touch two electrodes and the meter tells you the free-energy gap between two redox couples directly. The bench that has no colorimeter, no spectrometer, and no balance can still do quantitative electrochemistry. That is why this chapter, of all of them, is the ruined world's analytical toolkit in embryo — and why Chapter 27 (electroanalysis) will grow directly out of it.

The chapter's one law, previewed: electricity is chemistry and chemistry is electricity — separated by nothing but a wire. Chapter 11 watched electrons hop directly between atoms (displacement, corrosion). This chapter puts a wire in the hop's path and charges for the crossing.

Lecture 1 — The galvanic cell from the kitchen: the jam-jar Daniell

The build, no purchases required. Chapter 11's signature reaction — zinc pushing copper out of solution — happens in a beaker as a color change and nothing more: the electrons cross from metal to ion directly at the zinc surface, and their free energy leaves as imperceptible heat. Separate the two halves and hand the electrons a wire to cross by, and the same reaction pays you in volts. The kitchen version of the 1836 Daniell cell [convergent — the history; Dunn's Volta lecture is the book-canon telling, N23]:

  • Two jars. The left: zinc (roofing strip, galvanized nail sanded clean, or battery shell — N76's own sourcing) in a solution that carries Zn²⁺ — a spoon of Epsom salt or table salt dissolved in warm vinegar works; the zinc will sit in it doing almost nothing, which is the point (its couple is the supply side). The right: copper (pipe, wire, or the Ch 9/11 copper you smelted) in blue copper(II) sulfate — the Ch 9 solution, or table salt + a pinch of the root-killer stock if sulfate must be conserved.
  • The bridge. A paper towel rolled tight and soaked in salt water (or Epsom solution), one end in each jar. This is the salt bridge: anions drift toward the zinc side, cations toward the copper side, and the circuit closes without the solutions mixing. Without it, the zinc side goes positive, the copper side goes negative, and the current stops within moments — charge separation builds a wall out of itself.
  • The meter. Any multimeter on DC volts, or — ruined-world honest — a red LED plus the fruit cells of Ex 12.6 as a crude detector. The reading: about 1.0–1.1 V depending on your solutions [convergent — the textbook Daniell reads 1.10 V at standard concentrations; the house's full-precision table gives 1.105 V, the pin Chapter 11 already printed].

What is physically happening — the two-ledger picture. At the zinc (the anode): oxidation, Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ — the metal dissolves, and the electrons it releases run through the wire to the copper. At the copper (the cathode): reduction, Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu — blue solution fades, red-brown metal plates out. In the wire: electrons, left to right. In the bridge and solutions: ions, closing the loop. Nothing happens anywhere that Chapter 11's ledger did not already sanction — but now the electron transfer is routed, observable, metered, and useful.

The notation, because every reference you will ever read uses it. The cell is written, with the anode on the left:

Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) ‖ Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s)

One bar is a phase boundary (electrode to solution); the double bar is the salt bridge. The convention costs nothing to memorize and pays immediately: the cathode is always written on the right in the standard form, the cell potential is always cathode minus anode, and the mnemonic that keeps the hardest trap straight is the pair AN OX / RED CAT — the anode is oxidation, the cathode is reduction — in both galvanic and electrolytic cells. (The trap: in a galvanic cell the anode is the negative terminal and in an electrolytic cell it is the positive one, because the sign labels follow the external circuit's perspective. The chemistry words never change; the battery-industry words do. Say which you mean.)

Volta's pile — the historical spine (N23). Before Daniell separated the solutions, Volta stacked the couples: copper, brine-soaked cloth, zinc, repeat — the first battery, 1800, and the first sustained electric current in human hands. Dunn's telling makes the point this chapter inherits: the pile was built to settle an argument (Galvani's animal electricity) and it immediately remade every argument around it — electrolysis, electroplating, and within weeks of its announcement the decomposition of water. The cell was born as an instrument of analysis and became an instrument of production — exactly the arc this book follows from Chapter 8's pH metering to Chapter 15's reagent making.

The dry cell from the inside. A regular AA is the same architecture in a sealed can: zinc case or zinc powder (anode), ammonium chloride or alkaline electrolyte paste soaked in a separator (the bridge, collapsed into a layer), manganese dioxide around a carbon rod (cathode-side chemical reagent). One difference from the jar matters enormously at the bench: the cathode reagent is a solid, so no blue fades to tell you the cell is spent — the meter alone reads its state, which is exactly the Nernst work of Lecture 5.

Lecture 2 — Voltage is a meter: the E° table, ΔG°, and the Faraday price

The standard table, and why subtraction is legal. Every redox couple has a standard reduction potential E° — the cell voltage measured against the hydrogen couple, all solutions at 1 M, 25 °C [convergent — the standard tables; this chapter extends Chapter 11's working table with the couples the cell census needs]. A few entries the chapter will use constantly, in the house's precision [convergent, Ch 11 pin table extended]:

Li⁺/Li −3.040 · Mg²⁺/Mg −2.372 · Al³⁺/Al −1.662 · Zn²⁺/Zn −0.763 · 2H₂O/H₂+2OH⁻ −0.828 · Fe²⁺/Fe −0.447 · Sn²⁺/Sn −0.138 · 2H⁺/H₂ 0.000 (the defined zero) · Cu²⁺/Cu +0.342 · O₂+2H₂O/4OH⁻ +0.401 · Ag⁺/Ag +0.800 · O₂+4H⁺/2H₂O +1.229 · Cl₂/2Cl⁻ +1.358 · PbO₂/PbSO₄ +1.685 (in sulfate media) · PbSO₄/Pb −0.356 (in sulfate media)

The rule that turns the table into a battery of predictions: E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode. The Daniell: +0.342 − (−0.763) = 1.105 V [computed; the textbook's 1.10 V is the same number at two significant figures, both printed on purpose]. The corrosion cell of Chapter 11: +0.401 − (−0.447) = 0.848 V — the rust battery is a battery, and Chapter 11 printed the number. Every activity-series displacement of the last chapter is now a voltage waiting to be measured; the series was the sign of a subtraction, and this chapter performs the subtraction.

The beautiful cross-check: the water-splitting couple in both media. Acid medium: O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O at +1.229 V, against 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ at 0.000 — splitting water takes at least 1.229 V. Basic/neutral medium: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻ at +0.401 V, against 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻ at −0.828 V — +0.401 − (−0.828) = 1.229 V, the identical number [computed; convergent]. The two route-pairs differ in every electrode equation, and the voltage does not care — because voltage prices free energy, and the free energy of splitting water into the same three species is the same reaction in any solvent costume. When two different-looking chains give one identical thermodynamic number, that is not a coincidence to admire; it is a conservation law showing its face, and the book treats it as such (the same discipline as Chapter 9's two-route Ksp round-trips).

ΔG° = −nFE° — the bridge between Chapter 7 and the meter. The cell's free energy is the voltage times the charge it moves, per mole of reaction: ΔG° = −nFE°, where n is electrons per formula reaction and F is the Faraday constant, 96,485 C per mole of electrons [convergent — F = 96,485.33 C/mol exactly by the SI's own definition; the house works at 96,485]. Three immediate prices:

  • The Daniell reaction Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu: n = 2, E° = 1.105 V → ΔG° = −2 × 96485 × 1.105 = −213.2 kJ per mole of reaction [computed] — the same spontaneous push Chapter 11 read off the activity series, now in Chapter 7's own currency, kilojoules.
  • The lead-acid cell (Lecture 3): E° = 2.041 V, n = 2 → −393.9 kJ/mol [computed] — a working car battery is a very willing reaction.
  • Water splitting: minimum electrical work = 2 × 96485 × 1.229 = 237.2 kJ per mole of H₂ [computed] — and the thermodynamic literature prints 237 kJ per mole as the Gibbs energy of the split [convergent, web-verified this session]. The mole of gas you generate at the bench in Lab 12.2's arithmetic carries a fixed, knowable electric bill.

The Faraday price, made domestic. 96,485 C is also 26.80 ampere-hours [computed — the ampere-hour is the battery aisle's unit, and this is the conversion that lets you read a battery label in moles]. One mole of electrons per 26.80 Ah: an AA's 2,000 mAh rating is 7,200 C, or 0.0746 mol of electrons — a chemical quantity of electricity, small enough to price in grams of zinc (Ex 12.2 computes it: 2.44 g). The faraday is the book's Rosetta stone between the battery aisle and the periodic table.

And the equilibrium chapter's debt, paid. Chapter 8 measured acid-base equilibria with indicators and could only reach equilibrium constants that way; Chapter 9's Ksp round-trips needed careful precipitation. The cell measures them directly: at equilibrium the battery is dead — E = 0 — so Q = K exactly when the Nernst correction cancels E°, which gives log K = nE°/0.0592 (Lecture 5's constant, previewed). For the Daniell: 2 × 1.105/0.0592 = 37.33, so K ≈ 10³⁷·³ [computed] — a one-way street so complete that the words "zinc displaces copper" and "K = 10³⁷" are the same sentence in two languages. This is how equilibrium constants that no titration could ever reach get measured: couple the reaction to a wire and read the voltmeter.

Lecture 3 — The battery census: the house's voltage sources, read as chemistry

The alkaline AA — 1.5 volts of zinc. The label says 1.5 V [convergent — alkaline nominal]; the chemistry is Chapter 11's anode metal giving electrons to a manganese reagent. The simplified cell reaction, atom-balanced and machine-gated in Lab 12.2:

Zn + 2 MnO₂ → ZnO + Mn₂O₃

Zinc oxidized to +2 (anode, the case or powder); manganese(IV) reduced to manganese(III) (cathode, the black paste around the carbon rod). Why alkaline cells use powdered zinc in a conducting alkaline paste where the old zinc-carbon cells used a solid case: surface area — N76's whole zinc-powder episode is this design lesson performed as a synthesis, the same reason finely divided tin outperforms the bar (N103's surface-area rationale, Chapter 11's Lecture 4). The capacity label is a Faraday promise: 2,000 mAh says this can will deliver 7,200 C, which is 0.0373 mol of zinc — 2.44 g [computed] — before the reagent runs out. Ex 12.2 runs the full chain.

The lead-acid battery — 2.041 volts of sulfuric acid becoming solid. The car battery's cell reaction, atom-balanced in Lab 12.2:

Pb + PbO₂ + 2 H₂SO₄ → 2 PbSO₄ + 2 H₂O

The two couples, measured in sulfate media [convergent]: PbO₂ + SO₄²⁻ + 4H⁺ + 2e⁻ → PbSO₄ + 2H₂O at +1.685 V, and Pb + SO₄²⁻ → PbSO₄ + 2e⁻ at −0.356 V; the cell: 1.685 − (−0.356) = 2.041 V [computed; convergent table — six cells in series give the 12-V battery]. Three things to read off the equation, all of them household knowledge once the ledger is visible: (1) the electrolyte is a reagent — sulfuric acid is consumed into solid lead sulfate as the cell discharges, which is why a flat battery's acid is nearly water and a charged battery's is dense (the hydrometer reads state of charge as density — a Nernst meter in plumbing form [convergent]; Lecture 5 computes the voltage drift behind it); (2) recharging is electrolysis pointed inward — drive the reaction backward and the sulfate returns to solution, which real alternators do thousands of times; (3) the home chemist's relationship to this cell is mostly as a source — the battery-acid datum of N57: the Rooto drain-opener that supplies the book's H₂SO₄ exists because battery acid is the cheapest sulfuric acid in commerce, and the same bottle must respect the safety table's row on charging gas (hydrogen at the plates when charging hard — ventilation, no sparks).

The lithium-ion cell — 3.6–3.7 volts of intercalation. The phone's battery is the one the corpus has actually torn down (N98): the cathode LiCoO₂ — the same compound Chapter 11's leach audit pinned at Co(III) — a graphite anode, and lithium ions shuttling between hosts without ever plating as metal [convergent — the intercalation design; 3.6–3.7 V nominal]. The design is why the cell is both wonderful and unforgiving: nothing is consumed, so it recharges for years — and nothing buffers abuse, so a short or a puncture releases the stored energy into the one substance you cannot dilute (the cell itself; knowledge-only, the safety table's row 3, Chapter 28's forward flag). N100's salvage rule for any lithium cell recovered from a dump battery — test the charge state before you trust it — is the corpus's own safety datum and this chapter's to re-print.

The census, read as one table. Alkaline 1.5 V · zinc-carbon 1.5 V (the same zinc, cheaper paste) · lead-acid 2.041 V · Li-ion 3.6–3.7 V · silver oxide coin 1.55 V · lithium coin (Li/MnO₂) 3.0 V [convergent — nominals] — and the 9-V battery, which is six 1.5-V cells stacked in series inside one rectangular can. The stacking rule is Lecture 1's arithmetic made hardware: series adds voltage (6 × 1.5 = 9), parallel adds current capacity — and Ex 12.6's fruit-cell chains perform both rules with lemons.

Why batteries die — the preview. Every cell in the census dies the same death: the reaction approaches equilibrium, and the voltage drifts down toward zero at exactly the rate the Nernst equation prescribes. That drift — and its twin, the concentration cell that runs on nothing but dilution — is Lecture 5.

Lecture 4 — Electrolysis: running the reaction backward (and paying the bill)

The definition. A galvanic cell lets a spontaneous reaction push electrons through a wire. Electrolysis is the reverse conquest: an outside source pushes electrons into a cell and makes a non-spontaneous reaction go. The bench rule that keeps the bookkeeping honest: the anode is still oxidation (AN OX holds) — but now it is the positive terminal, because the external supply owns the sign.

Water — the signature experiment (N20). Thompson's Electrochemistry chapter makes water electrolysis the canonical home experiment, and it is this chapter's bench centerpiece: two electrodes in water made conductive (a pinch of salt, or Epsom solution — not too much salt, row 5 of the safety table says why), a 9-V battery, and gas at both electrodes: hydrogen at the cathode (2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻), oxygen at the anode — and the 2:1 volume ratio is the atom balance of 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ made visible at the bubble stream [computed — Lab 12.2 gates the equation; Chapter 10's pop test confirms the hydrogen side; the glowing splint the oxygen side]. The voltage story is the lecture's thermodynamic lesson: 1.229 V is the minimum [convergent], but real water electrolysis at a kitchen scale needs noticeably more — overpotential [convergent — the kinetic surcharge, electrode by electrode], the extra voltage spent forcing slow electrode reactions to run at a useful speed. The number to keep: 1.229 V prices the reaction; the power supply bills for 1.229 V plus kinetics — the thermodynamic minimum versus the practical bill, the same distinction Chapter 7 drew between ΔH and the fuel you actually burn, and Chapter 25 will re-draw as rate laws.

Brine — the industry in one beaker (knowledge-only). Pass current through concentrated salt water and the products are three reagents of this book's entire second half [convergent — the chlor-alkali process]:

2 NaCl + 2 H₂O → 2 NaOH + H₂ + Cl₂

Hydrogen at the cathode, chlorine at the anode, sodium hydroxide left in solution — the reaction behind bleach, drain opener, and (via the sulfate route) much of Chapter 14's alkali shelf. The electrochemical twist that makes it interesting: thermodynamically the anode should prefer oxygen — the water-splitting couple is 1.229 V against the chlorine couple's higher demand — yet brine cells make chlorine, because oxygen evolution is kinetically sluggish on real electrodes while chlorine evolution is fast [convergent — the classic kinetic-upset; overpotential again, this time deciding which product you get]. The home-lab boundary is absolute: chlorine is a weapon as a gas — N77's generator episode carries an explicit do-not-attempt disclaimer and the register's knowledge-only tag, this chapter cites it as the boundary itself, and Chapter 28 owns the hazard course. (The small electrolytic oxygen generator on the bench is fine — water with a little Epsom salt keeps the products tame and the gases separable.)

The corpus's electrolysis bench, cited honestly. The Canadian Chemist's element-isolation series gives this lecture its faces: N76 — the zinc-powder cell — the chapter's spine: zinc dissolved in alkali to the zincate bath, a nickel-mesh cathode, ~4 V across the cell [single-source — the corpus datum, plausible against the water-splitting couple it must beat; machine-checked in Lab 12.2's framework], and the practitioner's own honest price: a hydrogen current that competes with the metal deposition, the electrons leaking to water instead of zinc — the exact "current thief" this lecture's thermodynamics predicts at any cathode in water; the product pyrophoric when dried (the safety table's row 7). N99 — the tin crystals — electrolysis of a stannous solution growing bright metal crystals on the cathode, corroborated at comment level (the register's honest level; the elegant classroom-current face). N100 — the lithium honest zero — the episode considers running electrolysis on its LiOH process stream and refuses it explicitly on the combustion risk; the corpus keeps demonstrating that the alkali metals are the electrolysis chapter's boundary, not its bench (every alkali performer in the register — N78's potassium, N100's lithium, N105's cesium — got there by lithium displacement, a Chapter 11 reaction, never by aqueous electrolysis; molten-salt electrolysis is the industrial route the home cannot host [corpus faces, register-verified]). N79/N98 — the arc furnace — where process electricity leaves the household scale entirely (a welding-supply-class discharge), the P5 process-energy face, knowledge-only, Chapter 28's line.

Refining — the quiet industrial mirror. The world's copper wire is 99.99% pure because copper is refined electrolytically: the impure anode dissolves, pure copper plates on the cathode, and the impurities either stay in solution or fall unreacted as anode slime — where the silver and gold in copper ore are recovered [convergent — the industrial practice]. The household mirror: Ex 12.3 plates copper from the Ch 9 solution at coulomb prices — the same chemistry at 1/10,000th the scale. And the recharging lead-acid battery of Lecture 3 is refining pointed inward: the cell dissolves its own sulfate back into acid.

Lecture 5 — The Nernst drift: dead batteries and concentration cells

The equation. A cell at non-standard concentrations does not read E°; it reads

E = E° − (0.0592/n) · log Q (at 25 °C) [convergent — the Nernst constant 0.0592 V; the refined 2.303RT/F value is 0.05916, and the book prints both]

The reading is Chapter 8's equilibrium sense translated to volts: Q below K and the cell has voltage to spend; Q at K and the cell is dead; Q beyond K and you must pay — that is electrolysis. Three demonstrations, all machine-verified in Lab 12.1:

  • Dilution moves the meter. Take the jam-jar Daniell and dilute the copper solution tenfold (Q = [Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺] rises tenfold): E = 1.105 − 0.0296 = 1.0754 V [computed]. A hundredfold: 1.105 − 0.0592 = 1.0458 V [computed]. The meter reads dilution to three digits — which is why electroanalysis (Chapter 27) can measure concentrations: the wire is a concentration meter.
  • The concentration cell — electricity from nothing but dilution. Build a Daniell with the same couple in both jars — copper in concentrated Cu²⁺, copper in dilute Cu²⁺. E° is zero (cathode minus anode of the same couple); the meter is not: a tenfold ratio gives +0.0296 V [computed] — the electrons flowing from the dilute side to the concentrated side, cell chemistry running on pure mixing energy. The dead-battery corrosion of Chapter 11's crevices and the fruit cell's damp interior are both concentration cells in costume.
  • The dead battery is an equilibrium. E = 0 exactly when Q = K — log K = nE°/0.0592 = 37.33 for the Daniell [computed] — a discharged cell is not "empty," it is at equilibrium, the reaction finished in the thermodynamic sense. This is why jump-starting a truly dead battery is futile and why the meter (not the label) is the honest state-of-charge instrument: voltage sag is the Nernst drift you can hold.

The lead-acid meter, closed. Apply the Nernst equation to the cell reaction of Lecture 3: Q = 1/[H₂SO₄]², so E = 2.041 + 0.0592·log[H₂SO₄] [computed — Lab 12.1 runs it]. Freshly charged, the acid runs near 5–6 M and each cell reads above its standard value — at 5.0 M: 2.082 V [computed]; discharged, the acid approaches 1 M and the cell falls toward 2.041 V and below as products accumulate. The hydrometer's density read and the voltmeter's cell read are the same instrument measuring the same Q — and the charging-battery hydrogen evolution that the safety table warns about has the same signature: the 2.40-V-per-cell gassing threshold is overpotential making water electrolysis the side reaction when the main reaction's demand is met [convergent — the charging practice].

The fruit cell's mystery, stated (Ex 12.6 resolves it). The lemon cell reads ~0.9 V [convergent — typical Zn/Cu fruit-cell voltage], not 1.105 V — because the lemon carries no Cu²⁺; its cathode couple is the oxygen/water system of Chapter 11's corrosion cell, and the acid merely feeds the conductivity. But its current is set by something else entirely — the electrolyte's conductivity and the electrodes' area. Voltage is the couple; current is the medium — the single sentence that explains every "why won't my lemon run the motor?" question, and the pattern-unknown exercise this chapter hands the reader with a meter.

The forward flags. Chapter 15 inherits the brine cell's products (the mineral-acid routes need Cl₂ chemistry and the chlorate of Chapter 16 is made by electrolysis — the pattern the corpus flagged at the register level); Chapter 19 inherits electrometallurgy (plating and refining as synthesis); Chapter 27 inherits the whole analytical promise (the wire as a concentration meter); Chapter 28 inherits every hazard row this chapter printed (Cl₂, Li-ion, charging gas, the arc-furnace line). And the premise closes: the ruined world's reagent shelf is rebuilt through the cell — hydrogen, alkali, chlorine, cleaned metals, and the meter that measures them are all the same thing, electrons doing priced work.
