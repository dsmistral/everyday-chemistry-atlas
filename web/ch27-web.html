Vol III · Chapter 27 — Electroanalysis: The Bench That Reads Chemistry in Coulombs

Lecture 0 — The Multimeter Drawer (the house is already an electroanalytical bench)

Chapter 12 priced the electron; this chapter reads with it. Electroanalysis is the bench that turns a chemical question — how much salt, how much copper, how much vitamin C — into an electrical measurement, and the household has been running it unpriced for a century: the battery tester in the drawer is a loaded-voltmeter reading a cell's position; the TDS pen on the RO kettle is a conductivity meter wearing a costume; the aquarium's EC probe guards the fish's salinity; the plating kit at the craft store is Faraday's law sold as a toy; the phone in every pocket counts coulombs with a dedicated chip, invisible; the diabetic's glucose strip reads a blood sugar in five seconds by amperometry — electroanalysis at the consumer scale, one strip at a time [convergent, recognition level]. And Chapter 11's immersion-plated nail was electroanalysis all along: the activity series depositing copper on iron, asking the water what metal it held, without anyone calling it a meter.

The chapter's premise, in one line: chemistry is charge in motion, and charge is the cheapest thing in the house to count. A balance reads milligrams; a multimeter reads microamps — and the faraday between them is a fixed exchange rate no market moves (Ch 12's pin). Three benches follow from it: conductivity (the ion meter — Lecture 1), plating (the mass meter run in reverse — Lecture 3), and electro-titration (the endpoint found without an indicator — Lecture 4), with Faraday's quantity meter under all three (Lecture 2). Every one of them prices on numbers the house already owns: Ch 12's faraday and E° table, Ch 8's vinegar pin, Ch 9's root-killer salt, Ch 25's iodine bench.

The binding lines, stated up front

Eight standing lines govern this bench — six inherited, two new at this chapter's own faces (the count stated and machine-checked this time; the census discipline the Ch 26 review taught):

  1. The Ch 8 tasting rule. The plating bath looks like blue lemonade and smells of nothing; it is a metal-salt solution and is never tasted; the reaction pots stay off the food shelf, and the vitamin-C pot that ends Lecture 4 is the one drinkable reagent this book ever pours — before the KI and the acid enter it, and never after.
  2. The Ch 9/13 toxic-metal fence — central here. The plating bench is the Atlas's most concentrated metal-salt bench, and the fence stands at its widest: gloves throughout, no skin in the bath, and the industrial plating classes — chrome (the Cr(VI) class), the cyanide baths, nickel — are never run, never approximated, never bought for the home; the knowledge of what they are belongs to Chapter 28's hazard course, not to this bench. The bench's only plating salts are copper sulfate and zinc sulfate — the fence's own household members, and even they are handled as chemicals, not as "just salt."
  3. The Ch 16/20 never-combination. The copper bath is deliberately acidic, and the bleach jug never shares its surface, not even wiped — an acidified hypochlorite residue is the chlorine door, and it stays shut at the electrolysis bench exactly as at every other.
  4. The Ch 10 pressure line. Every electrolysis pot makes gas — hydrogen at the cathode, oxygen at the anode — and every vessel stays open, nothing capped, nothing latched, and the two gases are never collected together in one vessel: Ch 12's rules, re-stated as this bench's standing line.
  5. The Ch 10/25 peroxide discipline, by citation only. No peroxide demonstration runs here; the decomposition's price belongs to Chapter 25's ledger and this chapter quotes it only as a quantity.
  6. The bench/food separation. The plated key is a chemical artifact, not a utensil; the plated spoon is a demonstration, never a spoon again; the vitamin-C pot of Lecture 4 crosses to the bench and never returns as food.
  7. NEW — the electricity line. Nothing at this bench ever runs on wall current: the only sources are dry-cell batteries (≤9 V) or a current-limited USB power bank through a bench supply — never mains, never a car battery (a lead-acid cell can push hundreds of amps through a short, and this bench never needs one), never an improvised charger circuit. The multimeter sits inline as the ammeter; the circuit is disconnected before any electrode is touched; wires come off before the bath is moved.
  8. NEW — the bath-waste line. A spent plating bath is metal-salt waste, not drain water: it goes to a labeled bottle and thence to the county's hazardous-waste collection — never down the drain, never to the garden (copper sulfate is an herbicide and an algicide; the root-killer fence protects the drain the way the toxic-metal fence protects the skin).

The one standing line this bench never lights: the flame line (Ch 6) — the only chapter of the second half whose bench runs cold, and it says so with relief.

The element discipline: zero admissions. Every species this chapter composes prices on the held twenty-one (the twenty plus manganese, Ch 25's re-admission) — the second chapter of the Atlas to admit nothing: electroanalysis is the reading of reactions, not the discovery of species, and its whole battery — copper, zinc, silver, iodine, the ascorbic pair — was already on the table.

Lecture 1 — Conductivity: The Ion Meter

What the meter reads. Dip two electrodes in a solution, apply a small alternating voltage, and measure how easily charge crosses: resistance R in ohms, its inverse the conductance, scaled by the cell's geometry to the specific conductivity κ in S/cm (siemens per centimeter — the house's unit is the microsiemens, µS/cm). Two honest engineering faces before any chemistry: the meter runs AC at 1–10 kHz because DC would polarize the electrodes — ions crowd the surfaces into an insulating cloud and the reading drifts low [convergent] — and it temperature-compensates at about 2% per °C, because ions move faster warm [convergent]. The meter is honest about its conditions or it is nothing.

Two faces worth the walk: the proton's absurd ticket (349.8 — seven times sodium's) is not a fast particle but a hand-off — the Grotthuss hop, proton to proton along the water's own hydrogen bonds, named here at recognition level [convergent]; and the hydroxide's 198.6 rides the same mechanism backward. Every strong-acid–strong-base story of Lecture 4 is already priced in this one row pair.

The arithmetic of κ. κ = Λ_m × c: the molar conductivity times the concentration. At high dilution Λ_m sits near its limit; as c rises, Λ_m falls (ion atmospheres drag on each other — Kohlrausch's own √c law, named, not derived, at recognition level [convergent]), so tabulated mid-strength values do the work honestly: κ(0.01 M KCl) = 141.3 × 10⁻⁵ = 1,413 µS/cm [computed from the tabulated Λ_m — the meter's own standard reproduced from first prices]; κ(0.1 M NaCl) = 106.7 × 10⁻⁴ = 10,670 µS/cm [computed]; the acid face Λ°(NaCl) = 50.1 + 76.3 = 126.4, Λ°(KI) = 73.5 + 76.8 = 150.3 [computed from the pins].

The weak-electrolyte face: vinegar priced. Acetic acid pays its conductivity only through the fraction that ionizes — Ch 8's Ka pin (1.76×10⁻⁵) re-read as a meter: α = √(Ka/c) = √(1.76×10⁻⁵/0.8376) = 4.58×10⁻³, so Λ_eff = α(λ°H⁺ + λ°Ac⁻) = 4.58×10⁻³ × 390.7, and κ(vinegar) = 1.50 mS/cm [computed — the model's number at the pins; the honest seam: activity corrections are unpriced at 0.8 M, the flagged sketch]. The meter that reads tap water at 300 µS/cm reads the same house's vinegar at 1,500 — not because vinegar is "stronger" but because 0.84 mol per litre of a weak thing out-ions a millimole of a strong one: concentration and strength are different sports, the ladder's quietest lesson.

The TDS costume. The pen's "ppm" is κ × a conversion factor — 0.5 to 0.7 [convergent] — and the factor depends on which ions do the conducting: for a true NaCl solution the honest mass arithmetic gives κ/Λ° → c, and at 450 µS/cm that is 3.56×10⁻³ M = 208 ppm, while the pen prints 225 (×0.5) or 315 (×0.7) [computed]. The costume is not a lie; it is a convention — but the bench that knows κ knows the difference between a reading and a conversion, and Ex 27.2 prices the seam.

Lecture 2 — Faraday's Law: The Quantity Meter

The coulomb, the amp-second, the mole's electric price. Chapter 12 priced the faraday — F = 96,485 C per mole of electrons [Ch 12's pin; the SI's own exact 96,485.33 — the house works at 96,485] — and this chapter spends it. The electron's own face: e = F/N_A = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C [computed from Ch 12 and Ch 3's pins], and one mole of electrons weighs 9.109×10⁻³¹ kg × 6.022×10²³ = 0.5485 mg per faraday [computed] — the reagent that weighs half a milligram per mole. That is why the balance never sees electricity: the plating bench's true reagent is nearly massless, and only the coulomb counter can count it. Electroanalysis exists because the gram is the wrong meter for the electron.

The electrochemical equivalent table (Ledger B1, machine-checked): every ion that plates carries a fixed price per coulomb — **Cu²⁺ → Cu: 0.3293 mg/C (1.1855 g/Ah); Zn²⁺: 0.3388 mg/C; Ag⁺: 1.1183 mg/C (the silver coulometer's own number, 4.0258 g/Ah — the historical instrument that defined the ampere's care); Al³⁺: 0.0932 mg/C; the water face, H₂: 0.0104 mg/C** [all computed]. One table, and every plating question in the chapter answers from it: the amp-hour label on the battery, the milliamp reading on the meter, the time on the clock — multiplied together and priced.

The two meters, one electron. Current is Chapter 25's meter wearing wires — a rate, amps as coulombs per second, the amp itself a molar rate (1 A = 1.036×10⁻⁵ mol e⁻/s [computed]); charge is Chapter 26's meter — a quantity, the amp-second, state-function honest: route-free, additive, conserved. Kinetics asks how fast; electroanalysis asks how much — and the bench that owns an ammeter and a clock owns both meters at once. This is the Ch 25/Ch 26 two-heights ladder re-read in copper: the same 360 C that plates 118.5 mg of copper in Lecture 3 can be spent fast (100 mA for an hour) or slow (10 mA for ten hours) — the drop (the quantity) indifferent to the rate, exactly as Ch 26 promised.

The battery label re-read one last time. Ch 12's AA chain (2,000 mAh = 7,200 C = 0.0746 mol e⁻ = 2.44 g of zinc) is this chapter's calibration datum: every mAh in the drawer is a mole fraction wearing a label, and Ex 27.1 re-runs it as a plating supply — the same 7,200 C that was zinc in Ch 12 is 2.371 g of copper here, one coulomb count, two benches [computed].

Lecture 3 — The Plating Bench: The Mass Meter Run in Reverse

The cell. The workpiece (a steel key, sanded clean) is the cathode; a coil of copper wire is the anode; the bath is root-killer (Ch 9/11's CuSO₄·5H₂O, 249.677) at ~0.5 M with a small dilute-sulfuric-acid addition (the classroom acid bath's own recipe [convergent class]; the chloride alternative is refused — muriatic acid is the wrong acid at a plating bench). The supply: a current-limited bench source or dry cells through a resistor, the multimeter inline. And the bench's polarity honesty: reduction at the cathode, always (AN OX / RED CAT holds in both worlds) — but the signs flip between Ch 12's galvanic cells and this bench's driven one: the external supply owns the sign, the chemistry owns the names (Ch 12's rule, re-stated).

The design (Ledger B2, machine-checked): 12.0 cm² of cathode, 0.100 A for 60.0 min = 360 C → 3.731×10⁻³ mol e⁻ → 1.866×10⁻³ mol Cu → 118.5 mg, spread as 11.0 µm on the key — and the current density 0.100/12.0 = 8.33 mA/cm², inside the bright class 1–10 mA/cm² [convergent class]. The honest event of this design, printed as found in Lab 27.2: the first pass priced 0.300 A on the same cathode — 25 mA/cm², the burnt-deposit window — and the design gate caught it before the page. Current density is Chapter 25 wearing metal: too fast a rate and the crystal grains cannot grow — the deposit goes dark, powdery, burnt; slow the rate and the same coulombs buy shine (Ex 27.6's pattern, no worked solution).

The bidirectional ledger. Weigh both electrodes: the cathode gains 118.5 mg, the copper anode loses 118.5 mg — E2 at the anode feeds E1 at the cathode (the bath's Cu²⁺ held constant: the anode dissolves exactly what the cathode plates), and the balance closes on both ends of the wire [computed]. Swap the copper anode for graphite and the arithmetic changes character: the anode now makes oxygen (E7), the bath's Cu²⁺ pays for the plate (5.000 → 3.134 mmol in the assay bath, the blue visibly fading — Ex 27.3's two-anode problem, machine-checked). The plating bench's whole bookkeeping in one sentence: the anode's chemistry decides who pays; the cathode never cares.

Electrogravimetry — the assay face. The electrochemist's oldest trick is plating to measure: dissolve the sample, plate the copper out completely onto a weighed cathode, weigh again. The design (Ledger B3): 50.0 mL of root-killer solution at 0.100 M carries 5.00 mmol Cu²⁺ — it demands 964.85 C (10.00 mmol e⁻), which at 0.400 A takes 40.2 min, and the deposit closes the assay at 317.7 mg [computed]. The cross-check the book already owns: 5.00 mmol of pentahydrate is 1.2484 g of crystals, and the label-fraction route (Ch 11's 25.45% Cu) says 317.7 mg — the balance, the coulomb counter, and the label all read the same copper (Ex 27.5's summit, three meters). The endpoint's free indicator: the bath's own blue fades as Cu²⁺ runs out — the color is the endpoint, no starch, no litmus. The honest seams: real deposits run slightly light (the hydrogen thief at the cathode steals current — E6, Ch 12's zinc-powder cell taught this thief), and the current efficiency is a face, priced as one [flagged sketch].

Immersion plating — the meter without a supply. Chapter 11's nail in copper sulfate (Fe + Cu²⁺, E13) was electroanalysis without a battery: the activity series is the power supply, and the copper on the nail is the assay of the water. The same fence at this bench: copper will displacement-plate silver (Cu + 2Ag⁺ → Cu²⁺ + 2Ag, E14 — cited class; silver nitrate is not a household shelf item), but nothing plates copper onto zinc's position from below — E12's direction is Ch 11's fence, and the plating bench lives inside it.

The fence, restated at the bath. The industrial world's bright chrome is Cr(VI) chemistry, its "copper strike" is a cyanide bath, and its nickel plate is an allergen — all three are named here and never run; Chapter 28 owns the course in recognizing them, and this bench's line is the fence's plainest statement: the home plater's three salts are copper, zinc, and (by citation) silver, and the craft-store "chrome kit" that promises more is a hazard course, not a project.

Lecture 4 — Electro-Titration: The Endpoint Without a Buret (and With One)

Conductometric titration — the corner. Watch κ during a titration and the equivalence point arrives as geometry. The strong-acid face: each mmol of NaOH removes one H⁺ (349.8) and adds one Na⁺ (50.1) — the reading falls by 299.7 per mmol until equivalence, then every mmol adds Na⁺ + OH⁻ (+248.7) and it rises: a V, corner at the endpoint [computed from the λ° pins]. The weak-acid face (this house's vinegar, Ch 8's 0.8376 M pin): the curve starts low (α small), rises gently as Na⁺ and acetate accumulate (+91.0 per mmol while the acid feeds H⁺ behind them), then steepens at equivalence (+248.7) — two near-collinear rising lines, corner where they cross, subtler than the V and priced as such (the method's own honest seam, flagged). The design (Ledger B4): 10.0 mL of vinegar diluted to ~100 mL, 1.00 M NaOH added — equivalence at 8.38 mL, and the conductometric corner agrees with Ch 8's indicator endpoint because both are reading the same 8.376 mmol [computed]. Why bother, when phenolphthalein works? Because indicators fail where meters do not: dark cider, cloudy juice, a cold garage, a colored sample — the corner survives them all; the buret was always optional, the ions never are.

Coulometric titration — the reagent is a current. The deeper move: generate the titrant at an electrode and count the coulombs. The bench's face is Chapter 25's own chemistry — iodine from iodide at the anode (2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻, E8) into a KI reservoir that holds 176× the titration's demand — and the analyte is the household's most titrated molecule, vitamin C: C₆H₈O₆ + I₂ → C₆H₆O₆ + 2H⁺ + 2I⁻ (E11), the clock's quench re-read as a measurement [the coulometric ascorbic method: a standard teaching lab, convergent]. The design (Ledger B5): 25.0 mg of ascorbic acid (0.1419 mmol) in the stirred KI cell with a little acid and a starch trace, a constant 100.0 mA through the generator anode — the iodine drifts colorless as fast as it forms, and at 27.39 C delivered the first free I₂ survives to the starch: endpoint at t = 273.9 s = 4.57 min [computed]. The clock is the buret: the reagent's volume was never measured, its charge was, and the charge is exact — a standard solution synthesized in place, titration without titrant. The honest seam: the count assumes the generator runs at 100% (no side reactions at the anode) and the endpoint assumes the starch answers instantly [flagged sketch]; at these currents both faces are kind, and the chapter says so rather than hiding it.

27.4 ●●○ — The phone's coulomb counter. A phone battery is rated 4,000 mAh at 3.85 V. The summit's moral: the meters don't disagree, they disclose different costs — and the analyst who knows each meter's seam owns all three.
