Lecture 0 — The ant-trail pencil case: the census before the campaign

The phenomenon, before any notation. A line of ants crosses the kitchen counter at ten in the morning — thin, purposeful, door to crumbs and back. The beginner reaches for the poison shelf. The field chemist reaches for the pencil, because the ants are not the problem; the trail is, and the trail is a geography before it is a chemistry. Trace it: where does it enter (the window-frame's gap, the door's weather-cold crack, the pipe's sleeve)? Where does it end (the toaster's crumb tray, the jar-ring the honey left, the compost bucket's rim)? What is it carrying? The pencil writes three columns — entry, food, activity — and the census is done before anything has been killed, which is the point: the census costs nothing and cannot make anything worse.

The census almost always names the real problem, and it is almost never "ants." It is the crumb tray nobody emptied, the jar nobody wiped, the gap nobody caulked. Sanitation is the first rung of the ladder, and it is a broom — no chemistry at all, no consumable, no expiry, no label to read, no child to fence away from. Exclusion is the second: the caulk tube, the mesh square, the jar's lid. Between them they end most household campaigns before any chapter of this book needs to open, and the discipline they teach — date and initial the notebook (Lecture 0's habit, inherited whole from FC3's jug audit) — pays for every rung above them, because the treatment that is not written down is the one that gets repeated or skipped.

Why this is Lecture 0 and not a warm-up. Because every number in this chapter's arithmetic attaches to a pest and a place the census names first. The spray's 2% is per liter — of which sprayer, at which aphid colony? The bait's 5% is per gram — in which crack, against which trail? A treatment decided before the census treats an abstraction; made after it, the same treatment treats the kitchen you actually inhabit. And the census teaches the residual thinking FC3 priced for water, here on its first pest appearance: the endpoint is the pest population after the treatment, not the dose poured — the spray that empties the counter for a day and the caulk that empties it for a season are not the same rung, whatever the bottle cost.

Lecture 1 — The IPM ladder: pesticides last, and the canon says so (P19, home chapter)

Here is the chapter's spine, and the canon owns it outright: integrated pest management, the pesticides-last rule — "Pesticides can be part of IPM, but use them only as a last resort and only after you have tried other methods" [convergent] (UC IPM, the University of California's own urban page, What Is Integrated Pest Management?, ipm.ucanr.edu). The field chapter's ladder, rung by rung, ranked as the ruined world prices them:

  1. Prevention and sanitation. The broom, the sealed jar, the emptied crumb tray, the bin with a lid. Costs: labor. Chemistry: none. This rung never degrades in storage and never needs the fence.
  2. Exclusion and the physical rung. Caulk, mesh, the door sweep; the trap (mouse, fly, wasp) — a device, not a molecule; the hoe and the mulch on the weed's side of the ledger.
  3. The kitchen chemistry rungs, all owned and all understood. The soap spray (Lecture 2), the boron bait and the mechanical dust (Lecture 3), the boiling pour and the vinegar contact (Lecture 4) — every one of them makeable from the shelf this book has already built, every one priced in this chapter's battery.
  4. The last resort — and on this page, refused. The trade's poisons are the ladder's top rung in the canon's world of labels and licensing; in the ruined world they are unrecognized bottles in strangers' hands, and this chapter's answer to them is the recognition table (Lecture 5) and the fence (P18): never made, never dosed by improvisation, never fired as salvage.

The ladder's logic is the survivor's accounting FC3 wrote for water, verbatim: the cheap robust rung is the one still standing when everything else is looted, broken, or spent — and the rungs above it work better for the climbing order, not despite it (the trap set where the crumb tray was emptied catches more; the soap spray on the colony the caulk couldn't reach kills what the broom couldn't). P19, the method ladder, is on its third Field Companion appearance — FC1's fire-starting rungs, FC3's water treatment order, this chapter's IPM spine — and this is the pattern's home chapter, the one where the canon's own name for the ladder is the field's own: integrated pest management.

One more inheritance from the water chapter, because it organizes everything below: failures on the lower rungs are loud, failures on the higher rungs are silent. The unswept crumb tray announces itself (the trail is back tomorrow). The under-dosed poison announces nothing — the colony shrinks, shrugs, and returns. Climb in order because the order is what keeps the honest rungs honest.

Lecture 2 — Soap spray: the fatty acid turned pesticide, and the water that can spoil it

The spray is the soap pot's own product, cooked Ch 23's way. The soft-soap spray begins as a saponification — the acid-plus-base cook this book has run since the soap chapters, here on the potassium shelf: oleic acid C₁₈H₃₄O₂ (282.468 g/mol, P7; Ch 23's own oil) plus potassium hydroxide KOH (56.105, P3; Ch 23's pin, the Ch 12/14 line's heir) gives potassium oleate C₁₈H₃₃KO₂ (320.558, P10) and water — and the seam closes at 3.385.730 myu EXACT [computed] (B1): 338.573 g per mole-cook, a figure never before printed on any page of this book, the first new arithmetic this chapter claims on species the book already owned. The stearate cook walks the same seam one acid over: stearic C₁₈H₃₆O₂ (284.484, P12) + KOH → potassium stearate C₁₈H₃₅KO₂ (322.574, P11) + H₂O, 3.405.890 EXACT [computed] (B2). And the cook's quiet byproduct is glycerol (92.094, P6) — Ch 21/23's own seam, freed by the same ester cut, the trade's bottled moisturizer left in the kitchen pot where it belongs.

The 2% spray, priced. The field recipe is a 2% w/v spray — 20 g of soap per liter of soft water. Priced as the sodium salt (sodium oleate C₁₈H₃₃NaO₂, 304.450, P8 — the hard soap, the shelf's bar): 20 g/L ÷ 304.450 g/mol × 1000 = 65.6922 mmol/L [computed] (D1) — a sixty-five-millimole cloud of fatty-acid anions in the sprayer. The number is not the point; the scale is: this is kitchen dilution chemistry, orders of magnitude gentler than anything on the trade's shelf, and it still works, because of what the soap does at the insect's own surface.

Mode of action, honestly. Insecticidal soap is a contact poison for soft-bodied insects — the aphid, the whitefly, the spider mite, the young scale — and its mechanism is membrane work: the soap solution enters via the insect's spiracles and disrupts the cells' membranes, the contents leak, the cell dies [convergent] (the insecticidal-soap mechanism literature; the extension pest pages carrying the class, uga.edu's among them). Three field consequences follow, and they are the whole spray's instructions: spray the beast, not the leaf (no residue, no translocation — a leaf sprayed today protects nobody tomorrow); spray the soft ones (the beetle's shell shrugs; the aphid does not); and soft water only — for the reason that closes this lecture.

The water that spoils the spray — the scum equation, and why calcium joined this chapter's table. Hard water is calcium's water, and calcium hates soap with a perfectly balanced hatred the engine can prove. Two sodium stearate molecules meet one calcium chloride in the spray can's water: 2 C₁₈H₃₅NaO₂ (306.466 each, P13) + CaCl₂ → Ca(C₁₈H₃₅O₂)₂ (607.030, P9 — Ch 23's own calcium stearate, the bathtub ring) + 2 NaCl (58.443 each, P17) — and the seam closes 7.239.160 myu EXACT [computed] (B6). That closure is why this chapter's admission table admitted calcium (the one element the design checkpoint forgot and the build corrected, recorded in the Ledger): the scum equation is the spray's honest limit, and the engine prices it. The calcium strips the soap out of solution as the insoluble stearate scum — the spray's active cloud precipitates onto the can's walls, the anion that would puncture the aphid spent instead on the bathtub ring (Ch 23's hard-water lesson, Ch 33's surfactant chemistry, here turned pest-control). The field rule writes itself: rainwater or softened water in the sprayer; the kettle-scale's can is for the boil, not the spray.

Lecture 3 — The boron shelf and the mechanical dust: two stomach-and-surface rungs

Boric acid: the stomach poison the kitchen already owns. The bait rung's active is boric acid, B(OH)₃ — 61.831 g/mol (S4; Ch 24's own bench, pinned in its battery line) — the same mild acid the buffer chapter admits, here working a completely different job: a stomach poison [convergent] (the boric-acid pest literature: ingested, not contacted — it disrupts the insect's metabolism and digestion from within; the powder's cuticle abrasion is a lesser, second face). The field form is the bait, not the dust: the acid carried in sugar or fat, placed in the census's named cracks, eaten by the forager and carried home — the slow transfer that reaches the colony a contact dust never touches [convergent] (the bait-transfer literature: the slow-acting active carried to the nest).

The 5% band — the trade's own number, priced at the bench. The trade's own baits cluster at the 5% class: the liquid ant baits of the hardware shelf run 5.4% borax, the optimal boric-acid bait band is quoted at 1–5% by weight, and the industry's granular baits argue 10 against 5 [convergent] (the label classes: epestcontrol.com's Terro label at 5.4% sodium tetraborate decahydrate; poison.org's case note carrying the same 5.4%; pestcentric.com's 1–5% optimal band; Rockwell Labs' granular-bait discussion). The band is the trade's; the arithmetic is this chapter's, priced as 5 g of boric acid per liter of bait liquor: 5 ÷ 61.831 × 1000 = 80.8656 mmol/L [computed] (D2) — and the honest note that keeps it in the fence's light: the household makes the bait, thin bands in named cracks, away from food and fingers (Safety row 3); it does not scale, bottle, or store the poison beyond the campaign.

The boron seams, three of them, one new. The bait's acid sits on a shelf this book has been building since Ch 2, and the seams close exactly. Cook boric acid with the alkali and the borates stack: 4 B(OH)₃ + 2 NaOH (39.997, P2) → Na₂B₄O₇ (201.213, P15) + 7 H₂O, 3.273.180 myu EXACT [computed] (B3) — borax's own seam, the anhydride Ch 24 pinned. The decahydrate is a hydration seam, not a cook: Na₂B₄O₇ + 10 H₂O → Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O, 3.813.630 myu EXACT [computed] (B5) — the 381.363 salt (S6) Ch 2 first printed and Ch 24 closed. And the third seam is this chapter's new composition: the metaborate seam, B(OH)₃ + NaOH → NaBO₂ + 2 H₂O, 1.018.280 myu EXACT [computed] (B4) — its product sodium metaborate, NaBO₂ = 65.798 g/mol (N1), never printed on any page of this book before this one (the session-100 line, observed at authoring: zero hits on every prior page, every form, local and repo). The honest availability note: sodium metaborate is the trade's product (the patent litter of the pest-bait industry says so), made industrially by drying borax — the household owns the seam's arithmetic and the borax it sits on; the metaborate jar itself is recognition-class. And the seam's honesty gate, the must-fail the engine catches: the claim one water short, B(OH)₃ + NaOH → NaBO₂ + H₂O, is CAUGHT — ATOMS O 4 vs 3, mass 1.018.280 vs 838.130 myu, gap 180.150 myu = 18.015 g [computed] (E7) — the gap is exactly one water (P1), and the engine refuses it: not every tidy equation is a true one.

The mechanical rung: diatomaceous earth, the dust with no chemistry at all. The third insect rung is a rock: diatomaceous earth — DE — the fossil shells of diatoms, amorphous silica, SiO₂ (S5: O₂Si, 60.083; Ch 30's own network former, the glass chapter's oxide) — and the name itself is a first print on this page (the pre-freeze sweep: "diatomaceous" and "diatomite" at zero hits everywhere). Its mode is physics: the sharp dust abrades the insect's waxy cuticle and the insect dries out — desiccation, no molecule, no poison, no mode of action to misread [convergent] (the DE insecticide literature: earthworkshealth.com's insect-control pages; the UK insecticide reference's desiccation note). Two honest limits, both convergent: wet DE is furniture dust — moisture kills the desiccant action until it dries, so the outdoor band goes down again after rain (the wet-ineffectiveness literature: vegelab, pestevidence.org.uk, seedy.farm); and the grade matters — food-grade amorphous only, never the pool filter's crystalline forms (Safety row 4, the one place the fence touches a rung we own: the crystalline dust is the lung hazard the amorphous kitchen grade is not). The band, priced for the puffer: a 250 g puffer laid evenly over 50 m² of garden bed is 5.000 g/m² [computed] (D3) — a dusting, not a burial; the dust works at the crack and crevice, and the W1 dimension prices its ledger (g/m² = M¹·L⁻² [computed]).

Lecture 4 — The weed toolbox: mulch, steel, heat, and the acid that tells the truth

The weed ladder first, and its two zero-chemistry rungs. Mulch is light exclusion — the seed germinates, finds darkness where the sun was, and dies without ever meeting a tool: cardboard, paper, straw, the oldest herbicide on earth is a shadow. The hoe is steel: the draw-cut just below the crown, the annual killed in a stroke, the perennial set back and named (the root that survives the hoe wants the trowel, not the bottle). Both rungs cost labor and sharpening, and both are silent-failure-proof — you can see whether the bed is mulched and whether the crown is cut.

The boiling pour: the heat ledger, aimed. The kettle is the garden's contact herbicide, and it works by the same ledger Ch 7 priced for the pot: a liter from the 20.0 °C ambient carries 334.720 kJ [computed] (H1) up to the boil — and poured slow on the crown, that delivery is lethal to everything it touches, which is exactly its limit: boiling water "will act as a contact 'herbicide', killing only the portion of the plant it comes in contact with" [convergent] (Iowa State University Extension's yard-and-garden answer, yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu). The pour is aimed at the patio crack, the driveway seam, the path's edge — the places the hoe can't reach and the mulch can't cover. The kettle's ledger: the 4.0 L kettle carries 1338.880 kJ [computed] (H2) — four liters, four pours, and the discipline that keeps it honest: you pay the climb, not the boil-away — the steam above the pour is waste (the phase change's own 2,256.000 kJ per liter [computed], H3, recognition-only here: steam weeding is the trade's industrial version of the same physics, and the household pour kills at the boil's arrival, not at the whistle). Coverage is ammunition: the 4.0 L kettle over a 2.0 m² bed is 2.000 L/m² [computed] (D5) — a budgeted pour, scald discipline in the other hand (Safety row 5).

Vinegar: the contact acid, and its honest limits. Household vinegar is 5% acetic acid — CH₃COOH, 60.052 (P5; Ch 4's own acid, first printed in Ch 2's review appendix) — and it kills seedlings the way the soap kills aphids: by contact, from outside. Acetic acid "acts as a contact herbicide, meaning it impacts only the parts of the plant it touches"; it "does not translocate" [convergent] (Montana State University Extension's invasive-plants answer, montana.edu); "only the parts of the plant it contacts are injured so the roots are not affected" [convergent] (the Northwest Center for Alternatives to Pesticides, pesticide.org). Read the limits as the field rules they are: annual seedlings yes, established perennials no — the dandelion, the bindweed, the Canada thistle laugh at the pour and regrow from the untouched root (the extension literature's own ranking: 5% kills seedlings with one or two leaves, and very little else). The honest dilution row: vinegar cut 1:1 with water is 2.500% [computed] (D4) — a weaker contact still, fine for the path's moss and useless as an ambition. The trade's 20% horticultural vinegars are the strong-acid rung this kitchen refuses to climb [recognition-only] — they are sold as pesticides, labeled as pesticides, and handled with the same gloves and glasses as any bottle on Lecture 5's shelf; root kill was never in the acid, and the trowel was always the honest tool.

The table's reason is the fence's reason, and it is the same sentence for every row: these molecules work by poisoning chemistry we share — the nerve's cholinesterase (the organophosphates, the carbamate), the liver's clotting cascade (the anticoagulants), the plant's amino-acid path that the human gut's flora also runs. The dose that kills the roach is a breath from the dose that harms the child; the bait that ends the rat is the same molecule that ends the dog. That is not a reason to pretend the shelf doesn't exist — it is the reason recognition is the survival skill: the survivor who can read the bottle knows which room to leave, which jar to seal, which spill to dam and which to evacuate.

Reading the label: the signal words, the first-aid statement, the spill. The label's signal word is the law's own toxicity summary, and it is a fixed ladder [convergent]: DANGER — WARNING — CAUTION, for toxicity categories I, II, III (IV needs no word) — NPIC's Signal Words fact sheet (npic.orst.edu) and EPA's own label-review training (epa.gov) print the mapping; CAUTION is the least-toxic class's word, DANGER the most (the "Danger–Poison" of the skull-and-crossbones class, the category that includes the paraquat row's bottle). The first-aid statement lives on the label itself [convergent] (EPA's Label Review Manual: the precautionary and first-aid statements the toxicity category triggers) — and the field discipline reads it before anything goes wrong, with clean eyes and dry hands, because the moment it is needed is the moment it cannot be studied. The spill response, all classes: absorb (sand, earth, the DE puffer's own dust), contain, label, dispose — never the mop-bucket dilution that carries the molecule into the drain and the world; and never, on any page of this book, the improvised deployment — the warfarin "adapted" from the pharmacy shelf, the organophosphate "salvaged" from the ruined farm co-op: the fence refuses salvage with the same gate the engine shows at R1.

The lecture closes where the chapter opened: the ladder's lower rungs, owned and understood, make this shelf unnecessary — and when the world's ruin puts the shelf in your path anyway, the skill this chapter sells is the one the whole book has been teaching under harder names: recognize the thing, price the honest alternative, and keep your chemistry where your hands can see it.

Lab FC4.1 — The fixed-point rewrite verifier, and the honest run history

Engine 33 is this chapter's verification machine, built fresh for FC4 and sharing zero code with the thirty-two engines before it. Its idea is one sentence: the formula is a token tape, and parsing is rewriting to fixed point. The lexer emits symbols, integers, brackets, and the hydrate dot's segments; three production rules then fire wherever their patterns match, leftmost first, rewriting the tape and looping until nothing fires: P1 binds a subscript to its symbol (sym num → atom); P2 admits the bare symbol (sym → atom ×1); P3 expands the innermost all-atom bracket group by its trailing multiplier (or by one), dissolving the brackets. No recursion, no stacks, no depth maps, no shunting-yard, no regex rounds — the fixed point is the parse, and rounds (the deepest bracket nesting reached) falls out of the loop's ledger. The guards are the design's spine: unknown symbols refused at the table (TABLE REFUSES Pb, R3 — the fence's honest out-of-scope, and F alongside it, R4); the zero subscript refused (R5); unclosed brackets refused with the input quoted (R6); stray decimals refused mid-tape (R7 — the rule that run 1 misapplied to a segment-leading coefficient, see below); and the mass integrality gate: the total must be a whole number of myu, so CaCl₂·0.35H₂O refuses at 1,172,892.5000 myu (R8).
