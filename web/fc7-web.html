The Field Companion · FC7 — Resins, Gums, and the Forest Pharmacy

*A wounded tree does not bleed randomly. Cut a pine and a fragrant balsam wells up that is not sap, not juice, not water — it is the tree's own bandage, a chemistry of terpenes and resin acids evolved precisely to seal, sterilize, and trap. Scrape an acacia and the wound weeps a gum that hardens like glass. Tap a rubber tree and a white milk runs that is neither sap nor solution but a suspension of polymer spheres waiting for one drop of acid. Boil maple wood's own plumbing in spring and you collect a sugar solution so dilute it takes forty gallons of patience to make one gallon of the shelf. And when a Palaeolithic chemist rolled birch bark into a heap of embers, the bark itself distilled a tar that stuck flint to wood two hundred thousand years before anyone wrote the word adhesive. This is the exuding shelf — the fourth material kingdom of the Field Companion after fire, pests, and soil — and this chapter prices its chemistry the way the Atlas prices everything: masses from the bench's own tables, models labeled as models, recognition taught instead of extraction, and the Charter fence absolute over every poison the forest also makes.*

Lecture 0 — The tree that answers the knife

Begin with the census, because the forest pharmacy's whole discipline is telling these five liquids apart — and because Ch 4 already taught you the test. Water dissolves some things and not others; the shelf's five exudates sort themselves by exactly that behavior:

  • Resin — pine, spruce, fir: a terpene-and-resin-acid balsam, insoluble in water, soluble in alcohol and its own volatile oil, flammable, sticky-hardening in air. The tree's wound seal and insect trap.
  • Gum — acacia, cherry, plum: a polysaccharide that dissolves in water to a tacky mucilage. Also a wound seal, but a sugar chemistry where the resin is a hydrocarbon chemistry.
  • Latex — rubber, dandelion, milkweed: a suspension — polymer spheres afloat in water, neither solution nor resin, curdling to a solid on a pH shift the way Ch 34's milk curdles on acid.
  • Sap — maple, birch, walnut: not a wound response at all, but the tree's own plumbing tapped in season — water carrying ~2% sucrose where the pharmacy's other four liquids carry defense.
  • Tar — the one the forest does not give: no tree exudes tar; it is made, by heating bark out of air's reach, and its inventor was the Neandertal, not the tree.

Ch 4's own line — "Maple syrup, soda, the brine jar: all solutions" — is the Atlas's first meeting with this shelf, and the sentence to keep: sap is a solution; gum becomes one; resin refuses to become one; latex is none. Every bench technique in this chapter follows from that sorting. You do not leach resin (nothing dissolves); you distill it, because its volatile half boils at 155–156 °C. You do not distill gum (nothing volatile); you dissolve it. You do not boil latex (it is already mostly water); you curdle it. And you never, ever confuse the pharmacy's recognition shelf with its poison shelf — the same bark that yields salicin's family yields alkaloids the Charter refuses absolutely (Lecture 5, and the battery's gate rows R1–R2).

One premise stated once and load-bearing everywhere: the wound response is release-rate chemistry. A tree cannot run from a beetle, so it answers locally — resin ducts flood the cut, harden, seal, and trap; latex coagulates at the wound's edge; gum hardens over it. What the tree is doing, at the molecular level, is P17 — controlling where, when, and how fast its chemistry releases — and everything we harvest from this shelf is that controlled release, harvested.

Lecture 1 — Pine resin: the wound in a jar

Cut a pine and the oleoresin that wells up is two materials in one pour. The split is the lecture:

  • Turpentine — the volatile fraction (~25–30% of the oleoresin [convergent, the drying-literature's thermogravimetric measure; the sources band it around a quarter to a third]): a mix of monoterpenes, dominated by α-pinene, C10H16, 136.238 g/mol [computed, I1 — printed as an ISOMER, not a NEW number: Ch 34 already priced limonene at exactly this mass, and the two are structural isomers, the same census with different skeletons — the terpene family's most famous twins]. Turpentine runs ~60–75% α-pinene across sources and species [convergent: ACS's molecule page gives ≈60% α- plus ≈30% β-pinene; the drying studies band α at 65–75%]; it boils at 155–156 °C [convergent], which is the whole practical instruction: a water bath cannot touch it, a steam still barely reaches it, and Ch 6's distillation discipline is the door.
  • Rosin — the solid fraction (~70–75%): the resin acids, one family sharing the C19H29CO2H skeleton [convergent, the resin-acid family page], with abietic acid, C20H30O2, 302.458 g/mol [computed, N1 — NEW, zero-hit corpus-wide] as the family's trade name and its databases' own 302.45 g/mol [convergent] agreeing with the bench table. Rosin softens across 70–85 °C [convergent] — warm-water temperature, which is why rosin is a glue, a grip, and a violin bow's bite.

The model split, priced honestly. The battery prices 100 g of oleoresin at a 20/80 turpentine/rosin split [flagged model: the conservative low end — the sources' volatile fraction runs higher, ~25–30%, and the honest page prints the gap rather than laundering it]:

100 g oleoresin → 20 g turpentine ≈ 0.1468 mol α-pinene + 80 g rosin ≈ 0.2645 mol abietic acid [computed, L8]

That one row is the shelf's whole economy: the volatile third is a solvent and a fuel (Exercise 7.1 extends it); the solid two-thirds is an adhesive, a varnish, a string instrument's rosin, and the pitch that waterproofed the oldest boats.

Fatwood — the pharmacy's pantry. A pine that died standing spent years pushing resin into its stump and heartwood; the result is fatwood, kindling so loaded with resin that more than 40% of the heartwood's dry weight can be extractives [convergent: the Canary-Island pine study's figure; fatwood's resin-soaked heartwood is the source the resin-worker knows]. FC1 already met fatwood as kindling — the wound chemistry concentrated into a fire-starting material; here the same sticks are the resin pharmacy's own collected supply, and a shaving of fatwood in a small tin is the beginning of Lecture 1's only bench preparation: gentle warming drives the turpentine from the rosin, and the two fractions separate as they have since the naval-stores era [register: the session-123 register's resin shelf; the technique is the field canon's, the chemistry is this chapter's].

First aid, recognition-only. Pine resin's traditional first-aid role — smeared over cuts as a bandage-seal — is real chemistry (the oleoresin is a physical barrier and the terpenes are antimicrobial at the recognition level), and this book teaches it exactly that far: recognition, not practice [register]. The tree's wound seal on your wound is the forest pharmacy's founding image; the modern first-aid shelf (clean, close, cover — Ch 28's apparatus) owns the practice.

Lecture 2 — Birch bark tar: the Palaeolithic still

No tree exudes tar. Tar is what you get when you heat bark out of air's reach — dry distillation: solid heated so its molecules crack and the volatile pieces condense elsewhere, oxygen excluded [convergent: the dry-distillation page's definition; the same ladder FC1's char cloth climbed, but driven past the char rung and collected]. It is the oldest chemical engineering we have evidence for: birch bark tar as a hafting adhesive on Neandertal tools, two hundred thousand years back [convergent: the Palaeolithic-tar literature].

The bark's own chemistry. The white of birch bark is suberin, a waterproofing polyester, laced with betulin, C30H50O2, 442.728 g/mol [computed, N2 — NEW, zero-hit corpus-wide] — a triterpene that can run up to 30% of the dry bark's weight [convergent: the betulin page's figure; the extraction literature's 55–75% betulin-in-extract bands the refined product]. Dry-distill the bark and the tar that drips out carries the lupane-family triterpenoids — betulin and its cousins — which is how archaeology proves a black smear on a flint tool is birch tar and not charcoal: the molecular family is the tar's signature [convergent: the GC-MS biomarker literature].

The Kozowyk experiments — the canon this chapter prices. The modern baseline is the 2017 Scientific Reports experimental series [register: the session-123 register's wood-distillation shelf; the paper is the register's own entry], which tested the three plausible Stone-Age methods — the ash mound (bark roll buried under hot ash and embers), the pit roll (bark roll in a lined pit under fire), and the raised structure (bark chamber above a collection cup, fire built around it) — and measured yields, temperatures, and complexity honestly:

  • The simple burial methods yield about 1 g of tar per 100 g of bark at their best [convergent]; the raised structure — the most engineered of the three — delivered the series' maximum single attempt of 15.7 g [convergent].
  • The lesson the numbers force: the condenser is the invention. Tar does not survive contact with open flame; whichever method keeps the bark hot enough to pyrolyze (a couple of hundred degrees) but cool enough, and oxygen-poor enough, that the tar condenses instead of burning — that method makes adhesive.

Safety owns the method. A tar retort is the Charter's never-sealed rule wearing its oldest costume: the vessel vents (a covered tin with a gap, or a two-pot still with a path for vapors), and any sealed container over fire is a pressure vessel, excluded absolutely (the safety table's first row). P17 rides the whole preparation: too hot and the tar burns to char; too cool and nothing distills; the ash mound is temperature control by thermal mass — release-rate control over an exotherm, exactly as FC1's coal box controlled a slow oxidation.

And Ch 35's discipline holds here: the modern adhesive shelf (hide glue, PVA, epoxy) is priced and owned there; this lecture owns the Palaeolithic shelf and prices it honestly — birch tar is a real adhesive, it holds stone to wood, and its recipe is the one the fire ring, not the hardware store, supplies. [register: the Primitive Technology channel's tar episodes — the session-123 register's own N18 entry, the watching-first canon]

Lecture 3 — Maple: forty gallons of patience

Maple sap is the shelf's one non-defense liquid: the tree's xylem plumbing, driven by the freeze–thaw of early spring [convergent: the sap-flow biology's standard account], carrying about 2% sucrose [convergent: the field's standard figure; sugar maple runs ~2%, other maple species less]. Ch 5 owns the sucrose — 342.297 g/mol, priced there; Ch 4 owns the fact that sap is a solution; FC7 prices the concentration ladder.

The ladder, priced by the battery. Take a model liter of sap at 2.0% sucrose (20.0 g of sugar in 1,000 g of water-lean solution, sap density taken at 1.000 g/mL [flagged model]) and boil to the trade standard of 66.9% solids [convergent: the standard syrup density — the state standards run 66–66.9 Brix; the rule's own literature]:

1.000 L sap → 29.895 g syrup at 66.9% solids [computed, L1] water evaporated: 970.105 g [computed, L2] the boil's energy at 2,256 J/g (Ch 7's pin): 2,188,556 J ≈ 2.189 MJ per liter of sap [computed, L3]

The rule of 86. The field's own arithmetic — the Jones rule — says gallons of sap per gallon of syrup ≈ 86 ÷ sap-sugar-percent: at 2%, 43:1 [convergent: the rule-of-86 literature]. The battery checks the rule against the model honestly and prints the gap: at syrup density 1.33 g/mL [flagged model] the model liter asks 44.49 L per liter of syrup — about 1.5 (≈3.5%) more than the rule's 43 [computed, L5]. The gap is the rule's own honesty: 86 is a field constant rounded for the head, not a physical law, and the difference lives in the density conventions and the rule's older 86.3%-solids basis [convergent]. A rule you can audit against a model and find nearly right is a good rule; a rule you must never audit is not.

The hydrometer — P9 at the sugar works. How do you know the boil is done? Not by clock and not by hope: the syrup's own density reports its own sugar. A syrup hydrometer floats at 66.9 Brix precisely when the standard is reached — the batch reporting its own state, P9's discipline (the reaction verifying itself; FC5's slaking tub warmed its own water, FC3's liquor kept its own clock). The cheap candy thermometer does the same service by boiling point (syrup at 66.9% boils several degrees above water); the hydrometer is the cleaner read and the one the battery trusts [register: the field canon's finishing test].

Reverse osmosis — the membrane that removes the boil. Before the pan, the modern (and DIY-able [register]) sugar maker pushes sap through a reverse-osmosis membrane: water crosses, sucrose does not. Concentrating the model sap from 2% to 8% removes 750 g of the 970.105 g the pan must otherwise boil — 77.3% of the open-pan boil removed before the first flame [computed, L6; the industrial literature's optimized RO claims run far higher, up to ~90% energy reduction at high concentrations [convergent] — the bench-scale 77.3% is this model's honest, modest version]. The pan never disappears: the last stretch to 66.9% still needs fire's flavor chemistry (the caramel and Maillard notes are the boil's gift, and RO to finished density would give you sugar solution, not maple syrup — the honesty line the industry itself prints).

The wood-bill. The boil's 2.189 MJ per liter has to come from somewhere, and FC1's fuel table prices it: seasoned wood at 16 MJ/kg, an open pan at a generous 15% capture [flagged model] — about 0.912 kg of wood per liter of sap [computed, L3 ÷ the model inputs; Exercise 7.4 prices a whole 40-liter run]. The sugar orchard burns wood to make syrup from wood's own spring plumbing; the wood-bill is the price of impatience's opposite.

Lecture 4 — Gum arabic and the latex cup

Gum arabic — the soluble seal. Score an acacia and the wound weeps a gum that dries to amber glass: gum arabic, the trade's oldest emulsifier and binder. Its chemistry is the census test in one material: ~88% of it is arabinogalactan — a polysaccharide backbone of galactose and arabinose, C5H10O5, 150.130 g/mol [computed, N5 — NEW, zero-hit corpus-wide; the 150-family's near-twin is Ch 34's thymol at 150.221, 910 myu apart — the battery's PR3 prints the gap so the two can never be conflated] — with an arabinogalactan-protein fraction (~10%) and a glycoprotein tail [convergent: the gum-arabic fractionation literature; the fraction census 88/10/1–2]. In water it dissolves completely to a viscous, sticky, edible mucilage — the gum of the ink pot (Lecture 5), the gum of the pastel maker, and the reason a gum and a resin are never interchangeable on a label: one dissolves, one refuses (Lecture 0's test, priced).

Latex — the suspension that waits for acid. Tap Hevea brasiliensis and the milky wound-flow is not a solution but a colloidal suspension: roughly 35% rubber hydrocarbon plus 5–6% non-rubber solids in ~60% water [convergent: the Hevea latex composition literature]. The rubber is polyisoprene — a chain of C5H8 repeats, and Ch 35's own pin: isoprene, C5H8, 68.119 g/mol [computed, P13]; the polymerization bookkeeping (1,000 repeats close the ledger exactly) is the battery's B5 row [flagged model: the n=1000 bookkeeping only — the real chain lengths run far longer and the real mechanism is enzymatic in-tree]. The battery prices the cup: a model liter of field latex at 35% rubber carries 5.138 mol of C5H8 units [computed, L7].

The curdle. Add formic acid — CH2O2, 46.025 g/mol [computed, N6 — NEW, zero-hit corpus-wide; and the 46-family's near-twin of Ch 21's ethanol at 46.069, 440 myu apart, printed at PR4] — and the suspension collapses: the rubber particles' protein coats lose their charge near pH ~5, aggregate, and rise as a pale coagulum, the same curdling move Ch 34 taught for milk and the same destabilization Ch 10 taught for colloids [convergent: formic acid is the industry's standard coagulant — the rubber-processing literature is explicit]. The honesty line, printed as the model's own label: [flagged model: a pH trigger, not a stoichiometry] — the acid is spent on charge, not consumed by ratio; you cannot balance latex + acid → rubber the way you balance salicin + water → saligenin + glucose, and the ledger refuses to pretend you can (that refusal is why the reaction never appears as a battery balance row: it would be a false closure, and the Atlas does not print those).

The second shelf — recognition only. Hevea is the tropics' tree; the temperate pharmacy has its own latex plants, priced only at recognition level: guayule (a desert shrub whose whole tissue runs rubber — ~9% of dry weight in the 1941 Emergency-Rubber era's two-year plants, with modern breeding working the high-molecular-weight fraction up from ~2.8% toward commercial hopes [convergent; register: the session-123 register's guayule shelf, the Manzanar project, and the USDA assessment]), milkweed and dandelion (the backyard latex of the WWII schoolchildren's textbooks), and gutta-percha and chicle (the inelastic latexes — recognition only). Ch 36 recorded the gateway edge at Ch 35's close — sap → latex → solvent cement — and this lecture is that edge's chemistry: the suspension, its collapse, and the polymer left behind. Vulcanization — rubber's crossing with sulfur — is recognition-only here, exactly as Ch 35 taught the reactive shelf: the fence (P18) owns it; the word appears so the label can be read, and the recipe never (the safety table's row 7).

Lecture 5 — The bark pharmacy and the ink pot

The corridor, priced — never practiced. Ch 34 owns the plant-extraction shelf and the willow's corridor; FC7 extends it into the forest pharmacy: the bark's salicin — C13H18O7, 286.280 g/mol [computed, P10 — Ch 34's pin, the glycoside the willow stores] — is the tree's own aspirin ancestor, and the battery prices the whole corridor at recognition level:

salicin + water → saligenin + glucose [computed, B1 — 3,042,950 myu, closes exactly] saligenin + oxygen → salicylic acid + water [computed, B2 — 1,561,370 myu, closes exactly] aspirin + water → salicylic acid + acetic acid [computed, B7 — the corridor walked back, 1,981,740 myu, closes exactly]

Saligenin (salicyl alcohol) at 124.139 is Ch 34's own pin [computed, P11]; salicylic acid at 138.122 is Ch 13's [computed, P14]; aspirin at 180.159 is Ch 3's [computed, P12] — and the 180-family's seam prints with them: aspirin (1,801,590) sits 30 myu from glucose (1,801,560) [computed, PR2], the corridor's own sugar, so near in mass and so far in destiny that the battery keeps the two apart by row. Commercial willow-bark extracts standardize at 5–15% salicin [convergent]; the corridor above is the chemistry of the shelf, priced. The medicine door itself is Ch 34's fence and FC7 keeps it: this chapter prices the forest pharmacy's chemistry; it does not practice medicine, and the alkaloid shelf — the forest's poisons, morphine's and strychnine's class — is recognized, named, and refused at the engine's own gate (R1, R2, the P18 name gate, the fence absolute: recognition, never extraction).

The acorn and the leach. The oak's seed is food behind a chemical fence: raw acorns run 2–10% tannins [convergent], the astringent polyphenol family that binds protein (your tongue's dryness is the binding) and defends the seed. The universal human answer — discovered independently on every continent that has oaks — is leaching: tannins are water-soluble, so water carries them away; cold leaching preserves the starch for flour, hot leaching is faster and cooks as it cleans [register: the field canon; the chemistry is Lecture 0's census test — the tannin dissolves, the oil and starch do not].

The oak gall and the ink pot — Ch 22's door, extended. A gall is the tree's own overreaction: a wasp's egg commands the oak to grow a tumorous oak-apple, and the gall concentrates tannin to astonishing levels — the common oak gall-apple's 47.2% by dry weight in the modern measurements, Aleppo galls at 65–75%, Chinese galls approaching 89% [convergent: the gall-tannin analyses; register: the ink-canon's ingredient shelf]. Ch 22 opened this door at the kitchen tier — strong tea as the poor chemist's gall, copperas as the iron, the oxidation darkening on the page — and named the seam honestly: tannins are a family, not a formula; ferric tannate's stoichiometry is unpinned at that bench. FC7 extends exactly as far as the trade's own convention allows, and labels the model as the model:

Tannic acid, C76H52O46, 1701.206 g/mol [computed, N4 — NEW, zero-hit corpus-wide] — the trade's decagalloyl bookkeeping convention [flagged model: one glucose core, ten galloyl units, the pharmacopeia's "tannic acid"; the tree's real gallotannins are a family around this convention, and NIST's own 1701.1985 for the same formula is the basis seam — the databases' averaged atomic weights against the bench's held table, the same seam Ch 35 printed for chloroprene]. Gallic acid, C7H6O5, 170.120 g/mol [computed, N3 — NEW, zero-hit corpus-wide] — the galloyl unit itself; the hydrolysis that turns the trade's tannic convention into ten gallics and a glucose closes exactly [computed, B3 — 18,813,560 myu]. Copperas, FeSO4·7H2O, 278.006 g/mol [computed, P9 — Ch 19's pin, Ch 22's ink feedstock], and Ch 22's own E8 oxidation (4 FeSO4 + O2 + 2 H2SO4 → 2 Fe2(SO4)3 + 2 H2O) is cited, not re-derived; the drying of the heptahydrate to the monohydrate — losing six waters, 38.880% of its mass [computed, B4, X3] — is FC7's own row.

The recipe, priced at the bench tier. A classic working recipe in the ink canon's family: crushed galls infused in water (or rain, or wine), copperas stirred in, gum arabic for suspension — with the modern stability finding that a ~3:1 gallotannic-to-iron-sulfate weight ratio makes the most stable inks [convergent: the iron-gall-ink research canon; register: irongallink.org's ingredient and recipe shelf, and the 1770 recipe in the register's UCL entry — 2 oz galls to 1 oz copperas to ½ oz gum, i.e., the 4:2:1 family]. Lab FC7.1 — Engine 37, the CPS fold compositor, and the honest run history

Engine 37 — the CPS fold compositor — built fresh for this chapter, sharing zero code with the thirty-six prior engines of the Atlas sessions. Design: a continuation-passing-style fold — the parser never returns a value; every parsed token passes its partial census forward through an explicit continuation closure, and bracket groups are handled by composing continuations (each group scope parks its census in a private register; the subscript, read only after the closing bracket, scales that register — never the whole accumulator); the atom census lives as packed BigInt lanes — one 64-bit lane per admitted element in a single BigInt, so a whole composition is one machine word of the engine's own making; masses are exact BigInt myria-units (1 myu = 1e-4 g/mol) from the admission table {C, H, O, S, Fe} (C 120,110; H 10,080; O 159,990; S 320,600; Fe 558,450), transcribed and double-checked by the two checksums (T1 straight = 1,169,230 myu; T2 positional, alphabetical C,Fe,H,O,S = 3,510,210 myu). Gates in binding order: the P18 restricted-name gate FIRST (the fence classes refused before any formula is parsed — morphine and ricin are the battery's proof rows), then the admission gate ('N', 'P' refused — the eighteenth zero-admission chapter), then syntax (unclosed groups, bad characters, empty), then the non-integer hydrate refused as NON-EXACT at the dot.

Safety

#RuleThe chemistry behind it
1Heat without air, vented always — never sealed. Any tar retort, still, or dry-distillation setup vents its vapors freely; a sealed container over heat is a pressure vessel, excluded absolutely.Lecture 2: dry distillation is pyrolysis with a condenser; the vapors must reach the collector (FC1's never-sealed rule; the Charter's oldest costume).
2Hot sugar and hot tar are burns that keep burning. Syrup at finishing temperature and dripped tar both exceed water's scald range and cling; dry gloves, long tools, a clear drop zone, cool water 10–20 minutes if touched.Lecture 3: the finishing boil sits above 100 °C (the hydrometer's few-degrees-over-water is the danger's measure); sugar and tar are viscous — heat transfers to skin while the liquid clings.
3Turpentine is a flammable solvent, not a novelty. Small quantities, ventilation, no ignition sources; never indoors at scale; the same shelf discipline Ch 21 taught the solvents.Lecture 1: α-pinene boils at 155–156 °C and flashes well below it; the volatile fraction's vapors travel to flame.
4Latex allergy is real and dose-independent. anyone sensitized to latex avoids the latex cup and plant-family handling; gloves of a non-latex kind.Lecture 4: the allergy is a protein response to the suspension's coat (the non-rubber solids), not to the rubber — recognition, not a reaction to chemistry you can see.
5Tar smoke stays off skin and out of lungs. Outdoor or hooded work for birch-tar preparations; wash after; never breathe the smoke to "test" it.Lecture 2: pyrolysis smoke carries the same polycyclic-aromatic family that makes any smoke hazardous; the tar is the collected fraction, the smoke is the uncollected loss — treat it accordingly.
6Copperas is iron, not candy. The green crystals stay out of reach and away from alkalis; wash hands; the ink bench is a chemistry bench.Lecture 5/Lab: iron(II) sulfate heptahydrate is a strong astringent and an irritant; the gardening box's label is the honest label.
