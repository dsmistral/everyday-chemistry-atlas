The Field Companion · FC10 — Fungi and Lichens: The Fifth Kingdom at the Bench

The ruined world inherits two kingdoms it never thought of as chemistry: the fungus, which builds its walls out of a sugar the plants never chose, and the lichen, which is not an organism at all but a partnership wearing one body. The fifth kingdom is the shelf where botany's leftovers turned out to be a chemistry of their own — a wall of chitin instead of cellulose, a sterol that turns into vitamin D under the same sun that bleaches the curtain, a sugar that is sucrose's twin in every number and its opposite in every behavior. The lichen half of the shelf is stranger still: a fungus farming an alga, dyeing wool for the medieval trade from a fermentation no one could write down, and — because it has no way to shed its air — dying measurably when the air carries sulfur, so that the bareness of bark became the first air-quality instrument. This is the recognition chapter, and the fence is load-bearing from the first line: the Atlas reads the fifth kingdom, prices its molecules, and never once tells the reader to eat anything that was not bought as food — the amatoxin canon is taught at recognition distance, the spore print is a bench instrument not a meal ticket, and the family words of mycology are refused at the engine's gate before any formula parses, because on this shelf more than any other, the name you cannot refuse is the name that kills.

Lecture 0 — The fifth-kingdom census (before any notation)

Begin with the grocery store, not the woods, because the fifth kingdom's bench discipline is the inverse of the forager's: the cultivated button mushroom is the only fungus this chapter will ever tell you to put in your mouth, and everything wild stays at reading distance forever. The census runs on the two questions the Atlas has asked of every shelf — what is it made of, and what does it do at the bench:

  • The wall — chitin: the repeat C8H13NO5, 203.194 g/mol [computed, S1] — a glucose wearing a nitrogen, the structural sugar of insects' shells and fungal walls alike, and the molecule that makes a mushroom a mushroom.
  • The shelf's own polymer sibling — chitosan: the repeat C6H11NO4, 161.157 g/mol [computed, S4] — chitin with the acetyl hat removed, the near-twin of Chapter 25's cellulose 162.141 [pin, Ch 25] at almost exactly one oxygen's difference, disclosed here as the seam it is.
  • The sterol — ergosterol: C28H44O, 396.659 g/mol [computed, S5] — the membrane rigidener that animals replace with cholesterol, and the molecule the sun turns into vitamin D2.
  • The sugar — trehalose: C12H22O11, 342.297 g/mol [computed, S10] — the sucrose isomer, the same number Chapter 5 pinned to table sugar and the opposite behavior under the same tests.
  • The fungal twin-chemistry of familiar things — the lichen's orcinol, C7H8O2, 124.139 g/mol [computed, S11] — the same number Chapter 34 pinned to willow's salicyl alcohol, worn by a completely different molecule on a completely different shelf.
  • The instrument — the lichen itself: a symbiosis, a dye vat, and an air gauge in one body — Lecture 4's chemistry and Lecture 5's instrument.

And one premise stated once, load-bearing everywhere: the fifth kingdom is read, never eaten. The tasting rule the food chapters carried — the tiny, careful, cultivated taste — does not ride onto wild fungi at any point, and the chapter's own engine holds the line in machine form: the mycology family words are refused at the NAME gate before any formula parses, because a name that is not refused is an identification, and an identification is the one claim this shelf never makes [register: P18, the recognition-not-synthesis charter, absolute].

Lecture 1 — The recognition bench: spore prints and the amatoxin canon

The spore print, the honest bench instrument. Cut the cap from a bought cultivated mushroom, set it gills-down on white paper under a bowl, and wait overnight: the paper in the morning carries a print — the cultivated species' spore print is the color of cocoa [convergent: the spore-print canon, cap gills-down on white paper; dark paper or plastic beneath white-spored species]. The print is the instrument because it is the one identification datum that needs no microscope: spore color sorts the genera of the field guides, and the amanitas — the family that carries the amatoxins — print white [convergent]. The bench lesson is the chapter's whole method in one dish: the print costs nothing, destroys nothing, and reads the fungus without once telling you to eat it.

The amatoxin recognition canon, at distance. The amatoxins are cyclic peptides, and the family's number is α-amanitin, C39H54N10O14S, 918.977 g/mol [computed, S8 — recognition-only bookkeeping; the literature's 918.97 g/mol, convergent] — carried by the death cap and its relatives, and behind them a minimum lethal dose near 0.1 mg per kilogram of body weight, some 7–10 mg for an adult [convergent] — a figure the 2015 case study made unbearably concrete: two death-cap caps, 43.4 g fresh and 4.3 g dried, carried 21.3 mg of amatoxin [convergent]. The canon's recognition face is three field marks, taught as reading, never as a test: a skirt-like ring on the stalk, a sac-like cup at the base, and a white spore print [convergent] — and the fence over all three: no field mark is ever a safe ticket to the table; the cultivated market is the only door this chapter opens. The safety table carries the rule at row 1, and the NAME gate's refusals (R01–R08, Lab FC10.1) are the same fence in machine form — mycelium, hypha, gill, annulus, volva, thallus, lichen, amadou refused before any formula parses: the words of the family are names of things, not compositions, and on this shelf the distinction is the whole safety case.

Lecture 2 — The wall chemistry: chitin, glucans, ergosterol, trehalose

The chitin wall, priced as a condensation ledger. The fungal wall's repeat is N-acetylglucosamine minus one water: NAG, C8H15NO6, 221.209 g/mol [computed, S2], condensed into the wall's repeat, C8H13NO5, 203.194 [computed, S1], with exactly one water, 18.015 [pin, Ch 4], leaving per unit — E1's ledger, closed to the micro-unit: 221.209 = 203.194 + 18.015 EXACT [computed, E1]. Strip the acetyl and the wall's other face appears: chitin's repeat plus one water splits to the chitosan repeat plus acetic acid — E2: C8H13NO5 + H2O → C6H11NO4 + C2H4O2, 221.209 g both sides EXACT [computed, E2] — the deacetylation that turns the shell polymer into the water-treatment polymer, and the trade's own threshold names the boundary: degree of deacetylation ≥ 55% is chitosan, below ~40% still chitin [convergent].

The linkage lesson, inherited from Chapter 25's cellulose. The β-glucans are glucose polymers like cellulose — the same C6H10O5 repeat, 162.141 [pin, Ch 25] — but branched: the fungal glucan carries its glucose on β-(1→3) backbones with β-(1→6) branches, where the plant wall's is the straight β-(1→4) [convergent] — one linkage's difference between a mushroom's wall and a cotton fiber, the same lesson Chapter 25 taught with the α/β line and here in reverse. The honest-evidence class rides with the number: cultivated button mushrooms carry 4.5–8.1 g of β-glucan per 100 g dry weight, the same band as oats at 7.6, while the oyster mushrooms carry five times that, 32.5–37.4 g [convergent] — the "medicinal mushroom" shelf's most famous claim, priced at what the analyses actually found and not a gram more [register: the honest-evidence level the chapter-map line set].

The ergosterol door, priced on the photon ledger. The fungal membrane's rigidener is ergosterol, C28H44O, 396.659 g/mol [computed, S5] — provitamin D2, the molecule the sun opens: one photon in the UV band cleaves the B-ring to previtamin D2, and heat then walks the previtamin to vitamin D2 by a thermal [1,7]-sigmatropic shift, with lumisterol and tachysterol as the overshoot products under extended exposure [convergent] — the same photochemistry Chapter 32 priced for its own shelf. The isomerization's arithmetic is the chapter's cleanest lesson: E4, ergosterol → previtamin D2, moves no atoms at all — 396.659 g both sides, EXACT [computed, E4] — because light rearranges; it does not add. The door is commercial: UV-exposed mushrooms carry the D2 the dark-grown ones lack — a UV-treated portobello's 11.2 μg per 100 g, a treated crimini's 31.9, beside the untreated fractions of a microgram [convergent] — and the photon's price at the violet edge of the D-band is B4's own row.

Trehalose, the sucrose isomer. The mushroom's storage sugar is trehalose, C12H22O11, 342.297 g/mol [computed, S10] — sucrose's exact formula and exact mass, Chapter 5's own number worn by a different molecule [pin, Ch 5, isomer row] — but the two glucoses are joined head-to-head at the 1,1 positions, and the closed ring that makes sucrose non-reducing makes trehalose non-reducing too, only more so: acid-stable, heat-stable, the dessication shield of spores and insects' blood sugar [convergent]. Hydrolysis is the two sugars' shared identity: E3, trehalose + water → 2 glucose, 360.312 g both sides EXACT [computed, E3] — the very equation Chapter 5 wrote for sucrose, C6H12O6 180.156 [pin, Ch 21] the same product twice; the battery prints the row and the isomer row together because they are the same lesson.

Lecture 3 — Amadou: the tinder shelf (Fomes fomentarius)

The hoof fungus, at reading distance. The tinder conk is a bracket fungus — hoof-shaped, perennial, silvery-grey to almost black, growing on hardwood trunks — and its inside is the shelf's one classic material: amadou, the spongy layer that catches a spark [convergent]. The archaeology is the material's own credential: the 5,000-year-old Iceman carried four pieces of Fomes fomentarius as his tinder kit, and the Mesolithic site of Star Carr preserved fungus bits modified for the same purpose [convergent] — the fifth kingdom's oldest bench object, older than the metals.

The preparation, recognition-only. The craft is soaking and pounding, and the chapter teaches it at the same distance the kiln chapter taught firing: the conk is removed, the hard outer layer scraped or cut away, and the inner spongy layer sliced [convergent]; one traditional route soaks the slice in washing soda for a week, beating it gently from time to time, then dries it and pounds it with a blunt object to soften and flatten [convergent] — and the historical trade's variant boiled or soaked it in a nitre solution, saltpeter-impregnated amadou being the European tinder of commerce [convergent, recognition-only — KNO3 101.102 [pin, Ch 4] is an oxidizer's salt and the Chapters 16/28 fence rides with it: the reader reads the recipe, the bench never runs it]. The fly-fishing trade still values the felt for drying flies — the material's water-absorbing character is the whole point, and it is the only chapter datum the angler is invited to touch.

Why the fungus makes it. Amadou is the trama — the context tissue of the fruiting body — beaten into a felt; chemically the chapter reads it as the chitin-and-glucan matrix of Lecture 2 rearranged at the bench: the wall polymer is what holds the fibrous structure that pounding teases apart, the same way Chapter 25's cellulose fibrils make the felt of paper. The chapter prices the craft as a model and labels it: B2's trim-and-yield bookkeeping is a [flagged model], its percentages stated at the ledger, the craft's own numbers nowhere claimed as analyses.

Lecture 4 — The lichen half: symbiosis, dyes, and the Ch 22 door

The partnership, stated once. A lichen is a stable association of a fungus and a photosynthetic partner — an alga or a cyanobacterium — in which the fungus builds the body, the thallus, and the partner feeds it from the sun [convergent]. The chemistry this shelf cares about is the fungus's half: the secondary metabolites, the lichen acids, that the partnership makes and stores — usnic acid, C18H16O7, 344.319 g/mol [computed, S7], the pale yellow dibenzofuran that screens the partnership's sunlight and deters the grazing animals with its bitter taste [convergent] — a near-twin in number of Chapter 22's azurite 344.668 [pin disclosed, Ch 22], and nowhere near it in any other way, the seam printed here as the census demands.

The dye vat the medieval trade ran. The great lichen dyes begin from orcinol, C7H8O2, 124.139 g/mol [computed, S11] — the colorless precursor extracted from the "orchella weeds" of the trade — and the vat that follows is the shelf's most honest piece of kitchen chemistry: exposed to ammonia and air over weeks, the orcinol family oxidizes and condenses to orcein, the purple-red of orchil, a phenoxazone-family mixture whose formula face is Musso's C28H24N2O7, 500.507 g/mol [computed, S9 — the mixture kept at recognition level, its one-number face labeled for what it is; convergent for the elucidation] — the dye the trade brewed in urine and ammonia centuries before anyone could write its structure [convergent]. Run the same conversion in the presence of potash, lime, and gypsum and the product is litmus — a more complex mixture, the 10-to-15-dye family, CAS 1393-92-6, whose indicator chromophore is the 7-hydroxyphenoxazone ring, and whose working fractions the old texts named: azolitmin the principal blue, erythrolitmin, spaniolitmin [convergent].

The Ch 22 door, opened from the lichen side. Chapter 22 already holds this shelf's most famous face — its own line reads: "a dye pulled from lichen (the azolitmin class, the medieval purple's own family)" [register: Ch 22's disclosed prior page, lichen ×2 and azolitmin ×2 re-grepped there] — and this chapter returns the tie from the other end: the litmus the school trade cut into red and blue paper is the same vat the dye trade ran, and the indicator behavior is the family's own — red below pH 4.5, blue above 8.3 [convergent] — so that N32's home-made indicator papers of Chapter 22 and the orchil vat of this chapter are one chemistry at two centuries' distance. The vat's ammonia is Chapter 20's own reagent, and B1 prices it from Chapter 20's own bottle: 250 mL of the 2.8771 M ammonia [pin, Ch 20] carries 0.719275 mol NH3, 17.031 g/mol [pin, Ch 20], a working charge of 12.250 g [computed, B1] — and the vat's four-week color ladder, red through purple to blue [convergent], is a fermentation the chapter refuses to claim as stoichiometry: the orcinol-to-orcein conversion is biology and oxidation together, priced at its inputs and labeled [flagged model] where the model goes beyond them.

Lecture 5 — Lichens as instruments: the bioindicator shelf

The gauge that needs no glassware. Because a lichen has no way to shed the air it breathes, the air's acids write directly on the thallus: sulfur dioxide kills the sensitive species first, and the bark's bareness becomes data. The canon is the zonation scale of Hawksworth and Rose, 1970 — a qualitative scale mapping epiphytic lichen communities against air pollution, the zones running from the lichen desert of the worst air to the full community of the cleanest [convergent; the register's RM-GTR-224 the standard reference, register] — and the chapter keeps it at the scale's own resolution: the zone structure read as species count, no per-zone numbers the sources did not give printed anywhere [the honesty line]. The bench version costs nothing: pick a tree, count the lichen species on a hand-span of bark, repeat across a city — the gradient that appears is the instrument's whole calibration, the same discipline Chapter 9 taught with indicator plants and here carried by a partnership that cannot move away.

The conservation fence. The same slowness that makes the lichen a good gauge makes it a bad crop: the dye species grow a fraction of a millimeter a year, and the conservation position is the trade's own — lichens should not be collected for dyeing at any bench scale, the position stated in the safety table at its own row [register: the British Lichen Society's conservation position] — so this chapter's dye chemistry is a reading of the vat, not a license to strip bark; the one extraction the chapter prices (B1) runs on bought or found-dead material, at model level, the fence printed with it.

The network. The fifth kingdom closes the Atlas's bench loop: the chitin wall prices against Chapter 25's cellulose, the ergosterol door prices against Chapter 32's photon ledger, the trehalose prices against Chapter 5's sucrose, the orcinol prices against Chapter 34's salicyl alcohol, the vat prices against Chapter 20's ammonia and Chapter 22's indicator shelf — five volumes' doors, one kingdom; and the instrument half (Lecture 5) is the shelf's own gift back: an air gauge the household can read for free, the way the whole Atlas has tried to make its reader read everything else.
