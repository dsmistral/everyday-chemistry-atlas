Vol III · Chapter 26 — Thermochemistry and Calorimetry: The Meter That Asks How Much, Not How Fast

Lecture 0 — The House Is Already a Calorimeter

Chapter 25 built the meter that asks how fast. This chapter builds the meter that asks how much. Stand in the kitchen and take the heat census, and you find that the house has been running a thermodynamics bench all along, unpaid and unpriced.

The kettle is a calorimeter with a heating element in it: a litre of water going from 20 to 100 °C is 334.7 kJ of heat, delivered in about three minutes — the electric meter outside is reading this chapter's ledger in real time. The fridge is a heat pump: it moves the kitchen's heat into the kitchen, and pays for the move — every joule it extracts from the milk reappears at the coils behind, plus the work of the pump itself. The shower drain carries the day's heat to the sewer at a litre a second and nobody counts it. The instant cold pack in the first-aid drawer does not make cold — nothing makes cold — it spends 25.7 kJ/mol of ammonium-nitrate dissolution heat from the water, and the water's thermometer pays. The iron-powder hand warmer in the coat pocket runs the rust reaction of Chapter 25's slowest clock at powder speed, and the same reaction that takes two years under the sink drip yields 7.378 kJ per gram of iron when the surface area and the salt and the air are arranged for it. The reusable hot pack is a supercooled liquid waiting for an excuse: sodium acetate trihydrate, melted and cooled, crystallizes on a click and pays 19.7 kJ/mol for the excuse. The candle on the shelf holds about 41.5 kJ per gram of paraffin, and every gram of it demands 12.55 litres of air to burn — the O₂ ledger of Chapter 10, repriced. The food label counts Calories — kilocalories, 4184 J each — and the peanut in the bowl is a fat-rich fuel whose bench heat is the classic student lab. The compost bin runs slow exothermic oxidation on the yard's own ledger, warm in the middle in January. Even the Epsom salt in the bath jar takes a few kilojoules in to dissolve — the water pays a degree you can feel at the wrist if the bag is big enough.

Every item on this census has two numbers, and the two numbers are the chapter: a rate (Chapter 25's meter) and a quantity (this chapter's meter). The rust patch and the hand warmer are the same reaction with different speeds and the same heat per mole. The candle unlit and lit are the same quantity with a trillion-fold different rate. Kinetics asks how fast; thermochemistry asks how much — and the house has been paying the second meter since long before it owned the first.

The binding lines, stated up front

Nine standing lines govern this bench, six inherited, one central, two new at this chapter's own faces:

  1. The Ch 8 tasting rule, restated at the heat bench. The calorimeter pots look like lemonade, salt water, and weak tea, and none of them is ever tasted; reaction pots are not food pots, and the food items that enter the bench (the peanut, the sugar) leave it as fuel ash, never as food.
  2. The Ch 6 flame line — now the bench's central line. This chapter burns things on purpose: candle wax, ethanol in a spirit burner, a peanut on a needle. All open-flame work runs at one dedicated station, never unattended, never over solvents or peroxide or the acid bench, hair tied, and the burner is lit at the match, never the match at the burner's mouth over the fuel.
  3. The Ch 10 pressure line. Nothing latched with gas coming: the fizz pots make CO₂ briskly (E11), the peroxide demonstrations make O₂ if they are ever borrowed from Chapter 25's bench — every vessel open.
  4. The Ch 16/20 never-combination. The neutralization pots are acid pots; the bleach jug never shares their surface, not even wiped. An acidified hypochlorite residue is the chlorine door, and it stays shut at this bench exactly as at every other.
  5. The Ch 9/13 toxic-metal fence. Nothing in this chapter needs it — but the line stands: no heavy-metal species at the heat bench, and the thermometer the bench prefers is the electronic kind, because the mercury kind is a spill waiting for a drop.
  6. The Ch 10/25 peroxide discipline. The 3% drugstore bottle is the only peroxide this bench ever mentions, and it mentions it only by citation: the decomposition's −211.1 kJ belongs to Chapter 25's ledgers, re-read here as a quantity on a kinetics page.
  7. NEW — the oxidizer line. Ammonium nitrate is the cold pack's own salt and it is an oxidizer: never ground, never heated dry, never stored near fuel, never bought in bulk, and the bench dose is one pack's single-use sachet of 10.0 g at most (Ledger B3's design). The cold-pack class is a garden fertilizer away from a hazard class, and the line exists because the distance is real.
  8. NEW — the caustic line. Lye and quicklime burn by heat as well as by base: sodium hydroxide's dissolution releases 44.5 kJ/mol and calcium oxide's slaking 64.8 — so the solid goes into the water, slowly, never water onto the solid, gloves and glasses throughout, and the slaking pot (Ledger B6) is a ceramic or borosilicate vessel, because a thin glass one has met boiling water's nephew and lost.
  9. The bench/food separation. The peanut calorimeter is the exception that proves the rule: the peanut crosses to the bench and never comes back; the kitchen's own sugar jar sends a teaspoon only if the jar never returns.

The element discipline: zero admissions. Every species this chapter prices composes on the held twenty plus manganese (Ch 12's pin, re-admitted at Chapter 25) — twenty-one elements, and the composition battery in Lab 26.1 requests no twenty-second. This is the first chapter since the held table froze that admits nothing: thermochemistry is the pricing of reactions, not the discovery of species.

Lecture 1 — The Cup and the Meter: q = mcΔT

The instrument that prices the whole chapter costs nothing: a coffee cup, a lid, a thermometer, and a stirrer. Its law is one line — q = mcΔT — the heat is the mass times the specific heat times the temperature change, and the specific heat of water is the bench's golden constant: c = 4.184 J/(g·°C) [canon, OpenStax 2e Table 5.1]. A degree on 100 g of water is 418.4 J; the cup's whole job is to make that arithmetic honest.

The metals price differently — aluminum 0.897, iron 0.449, copper 0.385 J/(g·°C) [canon, same table] — and the difference is the first lesson the cup teaches: drop 40.0 g of aluminum at 95.0 °C into 150 g of water at 20.0 °C and the equilibrium is 24.06 °C**, not the 57.5 °C a naive equal-masses guess would give — the water's heat capacity per gram is 4.7 times the metal's, so the metal's heat barely moves the water [computed, the engine's Ex-check 26.2]. The pan is hot because aluminum is cheap to heat, and the water is slow to warm because water is expensive to heat — the two facts are the same table read in both directions.

Calibration — pricing the cup itself. The cup absorbs heat too, and the honest bench measures its take instead of ignoring it. The design: mix 100.0 g of hot water at 60.0 °C with 100.0 g of cold at 20.0 °C. The ideal mix is 40.0 °C; the real one reads 39.0 °C. The hot water lost 8786.4 J, the cold water gained 7949.6 J, and the difference — 836.8 J — is the cup's take for its own 19.0 °C rise: a water-equivalent of 10.53 g [computed, Ledger B1]. From then on, every ledger adds the cup as ten and a half grams of water. That is the whole instrument, and it is self-priced.

The honest seam — Newton's cooling extrapolation. A real cup leaks heat to the room while it measures, and the leak is fastest when the pot is furthest from room temperature. The bench's correction is the graph, not the gadget: start the clock before the reactants meet, plot temperature against time through the mixing and past the peak, and extrapolate the cooling tail back to the mixing instant — the corrected peak is where the leak is refunded. This chapter's designs quote ledger values (the arithmetic the meter would read with zero leak); the Newton correction is named honestly as the bench's own missing face, and the review holds the seam (Sources, flagged sketch 2).

The kettle chain — the meter on the wall. 1.000 L from 20 to 100 °C is 1000 × 4.184 × 80.0 = 334,720 J = 334.7 kJ [computed]. A 2200 W element delivering 80% to the water pushes 1760 W, and the kettle chain runs 190.2 s — 3.17 minutes, which is the time your own kettle takes, and now you know why [computed, B1]. Every lecture that follows is this one line of arithmetic with a different q — the chapter has no other machinery.

The fridge and the drain — the meters that move. Two more wall-meter readings close the lecture. The fridge is the census's trap item: it never makes cold, it moves the kitchen's heat into the kitchen and pays only the move's tax — the coils behind deliver more heat than the motor consumes, a quantity statement named here at recognition level (the coefficient of performance, the classroom's next rung) and deliberately not derived [the named-not-derived discipline again]. And the shower drain: an 80-litre shower raised from 20 to 40 °C carries 80 × 4.184 × 20 = 6694 kJ = 1.86 kWh to the sewer [computed, a design at the class's own 80 L] — the single largest heat number a household throws away daily, twenty times the kettle litre's 0.0930 kWh [computed]. Water heating, not lighting, is where the home's heat budget actually lives — the census's quiet headline.

Lecture 2 — The Benches That Move the Thermometer Without Fire

The kitchen's two great thermometer-moving benches need no flame at all, and both are on this chapter's ledger: mixing (acid and base) and dissolving (salt and water).

The neutralization core. One reaction prices the whole bench: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O, ΔH = −57.1 kJ/mol (E9) [convergent, the classroom band −57 to −58]. Every strong acid with every strong base pays the same figure, because the reaction is the same reaction — the spectator ions just watch. The molecular face the bench actually runs is vinegar's cousin: HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O, same −57.1.

The weak-acid discount. Vinegar is acetic acid, and acetic acid pays less: CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O, ΔH = −55.4 kJ/mol (E10) [convergent]. The missing 1.7 kJ is the deprotonation face — the acid must pay to hand over its proton before the water can form — and the cup can see a gap that small: the two designs (B2a/B2b) read +5.02 °C against +5.17 °C on identical 50/50 mL pots, and the 0.15 °C difference is the weak acid's signature, visible in a coffee cup [computed]. This is thermochemistry's quiet strength: a paper cup resolves kilojoules per mole.

The fizz, honestly colder than the ledger. Vinegar and baking soda — Chapter 10's gas generator, the balloon-filling pair — is endothermic: CH₃COOH + NaHCO₃ → CH₃COONa + CO₂ + H₂O, ΔH ≈ +11.8 kJ/mol (E11) [single-source, the classroom print; the deeper literature's own spread is named in Sources]. The design: 200 g of vinegar with 14.06 g of soda takes up 1975 J, and the ledger predicts −2.10 °C [computed, B2c]. The bench class observes roughly −4 °C — and the gap is not an error, it is unpriced faces: the CO₂ expanding out of solution does work, evaporation rides the fizz's spray, and the dissolved CO₂ has its own endothermic take. The chapter prints the gap as found, because a meter that hides its missing faces is not a meter (flagged sketch 3).

The dissolution bench — five salts, five signatures. Dissolving is chemistry too, and its heats are the cupboard's own map:

  • Sodium hydroxide (lye, Ch 14's own reagent): −44.5 kJ/mol (E12) [convergent]. The drain cleaner warms because the lattice pays. Caustic by heat and by base — binding line 8 exists at this row.
  • Calcium chloride (road salt, Ch 15): −81.3 kJ/mol (E14) [convergent]. The most exothermic common dissolution on the shelf — the design's 2.50 g in 50 g water reads +6.94 °C [computed, B3], and the sidewalk's ice-melting is this row read outdoors: the salt melts the ice and the melt pays the bill back into the pavement.
  • Ammonium nitrate (the cold pack): +25.7 kJ/mol (E13) [convergent]. The pack's 10.0 g takes 3.211 kJ from 50 g of water and the thermometer drops 10.88 °C [computed, B3] — the ankle pays for the bruise's chemistry.
  • Epsom salt (the bath jar): +10 to +20 kJ/mol (E15) [convergent band, printed honestly with its spread — the AP-class print sits at +20, the NBS-derived figure near +12]. The design's 10.0 g reads between −1.37 °C and −2.75 °C depending on where in the band the truth sits [computed, B3]. The chapter does not launder the band; it prices the band.
  • Table salt: +3.88 kJ/mol (E16) [convergent] — nearly nothing, the honest zero of the bench: 5.00 g reads −1.21 °C, at the edge of the cup's resolution [computed, B3].

Five salts, one lesson: the dissolution heat is the lattice's bill or the water's bill, and each solid files its own. No flame lit, and the thermometer moved five different ways.

Lecture 4 — Hess's Law and the Two Heights

The route never matters; the sum always does. If you dissolve solid sodium hydroxide in water (−44.5, E12) and then neutralize the solution with acid (−57.1, E9), you arrive where the direct route arrives: NaOH(s) into acid, ΔH = −101.6 kJ/mol (E17) [computed route sum]. The design verifies it on the bench: 1.00 g of lye into 50.0 mL of 1.00 M acid reads +9.87 °C on the calibrated cup [computed, B6], and the measured class sits at −101 to −105 — the route sum inside the cup's own honest noise. This is Hess's law — ΔH is a state function, the altitude difference, indifferent to the path — and it is the reason the tables work at all: enthalpies can be bookkept like money, summed along any route, and the books always close to the same balance.

Slaking — the exotherm your grandmother knew. Quicklime into water: CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂, ΔH = −64.8 kJ/mol (E18) [convergent, the sources' own spread −63.7 to −64.8 printed as a bracket, not laundered]. The design's 5.60 g into 100 g of water reads +13.32 °C [computed, B6] — the historic lime-water slaking that warmed mortar through freezing nights, priced in a coffee cup. The caustic line (binding 8) lives at this row: the solid goes into the water, slowly.

The reusable hot pack — crystallization priced. Sodium acetate trihydrate, melted at its gentle transition and cooled below its freezing point without solidifying (a supercooled liquid — Chapter 5's own town), crystallizes on a click and pays ΔH = −19.7 kJ/mol (E19) [convergent] — the pack's iron click is a nucleation event, and the heat it releases is the quantity this chapter meters. The pack is "recharged" by re-melting: the same joules carried forward, over and over, a heat battery in the plainest sense — and a ◆ exercise's worth of eventual failure (Exercise 26.6).

THE TWO HEIGHTS — the forward flag paid. Chapter 25 ended with the promise: every reaction owns a barrier (Eₐ, the hill the rate must climb) and a drop (ΔH, the heat the fall releases) — and they are independent numbers. This chapter is where the second height gets its own meter:

  • The candle unlit owns a drop of 41.5 kJ per gram and a barrier so tall that the wax sits on the shelf for years at 20 °C — thermodynamically eager, kinetically asleep, the rust patch's lesson wearing wax's clothes.
  • The hand warmer lit and the rust patch asleep are the same drop (−824.2/mol) at two different barrier arrangements — Chapter 25's four catalyst ladders moved the rate and never the ΔG; here is the other half: surface area and salt and water move the rate and never the ΔH. Rate is negotiable; the drop is not.
  • The hydrogen row's two faces: ΔH° = −285.8 kJ/mol is the heat the flame releases; ΔG° = −237.2 kJ/mol is the work the same reaction can deliver — a fuel cell's ceiling — and the 48.6 kJ difference, 17.0% of the heat, is the work term [convergent values; the distinction named, not derived — flagged sketch 1]. Chapter 12's electrolysis line runs the same seam in reverse: work in can split water at a voltage the heat ledger cannot explain, the overpotential being the toll. Three meters now hang at the bench — ΔG the spontaneity meter (Ch 24's position), ΔH the heat meter (this chapter), K the position's own number — and the fuel cell's promise is that the drop and the deliverable need not coincide.

Lab 26.1 — The Fresh Engine: An Outermost-First Divide-and-Conquer Compositor (Outputs Verbatim)

The chapter's drafting engine is new for this chapter and shares zero code with all four prior engines — not Chapter 24's drafter (a flat left-to-right loop over a stack of Maps), not Chapter 24's skeptic (innermost-group string rewriting), not Chapter 25's drafter (an event-emitting state machine with retro-fixup), not Chapter 25's skeptic (a recursive-descent grammar tree).

The design. The compositor prices a formula by splitting it at its outermost boundaries, recursively, with a memo cache keyed by the exact substring:

  1. Top level first: the charge annotation is stripped (^2-, trailing signs), and the body is split at depth-zero hydrate dots — KAl(SO4)2·12H2O splits before the compositor ever looks inside a bracket.
  2. The outermost group: each dot-free segment is divided at its first top-level bracket group into three pieces — the run before the group, the group's inner content, and the run after its multiplier — and the three pieces are priced separately and recombined (Fe4[Fe(CN)6]3 never scans an innermost pair; it splits at the [, recurses into Fe(CN)6, which splits at its own ().
  3. Memo by substring: every subformula — group contents included — is cached by its exact string, so a repeated motif is priced once (the caches report below: 60 species masses, 34 atom-maps, 54 leaf runs).
  4. The leaf: a bare element run, priced straight off the held table, behind a consumption gate that refuses any character the table cannot price.
  5. The exactness trick: the held table is stored in integer milli-units (every mass ×1000), so the entire balance ledger runs on integers — every mass closure is exact by construction, no floating point anywhere in the accounting.

The atom census mirrors the same divide-and-conquer with its own memo cache. Zero gate refusals occurred: every one of the chapter's 53 species composes on the held twenty (plus manganese, Ch 12's re-admitted pin) — the first chapter of the Atlas to admit no new element.

26.1 ●○○ — The kettle's arithmetic. (a) The heat needed to take 1.50 L of water from 22.0 °C to 100.0 °C; (b) the time at a 2200 W element that delivers 80% to the water; (c) why the real kettle disagrees with the sticker's "boils in 4 minutes." Answer: (a) 1500 × 4.184 × 78.0 = 489,528 J ≈ 489.5 kJ; (b) 489528/1760 = 278.1 s ≈ 4.64 min; (c) the 80% is the design's own assumption — limescale, the starting water's real temperature, and the element's age all move the capture, and the sticker quotes the best day. Ways: the direct chain; the per-litre 334.7 kJ × 1.5 × (22 °C correction) shortcut.

26.2 ●○○ — The spoon test. A 40.0 g aluminum spoon at 95.0 °C goes into 150 g of water at 20.0 °C. (a) The equilibrium temperature; (b) the same spoon in copper's place, and why the answer barely moves. Hints: the specific-heat table is the whole problem; heat lost = heat gained. Answer: (a) 24.06 °C — the aluminum face 2545.5 J exactly equals the water face; (b) copper's 0.385 vs aluminum's 0.897 moves the metal's heat budget, but water's 4.184 dwarfs both — the water's mass is the meter, the metal's identity a small correction. The lesson: the bath wins because of its heat capacity, not its temperature.

26.3 ●●○ — The calibrated cup meets the strong pot. 50.0 mL of 1.00 M HCl and 50.0 mL of 1.00 M NaOH, both at 21.0 °C, meet in the calibrated cup (water-equivalent 10.53 g). (a) The predicted ΔT; (b) the bench reads +5.90 °C — the apparent ΔH; (c) name the missing faces. Hints: E9's −57.1; the cup is water too; the Newton tail. Answer: (a) q = 0.0500 × 57,100 = 2855 J over 462.4 J/°C → +6.17 °C; (b) 110.5 g × 4.184 × 5.90/0.0500 = 54.6 kJ/mol apparent, vs −57.1 true; (c) the 4.4% loss is the cup's lag (it never fully catches the spike), evaporation at the peak, and the truncated cooling tail — the Newton extrapolation exists to refund exactly these, and the refund is partial at best. The meter's honesty is quoting both numbers.

26.4 ●●○ — The camp stove's honest capture. A spirit burner holds 5.00 g of ethanol. (a) The fuel's total heat; (b) at 40% capture to the pot, the water brought from 20 °C to 100 °C; (c) the grams needed for one 250 mL cup. Hints: the kJ/g ladder; the kettle chain per litre. Ways: per gram × capture × the 334.7 kJ/L chain.

26.5 ●●● — The summit: one cup of tea, five meters. The summit's moral: the drop is the fuel's own; the capture is the instrument's — the two chapters in one sentence.

26.7 ◐ — The dwelling's heat census (observational). Walk the house and file twelve items on the heat census: for each, the sign (exo/endo/moves), and where the heat actually comes from or goes. Answer key, ledger-filed: the kettle (exo — the meter's joules into the water); the fridge (moves — it never "makes cold"; the coils pay indoors, the wall pays the move); the cold pack (endo — the water's own heat, spent); the iron hand warmer (exo — rust at powder speed, E8); the reusable hot pack (exo — crystallization, E19); the candle (exo — 41.5 kJ/g, 31.84% captured at the design); the compost bin (exo — slow oxidation, the yard's own ledger); the Epsom bath (endo — slight, the band honest); the gas ring (exo — capture 40%, the 60% the room receives as warmth); the drain (the house's yesterday-heat leaving by the litre-second); the freezer (moves — the meter pays); and the trap entry, the hot pan on the stove (transfer, not a sign — conduction is the movement of heat, not its making). The census's whole lesson is binding line 1 of thermodynamics: distinguish making from moving.
