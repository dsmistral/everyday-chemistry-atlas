Chapter 31 — Food Chemistry: Fermentation, Preservation, and the Chemistry of the Table

The household has been running a chemistry bench for ten thousand years. It keeps its reagents in the pantry, its reactors in the cupboard under the sink, and its instruments in the drawer with the spoons. This is the chapter where the Atlas reads that bench with its own instruments — and where the tasting rule, so absolute everywhere else in this book, finally gets its one careful, fenced exception.

Lecture 0 — The Table as the Oldest Bench

The phenomenon, before any notation. Walk into any kitchen and count the chemical operations already in progress. The sourdough jar on the counter is a continuously operating bioreactor with a wild mixed culture. The vinegar bottle is the spent output of an aerobic oxidation chain. The honey jar is a material that has sat at room temperature for years without spoiling — not because it is fresh, but because its water is chemically spoken for. The pickle jar is an equilibrium system held three rungs below the pH where anything dangerous can grow. The bread from yesterday is browning on its crust through the same amine-plus-sugar reaction that colors a roast, a pretzel, and the top of a crème brûlée. The freezer is a kinetics experiment. The nutrition label on the cracker box is a thermochemistry ledger reduced to four numbers.

Nobody who runs this bench thinks of it as chemistry. That is the point of the chapter. Every instrument this book has built — the mole ledger (Ch 3), the concentration web (Ch 4), the equilibrium meter (Ch 24), the rate meter (Ch 25), the calorimeter (Ch 26) — was already foreshadowed by something in this room, and the food bench is where they all meet in one place, run by everyone, understood by almost no one.

The binding lines. Eleven lines bind this chapter — ten inherited and one new, and the new one is the most important safety sentence in the book:

  1. The tasting rule, scoped. The house rule since Chapter 8 is never taste, never smell — and it holds for every bench product of this book without exception. This chapter has a kitchen side, and the fence is this: food, handled as food, in food-grade vessels, by food rules, may be tasted as food. Nothing that has touched the bench, a bench vessel, or a bench reagent is ever tasted. The rule is not relaxed here; it is mapped, and the map's border is the next line.
  2. The bench/food separation. Dedicated kitchen vessels never return to the bench; bench vessels never reach the kitchen. The pickle jar you compute about in this chapter and the pickle jar in your refrigerator are the same chemistry, but they must never be the same glass.
  3. The sealed-food line (NEW). Never seal food anaerobically. No canning — water-bath or pressure — is performed, scheduled, or described step-by-step in this book. No oil-packing of vegetables or herbs without acid. No vacuum jars, no sealed anaerobic fermentations of low-acid foods. The reason is one organism: Clostridium botulinum, an obligate anaerobe that grows above pH 4.6, whose spores survive boiling water, and whose toxin is among the deadliest known. Every preserved food this chapter computes about is either acidic, salty, sugared, dried, refrigerated, or some combination — and always in a vessel that is open, vented, or elastic (Ch 10's never-latch rule, wearing a kitchen apron).
  4. The toxic-metal fence. Lead, cadmium, arsenic, mercury: museum-only (Ch 22/28). This includes the vintage glazed dishware class — recognition-only.
  5. The never-combination. Bleach and acid, bleach and ammonia: absolute (Ch 8/20/28). The kitchen owns both doors of each pair — the mop bucket and the vinegar bottle live under the same sink.
  6. The pressure line. Nothing latched with gas coming (Ch 10). Fermenting wort, rising dough, and the idling pressure cooker (which this chapter prices but never runs) are all pressure-line territory.
  7. The flame line. No open flame where flammable vapor can reach (Ch 6/21). The Maillard pan and the caramelizing syrup are flame-adjacent, and the grease-fire class belongs to Chapter 23: never water on a burning pan; lid, then heat off.
  8. The peroxide discipline. The 3% brown bottle is a kitchen disinfectant and a reagent (Ch 16); the concentrations above it are the Ch 15/16 ceiling, unchanged.
  9. The scale line. Kitchen chemistry is gram-and-liter chemistry; the response numbers of Chapter 28 (water ≤30 mL for a child, ≤250 mL for an adult, within 30 minutes; never induce vomiting) are re-pinned in the safety table.
  10. The response line. Poison control is part of the household's instrument set, exactly as Chapter 28 defined it.
  11. The sealed-source line. Nuclear topics stay at Chapter 29's recognition level (the smoke detector is not a toy); it appears here only because irradiated food ("cold pasteurization") is a label term the table might meet — recognition-only.

Count machine-checked: eleven, and the kitchen-side lines (1, 2, 3) are three fences around one idea — the food bench is safe exactly because its hazards are organized.

The element discipline. This chapter admits zero new elements — the sixth zero-admission chapter of the Atlas (Ch 26/27/28/29/30 before it). Everything on the food bench is built from the held twenty-two (the held-21 plus silicon, Ch 14/30). Even phosphorus — the backbone of ATP and DNA, the P of every recipe this chapter does not compute — stays refused at the gate: the food-energy ledger this book prices runs on carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sodium, potassium, calcium, sulfur, and chlorine, and the refusal is the honest statement of scope, not an oversight. The engine's gate log prints it.

One idea for the chapter. Every food preservation method ever invented is one of three moves, or a combination: kill the organisms' chemistry (acid, heat — recognition-only for the sealed kinds), starve it of water it can use (salt, sugar, drying), or slow it down (cold). Fermentation is the fourth move — the one that does all three at once for you: the culture acidifies its own medium, consumes the sugars, and leaves a product no pathogen can colonize. The chapter walks those four moves in order.

Lecture 1 — Fermentation: Redox Without Oxygen

The electron ledger. Chapter 11 built the redox ledger: oxidation is losing electrons, reduction is gaining them. Chapter 7 priced combustion as the fast version of that ledger. Fermentation is the same ledger run without an external oxidizer: the sugar oxidizes itself, one fragment accepting the electrons another fragment releases. No oxygen is needed, and none is consumed — which is why a dough rises in a sealed oven and a beer ferments in a closed tank.

The bookkeeping, in the Atlas's element table (all masses g/mol, this chapter's own engine, Lab 31.1):

E1 — alcoholic fermentation (yeast): $$\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 \longrightarrow 2\,C_2H_5OH + 2\,CO_2}$$

Glucose (180.156) becomes two ethanol (46.069 each) plus two carbon dioxide (44.009 each). The mass closes exactly: 180.156 = 92.138 + 88.018. In the glucose, carbon's average oxidation state is 0; in the ethanol the two carbons sit at −2 and −3 averaged (mostly reduced), and in the CO₂ the carbon is fully oxidized at +4. One molecule performs both halves of a redox reaction on itself.

E2 — homolactic fermentation (the pickle's culture): $$\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 \longrightarrow 2\,C_3H_6O_3}$$

Glucose becomes two lactic acid (90.078 each): 180.156 = 180.156. Same trick, different landing: the electrons that would have gone to CO₂ reduce the second fragment instead. This is the sauerkraut, yogurt, and sourdough reaction, and its product is the acid that preserves the medium.

E3 — heterolactic: $\mathrm{C_6H_{12}O_6 \rightarrow C_3H_6O_3 + C_2H_5OH + CO_2}$ — one lactic acid, one ethanol, one carbon dioxide, still closing exactly (180.156 both sides): the same three-way split some lactobacilli run.

E4 — sucrose, the table sugar of the pantry: $\mathrm{C_{12}H_{22}O_{11} + H_2O \rightarrow 4\,C_2H_5OH + 4\,CO_2}$ — the sucrose hydrolyzes (Ch 23's ester-hydrolysis cousin, run on an acetal instead) and the two hexose halves ferment; the water is a reagent, which is why the yield bases differ by whether you count it. The balance closes: 360.312 both sides.

The yields, priced. From 1.000 kg of glucose (Lab 31.2, ledger B1): 5.5507 mol of glucose → 11.1015 mol ethanol = 511.43 g, plus 488.57 g of CO₂ — the mass splits 51.14% / 48.86%, the yield pins Chapter 21 printed at its fermentation summit, reproduced here by a different engine on a different day. Per the sucrose route (E4), 1.000 kg of sucrose (2.9214 mol, Ch 21's own chain) yields the same 11.6858 mol ethanol — the sugar-plus-water basis and the products' basis agree because the water is consumed, and Chapter 21's three yield bases (51.14% on glucose; 51.14% on sucrose-plus-water; 53.84% on sucrose alone) are the same ledger seen from three doors.

The bread, priced. Why does dough rise? Because the yeast runs E1 and keeps the CO₂, which cannot escape fast enough from a gluten network. Ledger B1 prices the loaf's leavening: 5.00 g of sucrose in the dough → 0.0584 mol CO₂ → 1.429 L at the 24.465 L/mol pin (Ch 10); the same 5.00 g as glucose → 1.358 L. A tablespoon of sugar in a loaf buys about one and a half liters of gas — the entire architecture of a crumb, priced from a teaspoon.

The rate side. Chapter 25 built the clock: yeast runs fastest in the 26–37 °C window, slows below, and dies above ~45–50 °C. Every bread recipe's "warm place, about an hour" is Chapter 25's Arrhenius ladder in prose. The lactic cultures of the pickle jar run the cooler edge of the same window — which is why a sauerkraut crock lives in the cellar, not by the stove, and why Chapter 25's Q10 ≈ 2 returns in Lecture 3 as the refrigerator's whole operating principle.

The ethanol, fenced. The ethanol of E1 is the same molecule Chapter 21 priced at 29.67 kJ/g — and Chapter 21's fences are inherited unchanged: the beverage question is settled by the never-potable line's own arithmetic (Ch 21's RA-2 argument — the first fraction out of a household still is the worst fraction), and nothing in this chapter distills anything. The beer-brewing trade is recognition-only: this book computes its stoichiometry and never runs its process.

Lecture 2 — The Vinegar Door: Acetification and the Pickle's pH

The second fermentation. Leave wine open to air and it does not spoil into something random; it spoils directionally, into vinegar. The chemistry is E5:

$$\mathrm{C_2H_5OH + O_2 \longrightarrow C_2H_4O_2 + H_2O}$$

Ethanol (46.069) plus oxygen (31.998) becomes acetic acid (60.052) plus water (18.015): 78.067 both sides, exact. Where E1 ran the redox ledger internally, E5 runs it against atmospheric oxygen — which is why acetification is obligately aerobic, why a vinegar mother forms only where air meets liquid, and why the "mother of vinegar" (the pale gelatinous mat in an unpasteurized bottle) is a pellicle of acetic-acid bacteria holding itself at the oxygen interface. The culture is a catalyst with a habitat: it eats the ethanol, keeps the reaction's product, and floats at the only place its reagent reaches.

The yield, priced. Ledger B2 walks a wine to its vinegar: a 12.0% v/v wine carries 94.68 g of ethanol per liter (120 mL × 0.789 g/mL, Ch 21's density pin), i.e. 2.0552 mol — which, fully acetified, would deliver 123.42 g of acetic acid per liter. The table vinegar of the pantry shelf is 5.00% w/w at ρ ≈ 1.006 — 50.30 g/L, the 0.8376 M pin of Chapters 4 and 8, reproduced by this chapter's own arithmetic in B2's re-pin line. A wine fully acetified lands at 2.45 times the shelf vinegar's acidity, which is one honest reason household vinegar making starts from dilute alcohol and ends with dilution or partial conversion.

The acid, and the divide it owns. Acetic acid is a weak acid (Ka = 1.76 × 10⁻⁵, pKa 4.75 — Ch 8's pin), and Chapter 8 priced the bottle itself: 0.8376 M gives pH 2.41, with only 0.46% of the molecules ionized. But food preservation does not run on the acid's strength; it runs on the environment the acid makes. The food-safety divide is a single number: pH 4.6. Below it, C. botulinum will not grow or form toxin [convergent, USDA/FSIS and the food-safety literature]; above it, in a low-oxygen environment, it can. That number — not 7, not 4, not 3 — is the entire reason the pickle aisle exists, and it is the line the sealed-food fence guards. (Honesty: the 4.6 divide is a regulatory-safety convention, with occasional non-proteolytic strains reported below it under special conditions; the canning industry's own literature says as much, and this book simply refuses to operate anywhere near the boundary.)

The pickle's arithmetic. The quick pickle — this chapter's one designed kitchen bench (Exercise 31.5, never sealed) — is vinegar's pH carried into a vegetable. E9 is the soda equation of Chapter 8 returning in a jar:

$$\mathrm{C_2H_4O_2 + NaHCO_3 \longrightarrow NaC_2H_3O_2 + H_2O + CO_2}$$

and E10 is the descale of Chapters 8 and 9, organified — limestone and vinegar making calcium acetate:

$$\mathrm{CaCO_3 + 2\,C_2H_4O_2 \longrightarrow Ca(C_2H_3O_2)_2 + H_2O + CO_2}$$

(The balance of E10 is this chapter's own honesty event: the drafting run first wrote two CO₂ on the right — the engine's atom gate refused it, because the carbonate's three oxygens pay for exactly one CO₂ and one H₂O, and the corrected equation closes at 220.190 both sides. The catch is printed in the lab's honesty record, as found.) E10 is also the traditional "egg preserved in vinegar" reaction — the shell dissolves over days, and the vinegar's acid is consumed doing it, which is why a preserving vinegar is never reused for a preserving pickle: the acid spent on the shell is the acid that was guarding the food.

Why brine turns acid on its own. E12 is the quiet equation under every lacto-fermentation: $\mathrm{NaC_2H_3O_2 + HCl \rightarrow C_2H_4O_2 + NaCl}$. Run it left to right and it is the salt of the acid giving its proton back to chloride's water; run it right to left and it is exactly what a fermenting vegetable's own chemistry prevents — the buffer. Chapter 24 built the acetate buffer (pH = pKa + log [base]/[acid], the 4.745 pair) and Exercise 31.4 uses it: a brine that starts at vinegar's pH holds below 4.6 not by strength but by the buffer's teeth — the same teeth that hold blood at 7.40.

Lecture 3 — Preservation as Water Control: The Water-Activity Bench

The idea. Microorganisms do not drink water; they import it across membranes that respond to water's escaping tendency, not its amount. The name for that escaping tendency is water activity, aw = p/p₀ — the vapor pressure of the water in the food over the vapor pressure of pure water at the same temperature (Ch 6's vapor-pressure lecture, wearing a food-science badge). Pure water: aw = 1.00. The drier, saltier, or sweeter the matrix, the lower the aw, and each organism has a floor:

OrganismMinimum awThe datum
Most spoilage bacteria~0.90the classic curve
Staphylococcus aureus0.85the best-adapted pathogen [convergent]
Clostridium botulinum~0.93grows only in wet-enough food [convergent]
Most molds0.70–0.80the visible frontier
Xerophilic molds0.61–0.62the dry edge of life [convergent]

Three numbers organize the entire pantry: 0.85 is the line below which Staphylococcus — the most salt-tolerant pathogen — cannot grow; 0.61 is the driest any mold can manage; and 0.93 is the floor for the botulinum of the sealed-food line. A food held below 0.85 needs no refrigerator for those organisms; a food below 0.61 is essentially immortal on the shelf. Honey sits below the mold floor — which is why a jar of honey from an Egyptian tomb was still honey — and its low aw is also the reason the spores in it never germinate in the jar, but can in an infant (Lecture 4's fence).

Salt: the oldest water thief. Dissolved sodium chloride binds water into its hydration sphere (Ch 4) and lowers the vapor pressure — Chapter 4's colligative depression, read as a preservation instrument. Ledger B3 prices the brines: at 2% NaCl the ideal Raoult model (with the ν = 2 of the dissociated pair) puts x_w at 0.9878; at 3.5% (the classic cucumber brine) 0.9789; at 5% 0.9701. The trade's own working band — 2–5% salt for vegetable ferments [convergent, the fermentation literature] — is the interval where lactobacilli outcompete spoilers while texture survives: enough water theft to slow the competitors, not enough to stop the culture. The model's honesty is printed in B3 as the chapter's flagged seam: at saturation (26.4% NaCl) the ideal model reads 0.8600 while the measured value is 0.755 [convergent] — the ideal Raoult over-reads by 0.105 at ionic strength this high, the activity lesson of Chapter 24's gypsum and limewater seams, back on the food bench. The measured 0.755 is the number the salted-fish trade ran on for centuries: saturated brine preserves not because it is "very salty" but because it holds aw at 0.755, below every bacterium's floor and all but the xerophilic molds'.

Sugar: the same thief, sweeter. The jam kettle concentrates sucrose until the water is outnumbered. The trade's standard jam finishes at 60–65% soluble solids [convergent]; at 65% (in the food's own water) B3 computes 5.426 molal sucrose and an ideal x_w of 0.9110 — model-only, flagged; the real pectin-and-sugar matrix sits lower, which is why a proper jam seals itself against everything but molds, and why a scraped jar eventually blooms. The convergent anchors bracket the model: 1 molal sugar holds aw 0.98, 1 molal NaCl 0.9669 — the saltier thief per mole, exactly as ν = 2 predicts.

Drying and honey. Air-drying removes the water outright (jerky, dried fruit, pasta, the spice rack); honey and molasses are self-drying — so concentrated that their own water has no activity left to give. Every "why doesn't this spoil?" question in the pantry answers in aw.

Cold: the rate thief. Chapter 25 priced the thermometer's effect on rates: Q10 ≈ 1.95 near room temperature (the 51.2 kJ/mol class). The refrigerator is that number, weaponized: at 4 °C versus 25 °C, B4 computes the spoilage clock running 4.07× slower; the freezer at −18 °C, 17.7× slower — plus ice's removal of liquid water, the second water control stacked on the first. The food-safety "danger zone" — 4 to 63 °C [convergent] — is Chapter 25's ladder with a regulatory name: two hours in the zone is the discard rule, and every picnic cooler is a kinetics argument.

Heat and the sealed line. Heat is the third thief — it kills outright. Boiling water (100 °C) destroys every food pathogen's vegetative cells but not botulinum spores, which is why the pH 4.6 divide exists at all: acid foods are safe from the spore because the spore cannot germinate in them, so a boiling water-bath suffices; low-acid foods require the spore-destroying temperatures — 240–250 °F, i.e. 115.56–121.11 °C — that only a pressure vessel reaches [convergent, the extension services; the 121 °C figure is Ch 6's own pressure-cooker pin, repriced in B4]. And there is the fence: this book never runs the pressure vessel on food (the sealed-food line; Chapter 6's never-sealed line was always the parent of it). The home canning pressure cooker is a competent, industrially-validated instrument in skilled hands — and it is precisely the instrument this book refuses to teach, because the failure mode of a nearly right low-acid canning run is the toxin of the obligate anaerobe the run was designed to kill. Recognition-only: priced (B4), never scheduled.

The four moves, assembled. Acid (E1–E5), water theft (salt/sugar/dry), cold (the clock), heat (the kill, fenced). Fermentation is the fifth column: it makes the acid for you, spends the sugar for you, and leaves a medium no pathogen can take. The refrigerator pickle of Exercise 31.5 stacks three moves (acid + salt + cold) and leaves the sealed fourth to the store.

Lecture 4 — Browning: Two Chemistries, One Color

The phenomenon. Cut an apple and it browns in minutes; bake a bread and its crust browns in an oven; burn the sugar in a pan and it browns a third way. All three are oxidations of food molecules, and the entire lecture is the discipline of telling them apart — because one is an enzyme you can stop, one is a reaction you can only time, and one is a decomposition you can only catch at the right stage.

Enzymatic browning — the one you can stop. The apple's own polyphenol oxidase (PPO) converts its phenolic substrates (catechol is the model compound) to quinones, which polymerize to the brown melanins. E7, the model:

$$\mathrm{2\,C_6H_6O_2 + O_2 \longrightarrow 2\,C_6H_4O_2 + 2\,H_2O}$$

Two catechol (110.112) plus oxygen (31.998) become two o-benzoquinone (108.096) plus two water: 252.222 both sides, exact. The reaction needs all three of PPO's requirements — oxygen, the enzyme, and the substrate — and the kitchen blocks each one: lemon juice brings ascorbic acid, the chemical reductant (E6, the vitamin-C shield Chapter 25's apple wore: C₆H₈O₆ + ½O₂ → C₆H₆O₆ + H₂O, priced in B5 at the 1.012 g pin — 5.746 mmol, enough to consume 2.873 mmol of O₂, 70.3 mL at the 24.465 pin) and citric acid, the chelator that ties up the copper in PPO's active site [convergent, the enzymatic-browning literature]; blanching denatures the enzyme (Ch 25's protein rule); the fridge slows the clock (Q10 again). The lemons-on-the-fruit-salad habit is one of the oldest applied-kinetics protocols on earth, and it is Chapters 25 and 16 holding hands.

The sulfite door — inherited, re-pinned. The trade's other anti-browning reagent is the one this book already owns: sulfite, Chapter 17's own +4 shelf. The dried-fruit trade's bright apricots are E8/E15 chemistry: Na₂S₂O₅ + H₂O → 2 NaHSO₃ (190.095 + 18.015 = 208.110 = 2 × 104.055, exact), and under acid the bisulfite releases SO₂ (64.058, Ch 17's gas-train discipline unchanged: generated at match scale, consumed in situ, never bottled, never indoors). Ledger B5 re-prices the Campden tablet at Ch 17/25's pin: 0.44 g of metabisulfite = 2.315 mmol = 4.629 mmol of HSO₃⁻, and the SO₂ content closes on both bases (stoichiometric 4.6293 mmol; Ch 17's 67.40% available-SO₂ convention 4.6296 mmol — a 0.006% agreement, the pin's own 4-sig-fig rounding, and the gate passes at the honest tolerance). The fence: sulfites are a declared allergen class — the 10 ppm label threshold [convergent] — and the raw-produce sulfiting that sickened asthmatics in the salad bars of the 1970s–80s is banned outright. Recognition, and the label read as chemistry.

Nitrite — recognized, never run. The cured meat's pink and the botulism safety of the sausage are the same reagent: sodium nitrite (NaNO₂, 68.995, this chapter's own composition), which both fixes the myoglobin color and inhibits C. botulinum [convergent, the meat-curing literature]. The trade is heavily regulated for exactly the reasons Lecture 3 introduced, and this book's fence is total: no curing recipe, no proportions, no bench — recognition-only, the additive's label name and function taught, nothing else. The "uncured" label's celery-powder irony (vegetable nitrate reduced to the same nitrite in the same meat) is printed in the trade's own literature and changes nothing at the bench: the fence is the reagent class, not the label.

The honey fence. Honey's low aw preserves the honey — and preserves any C. botulinum spores in it ungerminated but alive, which a mature gut handles and an infant's does not. The public-health line is absolute and carries this book's weight: no honey for anyone under twelve months [convergent — the public-health agencies name honey the one dietary reservoir definitively linked to infant botulism]. It is the sealed-food line's smallest expression: an anaerobic, wet, low-acid environment is exactly the place Lecture 3 refuses to create — and an infant's immature gut is that environment already.

Non-enzymatic browning — Maillard. The crust, the roast, the pretzel's shellac, the toasted marshmallow: reducing sugars plus amino groups, run through a cascade nobody fully maps, ending in the brown melanoidin polymers. The Maillard reaction has no sharp threshold — it runs at any temperature, merely faster as heat rises, roughly doubling per 10 °C, becoming the dominant browning route on cooking timescales above ~110 °C and running hardest through 140–165 °C [convergent]. The engine's smallest partner, glycine (C₂H₅NO₂, 75.067), is the model amine — the lab's composition battery prices it knowledge-only; the full cascade is flagged sketch territory this book names and does not pretend to balance. (The reaction even gives this book one honest schadenfreude: the same chemistry that makes the crust makes the acrylamide that makes the alarmist headline — dose and context, as ever, are the whole story.)

Non-enzymatic browning — caramelization, the sugar-only route. No amines needed: each sugar decomposes in its own temperature band — fructose the eager one near ~110 °C, glucose mid-shelf near ~150 °C, sucrose the patient one at ~160 °C [convergent, the confectioner's tables] — and the pan runs the ladder: thread, ball, crack, and finally color at the moment the water is gone and the sucrose itself starts to convert. The safety-table row is Chapter 5's candy line: syrup above 160 °C is a liquid that burns skin on contact and keeps its heat — oven mitts, cold water ready, never the wet spoon splash.

Telling the two apart. One sentence each: enzymatic needs oxygen and life, runs at room temperature in minutes, and stops at acid, cold, or heat-denatured enzyme; Maillard needs protein and runs in the dry heat of an oven, 140–165 °C the sweet spot; caramelization needs neither protein nor life — sugar alone in its own band. An apple slice tests the first; the bread crust the second; the crème brûlée's glassy lid the third.

Rancidity — the shelf's slow browning. Chapter 23 priced the oxidative rancidity of fats (the IV ladder, the tocopherol/BHA-BHT quenchers at the 0.02% GRAS cap) and the hydrolytic kind (the free-acid grade). N96's iodine-number face (92.5 mg/g, the corpus's own datum) belongs here: the same double-bond census that Chapter 23 ran on the drying-oil shelf is the one that prices a nut's shelf life. The fridge, again, is Q10.

Lecture 5 — The Label as Ledger: Additives, Energy, and the Network

The additive census — a small acid-base chapter. The preservation shelf's synthetic helpers are weak acids and their salts, and their whole mechanism is the undissociated molecule crossing the microbe's membrane and letting go of its proton inside: which is why each works only below its pKa. Benzoic acid (C₇H₆O₂, 122.123, this chapter's own composition) with pKa 4.2 [convergent] and its sodium salt (144.105) guard the acidic drinks and condiments; sorbic acid's potassium salt (150.218) the same window; and the sulfites of Lecture 4 are the third family. Two recognition-only fences ride with them: the benzoate + ascorbic-acid pair can trace-form benzene in drinks under light and heat [convergent — the FDA's 2006 survey is the recognition datum; the levels, the mechanism, and the reformulation responses are the trade's], and this book's response is the label read as chemistry, nothing poured, nothing tested. (The triad — benzoate 211/212, sorbate 202, sulfite 220–228, ascorbate 300 — is a deck of little acid-base problems hiding in an ingredients list.)

The nutrition label — Chapter 26, back for its receipt. The Atwater factors — 4 kcal/g protein, 4 kcal/g carbohydrate, 9 kcal/g fat, 7 kcal/g alcohol [convergent] — were priced at Chapter 26's food bench, and ledger B6 runs a label through the engine: a 30 g serving with 17 g carbohydrate, 1 g protein, 8 g fat → 68 + 4 + 72 = 144 kcal (the label's print, within its own rounding rule). The factors themselves are the honest seams: ethanol computes to 7.092 kcal/g against the printed 7 (−1.3%); fat's 9 kcal = 37.656 kJ/g against triolein's own 39.64 kJ/g — the ~95% discount Chapter 23 printed (95.1% on the rounded 37.7 print; 94.99% on the exact 37.656 — the rounding-history datum, both named); and carbohydrate's 4 kcal = 16.736 kJ/g sits 1.5% above sucrose's own 16.489 — because the factor is a population average across starches and sugars with a fiber discount, not any one molecule's ΔHc. A nutrition label is not a bomb calorimeter's readout; it is an empirically corrected average, and knowing which is which is the difference between a number and an instrument.

Sodium arithmetic. The label's "140 mg sodium" is NaCl's sodium fraction at work: Na/NaCl = 22.990/58.443 = 39.34%, so 140 mg Na ≡ 355.9 mg of salt (and the day's 2300 mg Na ≡ 5.85 g) — the mole ledger of Chapter 3 in a milligram costume.

Flavor — Chapter 23's flag, honored. The fruit-ester family (Ch 23's E13/E14, smelled at a distance) is the flavor industry's backbone: the pineapple note of ethyl butyrate, the pear of isoamyl acetate, the wintergreen of methyl salicylate with its Ch 23/8 tasting-rule fence (1 mL ≈ 1.4 g ≈ 21–22 adult aspirin tablets — the corrected equivalence of Ch 23's review, re-pinned). Lab 31.1 — The Composition Engine: A Reverse Walk Compositor

Engine 15. This chapter's drafting engine is a reverse walk compositor — a design shared with none of the fourteen prior engines. The formula segment is reversed and walked left to right; in that reading, digit strings precede the token they multiply and must be read back-to-front ("21" in the reversed string is the 12 of C12H22O11); lowercase letters precede the uppercase that ends their symbol (a then C in the reversed string is Ca); a reversed ) opens the group that the reversed ( closes; and a leftover trailing digit-run — digits with nothing after them to multiply — is the segment's original leading coefficient (O2H2 walked backwards is 2H2O, and the engine knows it). The hydrate dot splits segments before reversing; each segment's own leading coefficient applies to that segment alone, never inherited across the dot. Exercise 31.1 (●○○) — The vinegar bottle, read two ways

The pantry label says "diluted with water to 5% acidity (50 grains)"; the hardware shelf's bottle says "6% acidity — cleaning."

(a) Show that the 5% bottle is the book's vinegar pin: 0.8376 M. (b) What is the 6% cleaning bottle's molarity, on the stated density assumption?

Hints. (a) Two doors: Chapter 2's label arithmetic (w/w → grams per liter through density) or Chapter 4's concentration web. (b) The 6% trade convention is w/w like its sister; a density must be assumed and the assumption labeled.

Worked. (a) 5.00% w/w at ρ 1.006 g/mL: 1000 mL × 1.006 g/mL × 0.0500 = 50.30 g acetic per liter; ÷ 60.052 g/mol = 0.8376 M — the pin of Chapters 4 and 8, reproduced by this chapter's own arithmetic (Gate G6). Ways: P1 the mole ledger (Ch 3) walking the label (Ch 2); P6 the concentration web (Ch 4) — the same number from the label's two conventions. (b) On ρ 1.008 [the flagged assumption]: 1000 × 1.008 × 0.0600 = 60.48 g/L → 1.0071 M — and the exercise's honest coda: the cleaning bottle is a concentrated food-grade acid by label arithmetic, and it never crosses the bench/food fence in either direction (cleaning vinegar cleans; pickling vinegar pickles; the same molecule, two fences apart).

Exercise 31.2 (●○○) — The 3.5% brine

Exercise 31.4 (●●○) — The buffer under the pickle

A brine starts as 1.00 L of 0.8376 M vinegar; 10.0 g of sodium acetate trihydrate (NaC₂H₃O₂·3H₂O, 136.079 g/mol) dissolves in it from the vegetables' own salts. Compute the pH both ways.
