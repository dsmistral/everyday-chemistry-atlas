Lecture 1 — The lever, the wedge, the pulley: mechanical advantage priced

Mechanical advantage is a ratio, and the engine prices it exactly. The lever's MA is the effort arm over the load arm — a 2.00 m effort arm pushing on a 0.50 m load arm is MA 4.000, exactly, and a 300 N load then costs 75.0 N of effort at the far end [computed, E1–E2, the SB grid: 200/50 = 4.000 and 300/4 = 75.0]. The pulley's MA is the count of rope segments supporting the load — a 2000 N load on four supporting segments costs 500.0 N of pull, and the fixed pulley at the top of the line is 1:1, a direction changer, not a force multiplier [computed, E3]. The wedge is the inclined plane worn as a tool — the same ratio, worn thin, and the trade's own honesty is that friction owns the wedge: what it gains in force it spends against the wood, and the splitting maul's ratio is the lever's with a shorter temper [register: the trade's own rule].

The honest limits, stated where the arithmetic is. Mechanical advantage trades distance for force, never work for work: the 4.000 lever that turns 75.0 N into 300 N moves the effort end four times as far as the load end moves, and no geometry on earth changes that ledger [register: the conservation line, Ch 7's own discipline honored]. Real pulleys pay friction — every sheave costs a share of the gain, which is why the trade's tackle counts its segments honestly and adds its friction allowance on top; real levers bend — which is why the crowbar is steel and the fulcrum is a stone or a steel plate, not a hope [convergent, the trade's own practice]. Lecture 2 — Gravity and sequence: felling, tipping, undermining

Gravity does the work; the cut only chooses the direction. The hand-felling canon prices the notch: the face cut runs about a third of the trunk's diameter deep, and the hinge — the uncut wood between the notch and the back cut — holds about a tenth of the trunk's thickness, and that tenth is the whole steering gear [convergent, the hand-felling canon; computed, E6 — a 0.90 m trunk prices a 0.30 m notch and a 0.09 m hinge, exactly]. The sequence is the method: face notch on the fall side, back cut above it on the opposite side, stopping short of the hinge — the trunk hinges on its own remaining tenth, the planet pulls, and the tree goes where the notch points [convergent, the trade's own order]. The engine prices the hinge at its row and refuses to price anything it cannot: the direction is geometry, the timing is judgment, and neither is a number [register: the recognition line].

Tipping, priced: the wall falls when the push beats the base. A wall tips about the downhill edge of its own footing, and the ledger is two lever arms: the wall's weight acting at its center, the push acting at the top. A wall 3.00 m tall on a 0.60 m footing — weight at 0.30 m from the edge, push at 3.00 m — tips when the push reaches 0.100 W: one-tenth of the wall's own weight, applied at the top, and the engine prices the ratio exactly [computed, E5, the SB grid: 30/300 = 0.100]. The demolition trade's undermining method is the same ledger run upside down: remove the footing's far side and the wall's own weight becomes the push, which is why undermining is done from outside the fall zone, in sequence, with the drop zone cleared [convergent, the demolition canon; Safety row 8].

Undermining and the controlled pull-down. The pre-machine trade pulled walls with sequence alone: undermine the base on the fall side, then pull with a winch line, a block and tackle, or a team — the pull is E3's arithmetic wearing a rope, and the block and tackle's MA is the supporting-segment count, priced [computed, E3's discipline applied; convergent, the trade's own gear]. The machine age did not abandon this; it motorized it — the hydraulic wedge, the pusher ram, the puller — and the modern industry's mainline is still gravity's own method: plan the sequence, cut the structure's sequence points, let the planet do the lifting [convergent, the demolition canon].

Lecture 3 — Fire as a tool: fire-setting, spalling, and the vinegar quench

Fire-setting is the oldest mining method there is, and it worked for three thousand years. The canon, read at distance [convergent, the register's own sources]: the Copper-Age workings of Hérault carry the traces; Diodorus Siculus describes the method around 60 BC — the rock heated by fire and struck with iron; Pliny the Elder carries it in the Natural History's book on gold mining (Book XXXIII); and the trade's own physics was always the same — heat the rock face with a sustained wood fire, then quench it, and the face cracks. Opencast fire-setting was safer than shaft work for a reason the air chapter already owns: ventilation [register: the FC11 inheritance, honored]. The method's history closes with two dates the census proved NEW: black powder entered mining at Selmecbánya in 1627 (Banská Štiavnica), and Nobel's dynamite in 1867 made fire-setting redundant in a decade [convergent, the dates at their sources; NEW, zero-hit before the freeze].

The physics: differential expansion, tensile failure, and the spall. Rapid heating alone breaks rock — different minerals expand at different rates, and the heated skin pulls against the cool core until the weaker one fails in tension [convergent]. The spall is that failure wearing a name: granite's thermal conductivity is low, so a fire builds a steep gradient in the outer centimeters; water-stored granite spalls explosively when the pore pressure and the thermal gradient gang up; and the failure is always tensile — rock is strong in compression and weak in exactly the way fire attacks [convergent, the spalling physics at its sources]. The word itself is a disclosed prior, owned two doors down: Chapter 30 printed steam spalling twice — Exercise 30.3's kiln spalling and the cracked-brick exercise's steam — and this chapter borrows the mechanism, names the owner, and adds fire's own face [pin: "spalling," Ch 30 ×2, disclosed prior].

The ceramics trade's own hygiene — slow ramps through 573 °C, both passes, ≤50 °C per hour on the cooling side — is dunting prevention, and dunting is the word the census proved NEW [convergent, the ceramics canon; NEW: dunting, 0.45%, zero-hit].

Lecture 5 — Water, ice, and mortar: the patient shelf

The freeze wedges what the fire only cracks. Water expands about 9% when it freezes — the exact fraction on the engine's grid is 9.051% (83/917, the expansion of ice at 0.917 g/mL against water's 1.000) [computed, E10, the SB grid exact; NEW: 0.917 g/mL, zero-hit — Ch 4 never printed ice's density]. The pressure that expansion carries is enormous: ice growing in a confined pore at −22 °C can reach 207 MPa [convergent, the frost-wedding sources; NEW: 207 MPa and −22 °C, zero-hit], and the freeze is most active between roughly −3 and −8 °C — the band where the phase change is fastest [convergent]. The construction datum rides with it: freeze-thaw damage begins when a porous material's volumetric moisture passes about 91% saturation — the pores need near-full water to wedge, not damp [convergent]. The overflow arithmetic prices the whole mechanism at a kitchen scale: a sealed-full 1 L container of water freezes to 1090.5 mL of ice, and 90.51 mL has nowhere to go [computed, E11 — 1000 × 83/917, exact]. That is the frost's whole method in one row: the rock's pores are the container, the freeze is the fill, and the wedge is the overflow [computed, the mechanism stated at its row; Safety row 7].
