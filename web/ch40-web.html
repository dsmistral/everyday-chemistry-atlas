Vol VI · Chapter 40 — Magnesium and Calcium: The Molten-Salt Pair

A ruined world is rich in exactly two things this chapter needs and cannot buy: magnesium and calcium, present in every bone, every shell, every brick of limestone, every bath-flake of Epsom salt — and absent, as metals, from every shelf in the house. This is the campaign's fourth chapter, and the first one with metal in the yield column: a pair of elements the household owns as ions the way it never owned lithium, run through the same gate every industrial cell runs through — the molten salt — and priced at the bench the way the Atlas prices everything, honestly. The chapter's title datum is a surprise the corpus's own performer did not expect: magnesium, made in its own melt, came out heavier than the salt it was born in. The chapter's title problem is the one that killed calcium at the same bench for a century: the metal that dissolves back into its own molten salt as fast as you make it. One melt, two metals, two opposite design problems, and the same sixty milligrams and six hundred and ten milligrams of honest product at the end.

Lecture 1 — What the Household Actually Owns (the anatomy, two ions and a ribbon)

Start with the inventory, the Chapter 2 way, because the pair's inventory is the richest in the book. Magnesium, as an ion, the household owns in the bath and the spice of the garden: Epsom salt — magnesium sulfate, the bitter salt Chapter 26 already priced as a bench staple of its own shelf — the ice-melt bag beside the door (magnesium chloride, the very hexahydrate this chapter will charge into a crucible), the chlorophyll of every green leaf (the metal ion seated at the center of the light-catcher, recognition-class), and the milk of magnesia in the medicine cabinet. [recognition-only: the household roster; the salts' chemistry is pinned in Chapters 26 and 14] Calcium, as an ion, the household owns in its own skeleton: the bone ash of the kitchen's spent carcass bucket (hydroxyapatite, Ca₅(PO₄)₃OH, 502.307 [computed, Engine 55, row C20] — and 39.89% calcium by mass [computed, row C21], a figure one careless web source prints as 39.54% and this chapter refuses), the seashell and the limestone and the chalk (calcium carbonate, Chapter 14's own trunk mineral, pinned at 100.086 in nineteen chapters of this book), the plaster on the wall, the quicklime and slaked lime of Chapter 14's cycle (56.077 and 74.092, Chapter 30's owner pin for the oxide), the calcium chloride of the dehumidifier and the ice-melt. And the metals: a single ribbon. The one place the household meets metallic magnesium is the camping fire-starter — shaved iron filings and magnesium ribbon in a block, the flare that lights the wet wood — and it was bought. That is the whole inventory of the pair's metals in a normal house, and it is the P21 verdict in one sentence before the chapter earns the longer one: the campaign's fire-starter face is a shelf item, not a bench product [Ch 19 owns the fire-starter face, ×4 on its own page].

The ion-not-metal rule, inherited from lithium's chapter, wears differently here, and the difference matters. The household's lithium was sealed inside devices; the household's magnesium and calcium sit in open bags in the kitchen and the shed. The route is not blocked by packaging — it is blocked by reactivity and by nothing else. Calcium metal reacts with cold water, visibly, hydrogen bubbles at room temperature [convergent]; magnesium holds out against the liquid (it wants steam) but both oxides are among the most stable lattices in chemistry, and carbon will not reduce either one at any temperature a bench or a household furnace reaches [convergent: the industrial route statement]. Sodium can displace calcium from its fused salt — the route one corpus bench actually ran (N178, Lecture 4) — but sodium is itself a campaign product, and paying sodium for calcium is buying metal with metal. The one road that does not cost a rarer metal is the road the industry took: electrolysis of the molten chloride. Not the aqueous solution — the water would out-shout the metal at both electrodes — the melt. And the melt, priced at the bench, is where both of the chapter's design problems live.

Lecture 2 — The Two Melt Problems (the eutectic, the water share, the inversion, the wall)

The eutectic is the toll gate. Pure magnesium chloride melts at 714 °C [convergent: the pure salt's melting point] — above the working range of the camping furnace, the kitchen oven, and every honest bench the book has priced so far. The way through is the oldest trick in the salt book: impurity lowers the melting point. Potassium chloride, molten at 773 °C on its own, mixes with magnesium chloride into a eutectic that melts near 430 °C [convergent: the MgCl₂–KCl eutectic at 32/68 mol%] — a temperature a charcoal furnace, a burner, or a good blowtorch can hold. The pairing is not a bench invention: the natural mineral carnallite, KCl·MgCl₂·6H₂O, is the very double salt the earth's evaporation beds crystallized it into, and the industrial cell has always charged the mixture the mineral suggests [convergent: carnallite as the eutectic's namesake]. The mixture's arithmetic is the first lesson the melt teaches: 32 mol% magnesium chloride is 37.54% by mass [computed, Engine 55, row C04] — a mole is not a gram, and the bench that weighs its charge by the mole-fraction figure has mis-dosed the pot by nearly a sixth. The carnallite composition itself prices at 277.852 [computed, row C02], 34.27% of it the magnesium chloride [computed, row C03].

The water share is the toll. The salt the household can actually buy — the ice-melt, the bath-grade — is the hexahydrate, MgCl₂·6H₂O, 203.301 [computed, row C01]. The figure that decides whether the charge is safe is not on the bag: 53.17% of that hexahydrate's mass is water [computed, row C05] — more than half the bag leaves the crucible as steam on the way to the melt. This is the datum behind the binding line. The corpus's own performer dried his hexahydrate in the oven first and said so plainly, and the register prices his statement as the class of honesty it was: the bake was partial, the salt was still hygroscopic and water-bearing when it went in, and the melt spat and seethed as the tail of the water share came off inside the pot [single-source: N160, transcript level; the register's own pretend-dry flag]. A fully dried charge means hours at temperature the bench cannot skip, because water into a melt is a steam explosion — a droplet flashing to seventeen hundred times its volume inside a liquid with no way to vent [convergent: the general steam-expansion datum, recognition-class]. The industry's answer is to start from the anhydrous salt or from the mineral route; the bench's answer, honestly, is caution the register has never once seen skipped.

Problem one: where does the product sit? Every cell in the series before this one had the same expected geometry — the metal floats on the melt, you skim it, the density does the separation for you. Sodium floats on its salt; lithium's chapter priced the same expectation. Magnesium broke the pattern, and the corpus's performer said the chapter's title datum in his own surprised words: "I really wasn't expecting the magnesium metal to be more dense than the molten salt. Normally, when we make these metals, they float." [single-source: N160, transcript level, frozen got-string] The tabulated densities say his surprise was honest at the melt's own temperature: solid magnesium is 1.738 g/cm³ and the liquid metal at its melting point is 1.584 g/cm³ [convergent], while the chloride eutectic at cell temperature runs in the same corridor — the margin between metal and melt is thin, and at bench temperature the dendrites can end up staying where they grew instead of rising to a skimmable pool. The design consequence is the catch-tray: a cooled tray or the crucible floor doubling as the cathode, so the metal that does not float still collects somewhere deliberate [single-source: N160, the redesign stated and not performed]. Geometry, not chemistry, decides where the harvest is.

Problem two: does the product survive? Calcium's melt answers no. Calcium metal dissolves in molten calcium chloride — appreciably, immediately, and as fast as the cell makes it at bench scale; the corpus's calcium performer said it in the words the register froze: "calcium metal will pretty much dissolve into the melt just as fast as you generate it." [single-source: N172, transcript level, frozen got-string] The wall is the metal's own solubility in its mother liquor — the same sodium-in-its-salt wall the campaign has met before, but at a height the home bench cannot out-wait [convergent: the Ca-in-CaCl₂ solubility literature]. The routes around the wall are all kinetic: beat the dissolution by making the metal faster than it re-dissolves. The bench's version is extreme current density — a very small exposed cathode carrying the whole cell current, ten amperes on half a centimeter of steel wire, one hour, harvest immediately [single-source: N172, its own figures]. The industry's version, carried into this chapter by the comment thread's German metallurgy textbook, is a water-cooled iron cathode drawn slowly upward out of the melt, so the calcium freezes onto the cathode inside a protective crust of frozen salt before the melt can take it back, and the metal leaves the cell never having met the wall [comment-level: the Pawlek Metallhüttenkunde citation, @ARRandomTroll, stated as diagnosis]. The plan B, when even that is not enough, is chemical capture: a copper-calcium eutectic that pools molten at 600 °C below the salt, dissolves the calcium as it forms, and gives it back later by vacuum distillation — the same distillation the bones route uses to take calcium out of mercury (Lecture 4). [comment-level and cross-referenced; the Cu-Ca eutectic's numbers are recognition-class here]

And the counter-face, priced now for Ch 41's benefit: potassium on molten KOH does not sink, does not dissolve quietly, and does not cooperate — it floats, and it "goes pop" [single-source: N161, transcript level]. The potassium run's honest yield was "not a single crumb" — the register's cleanest honest zero since lithium's chapter — and the KOH-solubility wall that ate it is the same wall calcium's melt raises, at a height no home bench has beaten to date. The pair's design space fits in one figure of speech: density decides where the product sits, solubility decides whether it survives, and the campaign's next metal (Ch 41, potassium) faces the worst of both. The demand book at this chapter's close points forward at it deliberately.

Lecture 3 — The Magnesium Run (N160, at its own figures)

The cell is the campaign's most assembled instrument yet, and the register priced every part of it. The crucible is the anode. A graphite crucible wired positive — the same arrangement the performer says "worked so well when we were making calcium" — because graphite survives the melt's chemistry where steel would become a product (the series rule, priced at the lithium bench first and holding across every melt since). The cathode is a steel wire, dipped into the melt from above. The charge is the eutectic: magnesium chloride with potassium chloride as the inert partner, dried as well as the kitchen oven manages, melted to a quiet pool. The supply: 8 amperes at 4 volts — battery-charger territory, the same class of current the aluminium chapter ran. [single-source: N160, every figure in this lecture]

First metal at five minutes. The wire comes up wearing dendrites — the frost-tree crystal habit the melt grows when the metal deposits faster than it can pool. The Faradaic ceiling on those first five minutes is 0.3023 g [computed, Engine 55, row C06: 8 A × 300 s = 2400 C, two electrons per magnesium, at 96,485 C/mol] — and the run's whole eventual product, 60 mg, would ask just 476.37 C of that same current, 59.5 seconds of it, priced perfect [computed, row C07]. The chapter prices that ladder twice, because it is the honest frame for every yield figure to come: the ask is a minute of the bench's own current; the distance between the ask and the harvest is where the design problems take their fee.

And then the inversion, in the performer's own surprised words — the chapter's title datum, at transcript level: the magnesium did not float. Dendritic metal stayed at the wire, and the observation that follows is the one this chapter keeps: the densities at melt temperature are close, the metal can lose the race upward, and the cell that expects to skim will harvest nothing. The performer's own stated redesign is the catch-tray — crucible floor as the cathode, cool it, let the metal collect where gravity actually puts it — stated, and not performed. The register prices this honestly: the redesign is an engineering intention, not a result; the run that produced the datum is the run that stops at the datum. [single-source: N160]

The disassembly is its own lesson. The run cooled and the cell waited — the register prices it at the performer's own twenty-four hours — because a frozen salt block is opened with patience or with a hammer that cracks the crucible. The separation is water: the salts dissolve, and the magnesium beads survive the bath — metal against water, the one reagent-purity test the pair's chemistry allows, since magnesium does not react with cold water at any honest rate. The beads come off the dissolved-salt liquor as the harvest: 60 milligrams. The performer's own verdict, printed at transcript level: about five percent of what he had hoped for, "a lot of effort" for what he got, and — the sentence that closes his episode and this lecture — it was "not worth it." The register keeps both halves of that verdict: the was (metal, real, weighed, his — the campaign's first) and the worth (a bench verdict, priced by its own maker, and the P21 anchor this chapter honors). [single-source: N160, the yield and the verdict at transcript level]

The burn test, because a fire-starter metal earns one: the blowtorch takes a bead to its ignition — the blinding white flare of the fire-starter block, the same chemistry Chapter 19 pinned recognition-only on its own shelf, now run on product the bench itself made. The arc closes across the whole campaign in the performer's own five-metal roll call — aluminium, sodium, lithium, calcium, and now magnesium — and the roll call is the campaign's running ledger: five metals, two of them this chapter's pair, all of them made at benches the register can name, none of them yet at a price the bench would pay twice. Lecture 4 — The Calcium Stations (four routes, four walls)

Lab 40.1 — The Melt and the Faradaic Ladder (Outputs Verbatim; a fresh zero-shared-code build)

The chapter's computational lab runs the way every campaign chapter has run since Chapter 37: a fresh engine, written this session, sharing zero code with the drafting engines of any prior chapter (Engine 53's ledger-fold compositor, Engine 54's review harness — none of their code survives into this build, and the constant tables are re-transcribed independently from the chapter's own printed pins).
