Vol II · Chapter 19 — Metals and Their Compounds: The Displacement Ladder, Eight Metals, One Bench

Lecture 0 — The scrap drawer the household already owns: eight metals read before anything is opened

Chapter 14 built the alkali shelf and the carbonate hub; Chapter 15 climbed the mineral acids; Chapter 17 walked sulfur's ladder; Chapter 18 read the one family the E° meter orders by position. This chapter walks the other half of the periodic table the household actually touches — the metals — and it opens with the observation that makes the metals a chapter rather than a materials list: the household does not buy metals as chemicals; it owns them as objects. The drawer holds a copper wire, a box of nails, a roll of foil, a spool of solder, a fistful of fishing sinkers, a strip of flashing, a tarnished spoon. Every one of these is a reagent the world sold as hardware — and the discipline this chapter teaches is reading the object as the meter reads it: which metal will push which, what each push costs, which pushes the household already runs without knowing (the battery, the tarnish, the rust, the water heater), and which pushes must stay demonstrations forever.

The thesis, and it is the volume's hinge stated a fifth time: the metals are the E° ladder sold as objects — the displacement series is the meter's own arithmetic, run in one direction only, priced per gram before it is scheduled — and the discipline is the same three-part rule the volume has kept since Chapter 14: let the trades take the dangerous steps for you (the smelter, the refinery, the plating line), bench only the pushes whose safety architecture exists (the displacement court in a jar, the tarnish strip in a boiling pot, the demonstration-scale thermite behind a binding line), and never once generate the faces the book refuses to make (the bulk lead bench, the alkali melt, the confined charge).

Sit at the kitchen table with the notebook and pour nothing. The pencil-and-paper census of the scrap drawer, before any apparatus:

  • The copper account — a length of scrap wire or a bit of bus bar (the pure metal, the root-killer bottle's own destination), and the hardware store's root killer bottle beside it: copper sulfate pentahydrate, the blue bank that Chapter 9 precipitated and Chapter 11 displacement-plated onto the nail. Copper is the one metal of the census the household owns in both states — object and salt — and that is why this chapter's round trip (Lecture 3) is a copper round trip.
  • The iron account — the nail box, the steel wool, the cast pan: iron as the structural metal, the most-owned and least-pure face of the census. Beside it the gardening shelf's iron sulfate (FeSO₄·7H₂O, the 278.006 g/mol salt the rose garden drinks), and the pigment shelf's red (Fe₂O₃, rust read as a color, 69.943% iron by mass) and black (Fe₃O₄, the lodestone's own).
  • The zinc account — the galvanized bucket, the roof flashing, the carbon-zinc battery's own can: zinc as the coat that dies so the iron underneath does not (Chapter 11's 0.316 V pin — zinc anodic to iron, the sacrifice priced), and the medicine shelf's zinc oxide ointment tube (ZnO, the white that Lecture 4 benches at 81.379 g/mol, 80.34% zinc by mass).
  • The aluminum account — the foil roll, the beverage can, the window track's white dust: aluminum as the metal that never shows its eagerness, because its own oxide skin answers every question the meter asks of it (Lecture 4's passivation paragraph). The most abundant metal in the drawer and the one the acid bench can barely touch — until the lye does (the amphoteric face, Chapter 14's own E15/E16).
  • The magnesium account — the camp store's fire starter: a ribbon or a block of shaving-tool magnesium, the most eager metal the household sells by the gram, priced at the meter at −2.372 V and benched exactly twice (the flame that must be respected, and the sacrificial anode that dies in a water heater so the steel tank does not).
  • The tin account — the food can's bright lining (a whisper of tin on steel), the plumbing shelf's spool of solder (60/40 or the modern lead-free SAC alloy), the antique pewter bowl on the shelf: tin as the soft trade metal, the one whose two common alloys — soft solder and pewter — are the census's own two-age story (antique Sn+Pb, modern Sn+Sb+Cu lead-free [convergent]).
  • The lead account — the fishing-sinker box, the wheel-weight jar, the old roof flashing: lead as the fence chapter's own metal, recognized here, never benched in bulk — Chapter 13 drew the fence and this chapter honors it at every mention.
  • The silver account — the heirloom spoon, the mirror's own back, the photography trade's heritage: silver as the quiet noble metal whose only household chemistry is the one the air runs for free — the tarnish (Ag₂S) — and whose one bench route back (Lecture 5's boiling strip) is the gentlest court this chapter prices.

That is eight metals in one drawer, and the census's quiet observation is the thesis seen as hardware: not one of these objects was sold as a reagent, and yet every one of them is the E° table's own merchandise. The chapter's five questions, in bench order: which metal pushes which (the displacement ladder read as one network, the meter's 14 rows), what does each push cost per gram (the per-gram and per-mol-e⁻ ledgers, Chapter 11's own arithmetic extended to tin and lead), which pushes does the household already run without knowing it (the dry cell's paste, the rust, the tarnish, the water heater's sacrificial anode — the keepers' network of Lecture 5), what are the bench's honest doors (the copper round trip, the zincate and Faraday accounts, the soft bench's acid faces and the strip), and which doors are demonstrations forever (the thermite class — priced, scheduled once at demonstration scale behind a binding line, and never scaled)?

One boundary before any apparatus is unpacked, and it is the book's binding line for this entire chapter, inherited from Chapters 13, 14, and 18 and stated here at its strictest, because this chapter holds the hottest reaction the volume will ever print. The thermite class — E19's iron oxide and E18's copper face alike — is scheduled exactly twice, at demonstration scale, outdoors, on sand, at the printed demonstration charges only (the 26.8 g iron charge of B4 and the 12.3 g gold shower) and never larger, never confined, never dampened, never scaled, ignited only from a distance through the magnesium-ribbon door (the >3000 °F ignition class [convergent]; the charge once lit is self-oxidizing and cannot be extinguished with water). Lead is the Chapter 13 fence — recognized in every census, priced at the meter, benched in exactly zero procedures; its salts' own burying habits (Lecture 2's Ksp lesson) are the reason the fence never bothers this bench anyway. The alkali metals are recognition-only (N100's lithium dump-battery face and N105's cesium, the register's own mineral-oil discipline read as a closed door), as are chromium and cobalt (N79's and N98's arc-furnace faces — refused at this book's element table, priced only through the E° pins Chapter 11 already established). The manganese dioxide of the dry cell is Chapter 18's recognition flag carried forward — the battery's paste is read in this chapter as a mass account, never as a reagent to be mined out of a flashlight. And the toothpaste's stannous fluoride face stays prose — Chapter 18 refused fluorine at the element table, and this chapter's battery refused it again at its own gate (the refusal is printed in Lab 19.1's gate log; no number for it ever reached a page).

Lecture 1 — The metals census: labels written by eight trades that never say "displacement"

Every metal in the drawer arrived through a trade, and every trade writes its labels in its own currency. The translation layer is the chapter's first work, and it runs on the same composition engine as always (Lab 19.1, executed in-drafting below).

The garden trade: copper sulfate as root killer. The bottle that kills roots in the sewer line is the bench's biggest copper bank — CuSO₄·5H₂O at 249.677 g/mol, 25.451% copper by mass (Chapter 2's own 25.45% label, reproduced at two decimals). The 100.35 g bottle of the corpus's founding ore run (N59) holds 0.401919 mol of copper — the exact account Chapter 1 re-derived and Chapter 9 precipitated, and the exact account this chapter's round trip (Lecture 3, ledger B1) walks from salt to green mineral to black oxide to metal and back to salt, closing to the gram. The trade's label never says "copper bank"; the bench reads it as one.

The bath trade: Epsom salt as the magnesium sulfate account. The 454 g bag is MgSO₄·7H₂O at 246.466 g/mol — Chapter 15's own pin, whose 51.17% water-of-crystallization label is the two-decimal display of the computed 51.165%. Magnesium's household metal face is elsewhere (the fire starter), but its salt face is this bag, and the meter prices the salt's metal at −2.372 V: the most eager electron donor of the census, held politely in a sulfate.

The hardware trade: galvanized steel, the two-metal object. The bucket is one object with two metals in electrical contact — and Chapter 11 already priced the pair: zinc anodic to iron by 0.316 V, the coat that corrodes first by design. Scratch a galvanized bucket and the scratch does not rust until the coat around it has died; that is not a defect, it is the trade running a sacrificial anode in miniature, the same architecture the water heater runs with a magnesium rod (the plumbing trade's own galvanic engineering [convergent], Chapter 11's and this chapter's meter behind it).

The camp trade: the fire starter as a meter reading. The magnesium block sells at the counter of a store that never says "−2.372 V"; the block's shaving-tool steel sells the spark that lights the shaving. The ribbon's white ash is MgO (40.304 g/mol); the burning itself is the one metal-flame of the census — it cannot be looked at directly and it cannot be put out with water (the safety table's own row) — and the meter's explanation is the same as the ignition's: the metal is that eager to give its electrons, to oxygen first and to nearly everything second.

The plumbing trade: solder, pewter, and the two-age tin story. The spool's 60/40 label is mass percent: 100 g of it is Sn 0.505433 mol + Pb 0.193050 mol (Lab 19.1 Run 5), two metals whose E° rows sit 12 mV apart — a tie so close the alloy behaves as one metal in the pot (the 183–190 °C melt class [convergent]). The modern spool says lead-free and means SAC — tin, silver, copper (the electronics trade's own alloy family [convergent]) — and the antique pewter on the mantel is the third age: Sn+Pb again, where the modern artisan substitutes antimony and copper (Sn+Sb+Cu [convergent]; antimony named for recognition — the element table refuses it, and no composition of it is printed). Tin's can lining is the purest face the household meets (the whisper that keeps steel from tasting of itself), and the corpus's own tin faces — N103's finely divided tin, N119's solder-tin recovery, N121's fuming SnCl₄ (the recognition-only liquid, 260.522 g/mol at the table but benched nowhere) — are all read at register level in Lecture 5.

The tackle trade: lead as the fenced metal. The sinker box is the census's own hazard lesson — a dense, dull, soft metal whose every bench use this book has already fenced in Chapter 13. The trade's oxide story is the battery trade's story (litharge, α-PbO the red and β-PbO the yellow, the storage battery's own plate faces [convergent], PbO₂ the charged plate at 239.198 g/mol — all recognition-only here), and the fence's quiet irony is Lecture 2's to tell: lead's salts bury lead's own chemistry.

The photography trade's heritage: silver nitrate, the most expensive gram of the census. The AgNO₃ bottle (169.872 g/mol, Chapter 18's own pin — the halide test's reagent) is the one metal salt the shelf sells at precious-metal prices, and Chapter 32 inherits the whole photographic face. This chapter benches two of silver's courts at kitchen scale: the air's own tarnish court (the slowest reaction the household runs, Lecture 5) and the gentle displacement court that plates copper with a silver film (E5, the penny's silver coat — 1.00 g of AgNO₃ demands 0.1870 g of copper and pays 0.6350 g of silver).

The battery trade, re-read: the Leclanché paste as a mass account. The flashlight cell is the household's own displacement engine, and Chapter 18 flagged its depolarizer's origin (MnO₂ — recognition-only as a reagent, never mined from a cell). This chapter reads the paste as bookkeeping (E22, the balance ledger's own entry): per mole of zinc consumed, the cell demands 2 MnO₂ — 173.872 g, the flashlight's own heavy account — and the meter row behind it is Zn|Cu's own family: the zinc can is the anode the trade built for the drawer, the carbon-manganese cathode is the court it built to receive the electrons.

And the two supporting shelves the volume already built: muriatic acid and the lye (Chapters 15 and 14's own faces — the acid that runs the metals' H⁺ court, the base that runs aluminum's and zinc's amphoteric face), and the drugstore peroxide (Chapter 16's 0.891 M pin — the oxidant that makes the copper round trip close without ever generating a gas the book refuses). The census's closing count: eight metals, six trades, three supporting shelves, one drawer — and a chapter that reads them all through the meter before the bench opens.

Lecture 2 — The meter on the metals: the ladder as one network, and the courts that bury themselves

Chapter 11 built the E° meter and read four couples; Chapter 12 priced the electron; this chapter reads the whole metals bench through it at once, because the metals' one organizing fact is the meter's reading: the displacement series is the E° ladder walked downhill. A metal will push out of solution any metal whose couple sits above its own — zinc pushes copper (E1a), iron pushes copper (E1b), copper pushes silver (E5) — and never the reverse, and the meter's subtraction prices every push before the bench schedules it.

Three readings before the bench opens. First, the ladder is one-way, and the one-way sign is the chapter's most practical number. Copper sits below hydrogen: +66.0 kJ uphill for the acid alone — which is why muriatic acid never dissolves the penny in the jar on the shelf, and why the round trip of Lecture 3 needs the drugstore peroxide (E21: the peroxide takes the electron copper will not give to H⁺, and the sulfuric acid only supplies the spectator sulfate). Every metal above copper in the table will give its electron to the acid — iron eagerly (−86.3 kJ), tin lazily (−26.6 kJ), lead more lazily still (−24.3 kJ, and fenced anyway) — and the gas rule of Chapter 10's bench applies to every one of those pushes: never sealed, never a flame in the room.

Second, the price per gram is not the price per mole — the ledgers of Lab 19.2 Run 2 (B2) sort the census by what the household actually pays. Aluminum's ΔG° per mole of reaction is the biggest number on the page (−1160.1 kJ, six electrons at a time), but aluminum pays those six electrons from only 8.994 g per mol e⁻ — the cheapest electron of the census (Chapter 11's own per-mol-e⁻ pin) — and so the per-gram ledger ranks aluminum first (3.533 g of copper per gram of foil), magnesium next (2.615), then iron (1.138), then zinc (0.972). Tin (0.535) and lead (0.307) sit at the bottom: expensive per gram not because their pushes are weak (−92.6 and −90.3 kJ — stronger per two electrons than copper's own silver court!) but because their atoms are heavy. The meter prices the push; the balance sheet prices the metal; the bench reads both.

Third — the lecture's one idea, and it deserves its own paragraph — some courts bury themselves, and the Ksp lesson of Chapter 9 is why. Lead should push copper out of the root-killer bath: the meter says +0.468 V, −90.3 kJ, eager by every thermodynamic measure (E4). But lead's product is PbSO₄, and lead sulfate is one of Chapter 9's own insolubility walls — Ksp ≈ 1.6×10⁻⁸ [convergent] — and the precipitate seals the metal's own surface as it forms: the court buries itself in its own paperwork, and the displacement stalls at a skin. Tin's face (SnSO₄, deliquescent [convergent] — the salt that cannot stay dry) is the opposite habit, and the chloride court PbCl₂ (Ksp ≈ 1.7×10⁻⁵ [convergent]) sits between: partially buried, half-stalled. This is the bench's first lesson in reading a displacement critically: the E° row is the court's willingness, the Ksp row is the courtroom's door — and the household's two most vivid examples (a lead sinker sitting untouched in a copper solution; a solder joint's tin dissolving while its lead stays) are both in the drawer already.

And the two recognition rows close the lecture: Al|Co at +1.385 V (N98's spent-battery couple — the register's arc-furnace face, priced here through Chapter 11's pin and benched nowhere, cobalt refused at the table), and O₂|Fe at +0.848 V — the rust account, −163.6 kJ per mole of iron, the one displacement the household never schedules and never stops running. The nail in the jar of water is a galvanic cell with no wires; the meter has priced it all along.

Lecture 3 — The copper bench: the round trip the household already prices at 95%

Copper is the chapter's spine because it is the only metal the household owns as both object and salt, and the corpus priced the round trip before this book existed. N58's flagship ore run (The Home Scientist's copper recovery episode, the register's own honest-ledger exemplar) walked a 100.35 g bottle of root-killer copper sulfate to 47 g of found copper against a 49.4 g theoretical — the 95% verdict this book's Chapter 1 re-derived twice (the source's own numbers 95.1%; the computed unrounded 94.6%, with the 49.4 g theoretical reconstructed as intermediate rounding of the honest 49.66 g) — and N59's carbonate hub (the channel's earlier episode) is the same bottle's precipitation: 100.35 g of the pentahydrate plus 67.99 g of baking soda. This chapter's copper ledger (B1) walks the whole cycle with the engine's arithmetic, stage by stage, and closes the loop the corpus opened.

Stage 1 — the precipitation, honestly speciated (E16). The cold bicarbonate route does not make the plain carbonate the old sources assumed; it makes the basic carbonate — malachite itself, CuCO₃·Cu(OH)₂, 221.114 g/mol (Chapter 9's own gravimetric trap, printed there as Cu₂(OH)₂CO₃; whichever way the string is written, the parser reads the same green solid [the composition battery's cross-check, Lab 19.1 Run 1]). The bookkeeping: 2 CuSO₄ + 4 NaHCO₃ → malachite + 2 Na₂SO₄ + 3 CO₂ + H₂O, so the 100.35 g bottle (0.401919 mol) demands 67.527 g of baking soda — N59's own 67.99 g charge reproduced as a comfortable 0.69% excess — and pays 44.435 g of malachite, 57.087 g of byproduct sodium sulfate (the Chapter 13 pin's own salt), 35.376 g of CO₂ (19.67 L at RTP) and 7.241 g of water when the boil is counted. Here is the chapter's first [flagged sketch] seam, stated plainly: the speciation of the cold-precipitated solid is honest work, not bookkeeping trivia — the sources' plain-carbonate theoretical (49.4 g) is the trap Chapter 9 taught, the malachite account (44.435 g) is the honest one, and the cold-versus-boiled speciation (what the green solid actually is, rung by rung) is a seam the chapter flags rather than smooths over.

Stage 2 — the boil (E17). Malachite decomposes to the black calcine: CuCO₃·Cu(OH)₂ → 2 CuO + CO₂ + H₂O — the green powder goes black at the boil, and the engine's CuO account for the bottle is 31.971 g at 79.545 g/mol (79.89% copper by mass, Chapter 11's own calcine pin). The mineral shelf's two carbonates bracket the lesson: malachite green, azurite blue (2CuCO₃·Cu(OH)₂, 344.668 g/mol — the composition battery's leading-segment-multiplier case, the parser's own syntax exercise), both of them copper's basic carbonates, both of them the same court in different costume.

Stage 3 — the gold shower (E18). The black calcine plus aluminum powder is copper's own thermite: 3 CuO + 2 Al → 3 Cu + Al₂O₃, a push the meter prices at +2.004 V and ΔG° −1160.1 kJ (n = 6). At the bottle's scale the engine prices 7.230 g of aluminum for the full 31.971 g of calcine, and the copper account closes exactly: 25.540 g of metal out of 0.401919 mol — the whole copper account of the 100.35 g bottle, exact. (The demonstration is scheduled at Lecture 4's binding-line scale — 10 g of CuO and 2.261 g of Al are the printed demonstration charge, ledger B4's gold-shower face: 7.989 g of copper, ΔH° −50.60 kJ — and the chapter's safety table holds the rules.)

Stage 4 — the regeneration (E21), and the round trip closes. The copper metal goes back to the blue bank the same way the smelter never does it: Cu + H₂SO₄ + H₂O₂ → CuSO₄ + 2 H₂O. The peroxide is the door around the one-way sign of Lecture 2 (copper will not give its electron to H⁺ alone; the peroxide takes it eagerly), and the account prices honestly: 39.417 g of sulfuric acid (40.22 g of the 98% wall-acid bottle) plus 13.671 g of peroxide as 451 mL of the drugstore 3% bottle (Chapter 16's 0.891 M pin) — and back comes 100.350 g of pentahydrate: the round trip closes to the gram on the same bottle. N58's 95% verdict is the honest ceiling between the theoretical closure and the bench's hands — losses at the filter, at the boil, at the shower — and this chapter reads that verdict, as Chapter 1 did, as the register's own honest-ledger culture: the engine's exact closure is the map; the 95% is the territory.

The blue bank's other face: the dehydration ladder (E20). Root-killer blue is the pentahydrate's own color, and the trade's chemistry runs a ladder the engine prices rung by rung: 249.677 → 213.647 (the 63 °C rung, two waters gone) → 177.617 (the 109 °C rung, two more) → 159.602 g/mol (the 200 °C rung, the last water and the white anhydrous face) — 36.08% of the crystal is water, and the color goes blue to pale to white as it leaves [convergent, the trade's own rungs]. The white anhydrous salt is the same court the blue one is — the displacement engine does not care about the water — but the chapter's keepers' note (Lecture 5) is that the blue bottle re-absorbs its water and its label's mass with it; date the bottle.

The amphoteric footnote that points forward. Copper's own hydroxide (the blue gel of E15, 97.560 g/mol) does not dissolve in the lye the way zinc's and aluminum's do — copper is not one of the census's amphoteric faces in dilute sodium hydroxide — but it does dissolve in ammonia with a color deep enough to name (the deep-blue tetraammine face), and that is a door this book opens in Chapter 24's complex-ion chapter and hands forward from here as a flag: the copper bench's blue gel is Chapter 24's feedstock; the displacement ladder of this chapter is only half of copper's chemistry.

Lecture 4 — The reactive bench: zinc, aluminum, magnesium — amphoterism, passivation, and the demonstration that stays a demonstration

The three most active metals of the census share a bench because they share the chapter's most instructive irony: the meter's most eager metals are the drawer's least reactive-looking objects. Zinc sits quietly in water; aluminum sits quietly in almost everything; magnesium sits quietly until something lights it. The lecture's work is reading each metal's hiding habit as chemistry.

Zinc: the honest workhorse, and the register's own route (N76). Zinc's faces are all bench-honest: the acid face (E7: Zn + 2 HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂, the Chapter 10 gas generator this book benched in its gas chapter), the displacement court (E1a: the Daniell's own anode), and the amphoteric face that Chapter 14 built and pinned — the zincate bath, E12 = Chapter 14's own E16 (Zn + 2 NaOH + 2 H₂O → Na₂Zn(OH)₄ + H₂, total mass 181.404, the cross-pin audit's own row). The corpus's zinc episode (N76, The Canadian Chemist's zinc from zinc oxide) runs the zincate route end to end — the oxide dissolves in the lye, the bath electrolyzes at the cell (the register's own datums: the 4 V class, the two-day run, the hydrogen current control, and the register's own hazard note that the metal comes down pyrophoric if the deposit is dried carelessly) — and ledger B3 prices the whole account as bookkeeping: 20.00 g of the ointment shelf's ZnO is 0.245764 mol, worth 70.67 g of the sulfate heptahydrate on the crystallization face, and the Faraday account prices the metal's own recovery — 2951.5 C per gram, 0.8199 Ah/g, a 5.00 g account at 4.10 Ah, a 48-hour bench at 85 mA — with the electrolysis literature's own current-efficiency class (~90% [flagged sketch]) named as the honest gap between the Faraday map and the bench's territory.

Aluminum: the metal that hides behind its own oxide skin. The foil should, by the meter, be the most violent object in the drawer (−1.662 V, six electrons, the Al|Cu court priced at −1160.1 kJ). It is instead the tamest — because aluminum's product is its own armor: Al₂O₃ (101.961 g/mol), a skin that reforms in air in the time it takes to notice the scratch. The acid face barely breathes through the skin (the mercury-class activation routes of the old literature are recognition-only chemistry this book does not print); the lye face dissolves the skin and then the metal (E11: 2 Al + 2 NaOH + 6 H₂O → 2 NaAl(OH)₄ + 3 H₂ — the drain-cleaner class reaction, and Chapter 14's safety row holds the rule: never sealed, never with the drain's own architecture). The oxide itself dissolves cleanly in lye (E13), and the white oxide pigment shelf (the polishing-tray white) is the same Al(OH)₃/Al₂O₃ pair — the antacid shelf's own Al(OH)₃ at 78.003 g/mol.
