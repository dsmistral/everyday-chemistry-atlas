Vol I · Chapter 10 — Gases: Generation, Collection, Recognition; the Gas Laws

Every chapter so far has treated gases as a rumor — Chapter 4 priced the CO₂ of a descale, Chapter 6 distilled on a cushion of vapor, Chapter 8 released 4.08 liters of CO₂ at a fizz and never asked where a liter of gas comes from, Chapter 9 let a gas escape the ledger entirely ("the cloud took 94.8% of the magnesium" — and the other product, the gas, flew away unweighed). This chapter stops the escape. A gas is the one reagent the home lab makes better than it buys: no bottle, no label, no grade — one reaction away, in unlimited quantity, and it fills its own container for you. The premise's ruined-world reading is strongest here: compressed-gas cylinders are the first thing a supply chain loses, but bread dough rises, drains hiss, pond weed bubbles, and a sealed soda bottle still holds three atmospheres of dissolved gas — the household is already a gas factory; the lab's job is to catch, name, and price what it makes. The corpus supplies the practitioners: the dry-ice store-and-recover episode (N104), the chlorine generator whose explicit do-not-attempt disclaimer is itself a teaching datum (N77), the electrolysis bath whose hydrogen current is priced as a competing loss (N76), the lithium episode whose water-drop cascade is a hydrogen lecture in one gesture (N100), the retort videos whose off-gas flares are a flammable-gas handling lesson performed at bonfire scale (N91/N93), the activated-carbon episode where a gas meets a surface and gets caught by adsorption instead of chemistry (N96), and the carbonate hub's own foam-and-hiss eruption (N59) — the Chapter 9 flagship re-read as a gas-generation event. The chapter's four questions, asked in the order the bench needs them: how do you make a gas at home, how do you catch it, how do you know which one it is, and what law governs the space it takes up?

Lecture 0 — The house is already a gas factory

Start the census before the theory, because the inventory is astonishing. In one week an ordinary house generates: carbon dioxide from the bread starter, the rising dough, the beer bottle, the drain cleaner's fizz, the aquarium filter, the compost bucket, and every human breath (about 0.5 L of pure CO₂ per hour at rest, exhaled into a room that holds it at a mere 0.04%); water vapor from every kettle, shower, and simmering pot — Chapter 6's whole subject, now re-read as the house's most plentiful gas; hydrogen from the battery being charged (the garage charger's fizz is electrolysis — the N76 face at household scale, the same current that plates zinc also liberates hydrogen, which is why the charger's manual says ventilated area only); oxygen from the aquarium's bubbler and the houseplant's daytime ledger; methane from the septic system and the swampy corner of the yard; and, when things go wrong, carbon monoxide from the badly drafted heater — Chapter 7's silent datum, the one gas the house makes that can kill you without a smell.

The census teaches the chapter's first lesson before any equation: gases are made by reactions, not bought by the gram — and the reactions are already running. The lab's job is to run them in a jar, on purpose, at a known scale. The second lesson follows immediately: you cannot pour a gas the way you pour a liquid, so the whole skill of gas work is plumbing — the gas leaves its generator whether you are ready or not, and an uncaught gas is simply lost reagent. Chapter 5's separation train was about catching what refuses to dissolve; this chapter's train is about catching what refuses to stay put. The third lesson is the payoff: a gas carries its own ruler. A mole of any gas at room temperature and one atmosphere takes up the same volume — the 24.5-liter room of Chapter 8's pin — so a gas can be measured with a graduated cylinder instead of a balance. That is the instrumentless theme of the whole Atlas at its purest: the volume is the mass, the barometer is the scale.

Lecture 1 — The gas laws, rebuilt from the kitchen

The gas laws are usually taught as four separate equations. At the bench they are one sentence: the space a gas takes up is set by three knobs — how hard you squeeze it (P), how hot it is (T), and how much of it there is (n) — and every gas, no matter its chemistry, obeys the same knob-settings. Turn the sentence into arithmetic and you have the ideal gas law:

P V = n R T        R = 0.082057 L·atm/(mol·K)

R is the conversion rate between the mole-currency and the liter-atmosphere currency, and its value is the same for every gas — that is Avogadro's discovery priced as a constant. The three classic laws are the same equation with two knobs frozen:

Boyle (T, n fixed): squeezing works — the pond pitcher. Push an inverted 2.00-L pitcher of air 3.00 m down in water and the water column above you weighs 29.4 kPa — a quarter of an atmosphere on top of the one you already carry: P₂ = 1.2905 atm, so V₂ = 2.00/1.2905 = 1.55 L. The trapped air squeezed to 77.5% of its room volume; that is why a diving bell needs a pump and why the pond bubble wobbles as it rises (it is expanding the whole way up — same law, arrow reversed).

Charles (P, n fixed): heat works — the freezer balloon. A 2.00-L party balloon at 25 °C goes into a −18 °C freezer and comes out 2.00 × 255.15/298.15 = 1.71 L, a 14.4% shrink, no gas lost. The tire-pressure cousin runs the same law at constant volume: a tire at 35 psig overnight (25 °C → 15 °C) drops to 33.3 psig — the 1.7-psi morning swing, and the mechanic's rule of thumb "a few psi per ten degrees" is just Charles with a gauge on it.

Avogadro (P, T fixed): counting works — the room. One mole of any gas at room temperature (25 °C, 1 atm) takes up

V_m = RT/P = 0.082057 × 298.15 = 24.465 L/mol  — the book's working pin 24.5 L/mol

and at STP (0 °C, 1 atm) exactly 22.414 L — the number on every reference table [convergent]. Weigh out 1.000 g of four different gases and let them expand at RTP: hydrogen takes 12.14 L, oxygen 0.765 L, carbon dioxide 0.556 L, methane 1.525 L — the same moles-per-liter exchange rate for all of them. The working pin of 24.5 is the chapter's honest rounding: full precision 24.465, the two significant figures 24.5 — and every prior chapter's gas figure (Ch 8's 4.08 L of CO₂, Ch 9's 9.85 L) was priced on the working pin. This chapter prints both, and the cross-check below shows the pin costs at most half a percent.

The pressure cooker re-pins Chapter 6 from the gas-law side: at 15 psig (1536 mmHg absolute) water boils at 121 °C — Charles and vapor pressure shaking hands. And the barometric correction the collection bench will need is already in your pocket: 1 atm = 760 mmHg = 101.325 kPa = 14.70 psi — the four gauges of the same squeeze.

What makes all of this work — why the knob-model holds for hydrogen and for carbon dioxide equally — is the kinetic picture: a gas is a crowd of molecules in free flight, pressure is their drumming on the walls, temperature is their average speed of flight (H₂ molecules at RTP average 1921 m/s — faster than a rifle bullet; O₂ 482 m/s; CO₂ 411 m/s — the speed is the temperature, the mass only sets how many trips each molecule makes). The full kinetic treatment and the model's honest seams (real gases, van der Waals corrections, the near-boiling failures) are named at the end of Lecture 5 with the [ideal model] tag the battery carries.

Lecture 2 — Making the house gases: the generation roster

A gas recipe is three decisions: the reaction (what chemistry pushes molecules into the air), the scale (a pop-bottle burp or a balloon-fill), and the price (how many grams of household reagent per liter of gas). Every equation below is gated by the atom-balance ledger in Lab 10.2, and every price is the per-gram yield from the same lab. The roster, priced at RTP (24.465 L/mol):

Carbon dioxide — the workhorse, three ways.

  1. Vinegar + baking soda (the Chapter 8/9 chain, now read as a gas generator): NaHCO₃ + CH₃COOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂. Price: 0.291 L of CO₂ per gram of soda (1.00 g → 0.011904 mol). The balloon-fill chain: a 1.50-L balloon wants n = 0.061311 mol → 5.15 g of soda + 73 mL of 5% vinegar (the 0.8376-M pin). This is N59's foam-and-hiss eruption at party scale — the Chapter 9 flagship, re-read: the gas was the eruption's engine all along, and the cloud-in-the-filter was just the solid that stayed behind.
  2. Yeast + sugar: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 C₂H₅OH + 2 CO₂ — the bread-and-brew equation, the only gas recipe the house runs daily. Price: 0.272 L of CO₂ per gram of sugar (0.011101 mol), with 0.512 g of ethanol made beside it — the thrift ledger's friendliest entry (the gas is free; the drink is the byproduct). The honest seam: real fermentation is slower and partial; the pricing stands, the kinetics belong to Chapter 24's world.
  3. Dry ice: solid CO₂ at −78.5 °C [convergent], subliming straight to the gas at 1 atm — no reaction at all, only a phase. Price: 0.556 L per gram — the densest gas-stocking form the house ever sees. N104's store-and-recover episode (the stocking in the chest freezer, the recover-and-reuse rationale) is the corpus's gas-logistics face: a gas you buy by the pound and keep like a perishable, because at room temperature it insists on becoming 550 times its own volume.
  4. The calcination footnote (N19, Chapter 8's datum): 2 NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂ — baked soda loses 36.9% of its mass as gas and steam, which is why it cannot be oven-dried to constant mass and why the kitchen's "baking soda" hardens in the box: it is quietly calcining at the top of the pile. Price: half the acid route, 0.146 L/g.

Oxygen — the relighter, two ways.

  1. Peroxide decomposition: 2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂. Pure peroxide prices at 0.360 L O₂ per gram, but the bench meets it at 3% w/w: 10 mL of the drugstore bottle holds 0.300 g → 108 mL of O₂ — the pop-bottle burp scale. The 12% hair developer runs the same chain at 4× (432 mL per 10 mL). A pinch of manganese dioxide (the dead battery's black paste, rinsed) or even baker's yeast catalyzes the release from a fizz to a rush; the catalyst is unspent — Chapter 33's network datum, re-used for free.
  2. Bleach + peroxide: NaOCl + H₂O₂ → NaCl + H₂O + O₂ — two cleaning products meeting to make the burner-friendly gas, 0.329 L per gram of NaOCl. The two never-combination rules still own the shelf: bleach never meets acid (that route makes chlorine — the never-generate list below), and peroxide never meets the drain opener; but bleach + peroxide is the oxygen handoff, safe at sink scale with the ventilation rule.

Hydrogen — the lightest, one safe way, one never.

  1. Metal + acid: Zn + 2 HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂ — galvanized nail or zinc washers into diluted muriatic. Price: 0.374 L H₂ per gram of zinc (0.015295 mol). Hydrogen is the only common gas meaningfully lighter than air (0.0824 g/L vs air's 1.184 — the lift chain: a balloon of it lifts 1.10 g per liter), which makes it collect itself: point the generator's outlet up and the gas obligingly rises into the bag. It also pops at a flame — the recognition test — and this is the chapter's scale rule: the pop test runs at match-head scale, at the mouth of the test tube, never at the generator, never over a full bag (the energetics binding line — hydrogen–oxygen mixtures are recognition-knowledge only; Chapter 28 owns the hazard side).
  2. Electrolysis (N76's face): the zinc-plating bath runs 4 volts between its electrodes and a second, unwanted chemistry competes for the current — hydrogen evolution at the cathode. The episode's H₂-current control is the corpus pricing that current-thief as a real cost; the same fizz at the garage charger is the same chemistry. Electrolysis as a generator works (it is the oxygen-maker of every aquarium bubbler's cousin), but at household current it prices poorly against the zinc route — the make-or-buy verdict Chapter 2 taught, applied to a gas.
  3. The lithium cascade (N100, knowledge-only): 2 Li + 2 H₂O → 2 LiOH + H₂ — the episode's water-drop-on-lithium gesture is this chapter's most spectacular per-gram datum: 1.76 L of hydrogen per gram of metal, nearly five times the zinc price (the reactivity-series arithmetic of Chapter 11 explaining the gap before Chapter 11 is read). It is printed as knowledge, not bench: the lithium datum stays in the register's frame — dump-battery teardown, mineral-oil storage, the water-drop done on camera at a scale the home lab does not repeat.

The never-generate list — gases the house can make and the bench never does, each for a reason the book states once and holds absolutely:

  • Chlorine (bleach + acid — Chapter 8's never-combination absolute; N77's generator episode is cited as its own warning label: the transcript's explicit do-not-attempt, the pool-puck chemistry, the drain-cleaner drying train — all of it knowledge-only, and the recognition datum is the yellow-green color and the smell you must never lean into to check).
  • Carbon monoxide (Chapter 7's gap datum: the badly-drafted flame's product; the detector is the only correct response, never a test).
  • Hydrogen sulfide (the drain's rotten-egg gas — recognized by smell at trace, lethal by nose-blindness one whiff too many; never generated, never tested).
  • The energetics line (hydrogen–oxygen mixtures and anything like them: recognition-knowledge to Chapter 28, excluded at the bench in all sessions — the binding charter, restated in its gases chapter).

The roster's prices deserve one more read before moving on: the cheapest gases at the house scale are the ones whose reagents are food — a dollar of sugar makes 270 liters of CO₂ on paper (and the yeast gives the ethanol back), a dollar of baking soda about 100 L with vinegar doing the pushing. The expensive gas is the one you cannot make safely at all, which is why the blanket-drill pricing of Exercise 10.5 ends with a make-or-buy verdict the Chapter 2 scoring engine would recognize.

Lecture 3 — Collection: catching a ghost

A liquid sits in its jar; a gas leaves. The entire craft of collection is giving the leaving gas somewhere to go, and there are exactly four somewheres at household scale, each with its own physics:

Water displacement — the inverted bottle. Fill a bottle with water, invert it in a basin of water, run the generator's outlet under the rim: every bubble rises into the bottle and the water level is the gas volume, readable on the bottle's own graduation marks — a gas measured with a kitchen tool, the instrumentless theme again. The method's price: the gas arrives wet. Water evaporates into the collected space until its vapor joins the mix at its own equilibrium pressure — 23.76 mmHg at 25 °C [convergent] — and the correction is one subtraction:

P_dry = P_barometric − P_H2O(T)        e.g. 745.0 − 23.76 = 721.2 mmHg = 0.949 atm

The worked case prices the penalty: 5.00 g of zinc makes 0.076476 mol of H₂ = 1.871 L dry at RTP, but collected over water at a 745-mmHg day it occupies 1.972 L wet — a 5.4% volume surcharge that is nothing but water vapor's share (the wet-to-dry ratio 1/0.949 = 1.054). Two honesty notes live in the method itself: gases that dissolve in water (CO₂ partly — the soda's whole trick; NH₃ greedily — 700 volumes per volume of water at room temperature, so never collect ammonia over water) come out light, and hot gas straight from a generator must cool to bath temperature before its volume means anything.

Downward delivery — the pour. Carbon dioxide is 1.52× heavier than air (1.799 vs 1.184 g/L at RTP): it pours like an invisible liquid, down a ramp, into a jar held lower, filling it from the bottom as the air leaves the top. Hold a lit candle at the jar's rim and it snuffs when the invisible surface passes it — the extinguishing is the recognition (Lecture 4). The jar of CO₂ sits on the bench as stable as a jar of water; this is the one gas whose collection needs no water, no bag — only the patience to pour something you cannot see.

Upward delivery — the rise. Hydrogen (0.0824 g/L) and ammonia (0.696 g/L) collect at the top of an inverted jar, like helium in a party balloon that collects itself. The same density arithmetic that prices the pour prices the rise: a gas lighter than air climbs, and the generator's outlet points up into the waiting bag.

The bag and the balloon — the flexible vessel. A party balloon or a food-storage bag on the generator's outlet is the household gasometer: it accepts any volume, its pressure stays at one atmosphere (that is its charm and its limit), and its fill-state is legible at a glance. N77's drying-train face teaches the professional upgrade the same episode shows and disclaims: generate → drain-cleaner sulfuric-acid drying bottle → collect — the corpus's own train, priced as knowledge (the never-latch rule below applies to every vessel in it).

The vessel rule, restated from Chapter 4 and held absolutely: every gas vessel in this book is open, vented, or elastic — never latched, never sealed, never rigid-and-closed. A gas generator builds pressure without limit; a latched vessel is a bomb with a chemistry set on top. The dry-ice stocking and the N104 freezer box vent; the balloon stretches; the displacement bottle is open at its mouth to the basin. Chapter 6's never-sealed still and Chapter 8's never-latched fizz are the same single rule, and this chapter is its home.

Lecture 4 — Recognition: the gas detective

Chapter 13 will build the wet qualitative scheme for ions; gases get their scheme now, because a gas announces itself by four honest signals, and the bench kit is a candle, a splint, a match, and a jar of limewater.

The flame answers. Three gases, three behaviors at a flame:

  • Oxygen relights a glowing splint — the ember that bursts back to flame because the air around it suddenly holds five times the oxygen concentration. (This is also oxygen's safety row: enrichment makes everything burn fiercely — the Chapter 7 charcoal asterisk, priced.)
  • Hydrogen pops at a burning splint — a sharp bark as the H₂ burns in air at the tube's mouth. The scale rule from Lecture 2 governs: match-head volume, at the mouth, never the generator, never the bag.
  • CO₂ snuffs a burning splint dead — it smothers flame better than air pours it (no fuel, and it blankets the ember). Ammonia and most other "inert-ish" gases also snuff; the flame test alone never convicts, it only narrows — recognition is always a two-test case, Chapter 13's rule learned early.

The limewater answer. Bubble the unknown into limewater — saturated calcium hydroxide, 1.73 g/L at 20 °C [convergent] — and CO₂ clouds it milk-white: Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O. The cloud is Chapter 9 living in this chapter: a precipitation with a gas as its missing partner, and the Ksp arithmetic prices why the cloud is so unconditional: limewater carries [Ca²⁺] ≈ 0.0233 M, and CaCO₃'s Ksp (3.36 × 10⁻⁹, the Chapter 4 battery's constant) means the carbonate concentration needed to cloud it is Ksp/[Ca²⁺] = 1.44 × 10⁻⁷ M — a whisper of carbonate saturates it. The honest seam is the same one Chapter 9 flagged: bubbling CO₂ into water makes carbonic acid first, and how much of that shows up as free CO₃²⁻ is a coupled Ka–Ksp question that belongs to Chapter 24; the recognition verdict needs no seam-walking, because the precipitation is observed, and the cloud's chemistry is Chapter 9's to own.

The density answer. The pour-down-ramp (CO₂, 1.52× air) and the self-collecting rise (H₂ at 0.0824 g/L — the balloon that fights gravity) are recognition by scale: the balance-vs-the-atmosphere, no reagents, the P9 instrumentless face. Even the party balloon participates: a helium balloon deflates in a day or two while an air balloon lasts a week — helium effuses through the same latex pores 2.69× faster than air's nitrogen (Graham's law: rate ∝ 1/√M; the He/N₂ ratio √(28.014/4.003) = 2.65, He/air 2.69), and hydrogen, if you had it in a balloon, would outrun oxygen's escape rate by the famous 4:1 (√(31.998/2.016) = 3.98). Effusion is recognition's quiet cousin: identity by speed of leaving.

The adsorption answer (N96). The activated-carbon episode is this chapter's capture-without-chemistry face: a gas meets a prepared surface and sticks — the iodine number's 92.5 mg/g was the Chapter 7 datum; here the same surface is the general principle that vapors can be pulled from air by nothing but surface. The retort videos' off-gas flares (N91/N93) are the opposite face — a flammable gas mixture deliberately ignited at the vent, the safe handling of a gas you cannot keep: flare it, never store it. Both faces bookend the collection lecture's lesson: catch it, vent it, or burn it at the exit — the one thing you never do is latch it.

The full scheme as a decision tree:

                 ┌─ relights the glowing splint ──────────────── OXYGEN
   the gas ──────┼─ pops at the burning splint (mouth, match-head) ─ HYDROGEN
   (candle jar) ├─ snuffs + clouds limewater ──────────────── CARBON DIOXIDE
                 ├─ snuffs + turns red litmus blue ─────────── AMMONIA
                 ├─ snuffs, no cloud, pours down-ramp ───────── CO₂ (dry check) or N₂
                 └─ snuffs, nothing else …─────────────────── a Chapter 13 handoff
    KNOWLEDGE-ONLY rows (never bench-tested): yellow-green + choking = chlorine (N77);
    odorless + a CO detector's alarm = carbon monoxide (Ch 7); rotten egg = hydrogen sulfide.

The knowledge-only rows carry the chapter's most serious discipline: the nose is not an instrument for these gases — chlorine's smell is a warning you were already too close, CO has none at all, and H₂S deadens the very receptor it rings. The detector, the label, and the never-generate list are the bench's only correct tools there.

Lecture 5 — The gas laws in the ledger: stoichiometry with a 24.5-liter room

Now the payoff lecture, where the generation prices of Lecture 2 meet the law of Lecture 1 and the whole book's gas figures close their loop.

The volume shortcut. Because a mole of gas takes 24.5 L at RTP, the stoichiometry of gases skips the molar mass entirely for volume questions: the ledger's gram-route and liter-route must agree, and the engine checks them against each other. The balloon-fill chain, both ways: 1.50 L at RTP is 1.50/24.465 = 0.061311 mol of CO₂ → × 84.006 = 5.15 g of soda (gram route); or 1.50 L / 0.291 L-per-gram = 5.15 g (liter route from the yield table). The two routes agreeing is the P12 cross-check culture applied to a gas.

The honest cost of the working pin. This chapter's cross-checks price the 24.5-vs-24.465 choice on the book's own printed figures: Chapter 8's 7.334 g of CO₂ is 0.166648 mol = 4.077 L at full precision, 4.083 L at the working pin (printed 4.08 — the pin rounds to the printed digit); Chapter 9's 17.69 g is 0.401963 mol = 9.834 L precise, 9.848 L at the pin (printed 9.847 — one more honesty note: Chapter 9's ledger carried its own unrounded 0.401919 mol, whose × 24.5 gives the printed 9.847 exactly; the 0.401963 re-derived here starts from Chapter 9's rounded 17.69 g, and the 4-mmol gap between the two is pure operand rounding — no verdict anywhere moves). The working pin costs half a percent; the book prints both numbers here so every future chapter can inherit the honest pair.

The hiss chain. The sealed soda bottle is the household's own compressed-gas cylinder: a 2.00-L bottle with a 60.0-mL headspace held at 2.50 atm absolute (the stated working assumption — a well-carbonated pop) carries n = PV/RT = 0.006131 mol of CO₂ in that headspace alone = 0.270 g, gas that wanted 0.150 L at one atmosphere — two and a half times the room it was given. Open the cap and the hiss is 0.27 g of reagent gas escaping at the speed of decompression; the same bottle latched shut and left in a hot car is Charles's law against the vessel rule — the never-latch line priced in grams per square centimeter.

The kinetic sketch, and the model's seams. The speeds of Lecture 1 (H₂ 1921 m/s, O₂ 482, CO₂ 411 at RTP) are the kinetic-molecular picture: temperature is the flight energy, pressure is the drumming, and the gas laws are its bookkeeping. The picture's honest seams, named in the battery's tag as everywhere else: the model carries [ideal model: point molecules, no attractions] — and it bends exactly where gases get cold or crowded (real-gas behavior, van der Waals' corrections, the near-liquefaction failures). Water vapor bends it most at household scale (it is never far from condensing — Chapter 6's whole subject), which is one more reason the wet-gas correction of Lecture 3 is not a formality. The chapter's verdicts all live comfortably inside the model's honest range: room temperature, near one atmosphere, far from condensation — the same regime the reference tables price.

The blanket drill — the ledger's summit. The chapter's capstone chain prices a real bench question end to end: to blanket a 40.0-L trough with CO₂ for the pour-down candle demo needs n = 40.0/24.465 = 1.635 mol — and the three routes of Lecture 2 price it: 137 g of baking soda + 1.95 L of vinegar (the acid route, at the 0.8376-M pin), 72 g of dry ice (no liquid byproduct, the N104 face), or 147 g of sugar through the yeast (0.272 L/g, with 37.7 g of ethanol as the byproduct — and the honest kinetic note that the yeast route is the slow one). Exercise 10.5 hands the reader the drill with the make-or-buy question attached, and the Chapter 2 scoring engine would recognize the shape: the winner depends on the axis you weight — speed, dryness, or thrift.

10.1 ●○○ — The pond pitcher. An empty 2.00-L pitcher, mouth down, is pushed to 3.00 m depth in a pond (water density 1000 kg/m³). (a) The pressure on the trapped air, in atm; (b) the trapped volume; (c) the percentage squeezed. Hints: Boyle; the water column ρgh; the four-gauge set of Lecture 1. Answer: (a) P₂ = 1 + ρgh/P_atm = 1 + 29,430 Pa/101,325 Pa = 1 + 0.2905 = 1.2905 atm (the column adds 29.43 kPa — a 2.9-psi squeeze); (b) V₂ = P₁V₁/P₂ = 2.00/1.2905 = 1.550 L; (c) 22.5% squeezed. Ways: the Boyle route direct; the bubble route — the same bubble rising doubles its volume by 10 m of ascent, the same law with the arrow reversed.

10.2 ●○○ — The freezer balloon. A 2.00-L balloon at 25 °C is put in a −18 °C freezer overnight. Answer: (a) V₂ = 2.00 × 255.15/298.15 = 1.712 L; (b) it returns to 2.00 L — nothing was lost, only cooled (the test that Charles is reversible: warm it back and the volume returns); (c) P₂ = 49.7 × 288.15/298.15 = 48.03 psia = 33.3 psig — the 1.67-psi morning swing.
