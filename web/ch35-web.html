Chapter 35 — Adhesives and Polymers: The Shelf That Binds

A ruined world is held together by glue. Every chair leg, every book spine, every sole of every shoe is a joint, and behind every joint is a shelf: the white bottle, the amber tube, the two syringes, the hot gun, the forgotten tin of the furniture trade. This is the chapter where the Atlas reads the adhesive shelf the way it has read every other shelf — as chemistry, weighed, priced, and entered in the ledger — and the shelf repays the reading with the book's own subject in miniature: polymers are how chemistry builds things larger than molecules, and adhesives are polymers asked to hold two worlds together. Two great divides organize everything here — how the chain is built (addition against condensation) and what the finished joint can do (thermoplastic against thermoset) — and one fence stands above the whole shelf, printed on every warning label the trade has ever written: never glue skin.

Nothing in this chapter should be read as bench-verified beyond the labels shown; the labels are the contract — and this shelf's contract carries one fence no arithmetic can replace: the joint that holds a cup holds nothing on an eyelid, and the trade says so on every tube.

Lecture 0 — The Broken Cup at Midnight

Start with the phenomenon, before any formula. A cup breaks at midnight, and nobody is buying another one — you reach instead for the shelf, and the shelf answers with a white bottle. Squeeze; the crack fills with milk; overnight the milk disappears and a glassy film remains, and the cup holds coffee again. Nothing was added to that crack except a polymer already dissolved in the bottle's own water. The lesson of the dried glue ring left in the saucer — scrape it and it peels like skin — is the lesson of the whole shelf: the glue did not stick the cup to itself; it made a third material inside the crack, and the third material sticks to both.

Why it sticks at all has only two answers, and every adhesive on the shelf pays in both:

  1. The mechanical answer: interlock. The liquid climbs into the pores of a porous material — wood, paper, leather, sponge, bread crumb — and hardens there as ten thousand tiny keyholes. This is why the woodworker clamps a joint: the glue must wick in before it can lock. The white bottle holds wood better than it holds glass, and glass better than it holds the plastic of the refuse sack.
  2. The chemical answer: polar reach. The polymer's oxygen-bearing groups reach for the electrons at the joint's surface — hydrogen bonds, dipole bonds, the same quiet attractions that hold water to salt. This is why nothing glues polyethylene refuse sacks, polypropylene tubs, or PTFE pans: those surfaces are nonpolar, electroneutral, with nothing to reach for. The trade's "plastic adhesives" are chemistry's confession that the plain polymer cannot do it; they prime the surface first, oxidizing it into something reachable [recognition-only].

And one practical confession that belongs in Lecture 0 because every label states it: the best joint uses the least glue. The polymer film is weaker than the materials it joins, so a thick glue line is a weak joint — the thin film transfers stress; the thick film absorbs it and tears [convergent]. The bottle is not asking for more; it never was.

The shelf's roster, as the Atlas will weigh it across this chapter: the waterborne (white glue, hide glue, flour paste), the reactive (cyanoacrylate, epoxy, silicone), the hot and the dissolved (hot-melt, rubber cement) — and behind all of them, the condensation plastics of history (Bakelite, urea–formaldehyde, melamine), which are adhesives' closest kin: the chemistry that bonds wood fibers into board is the same chemistry that bonded the first synthetic world together.

Lecture 1 — The Two Great Divides

Divide one: how the chain is built. A polymer is a repeat, printed thousands of times, and the printing press comes in exactly two designs:

  • Addition: the monomer simply adds to the chain's end, atom for atom, and nothing leaves. Ethyl cyanoacrylate (125.127 [pin, Ch 25]) polymerizes by addition (E4): the joint weighs what the tube weighed, to the milligram. So does the epoxy network (E6). The ledger of an addition cure has no byproduct column — its zeros are its signature.
  • Condensation: every bond is paid for with a small leaving molecule, usually water. Glucose (180.156 [pin, Ch 21]) condenses into the starch repeat (162.141 [pin, Ch 25]) by surrendering one H₂O per repeat (E1: 1,621,410 + 180,150 = 1,801,560 myu, exact). Urea (60.056 [pin, Ch 20]) plus formaldehyde (30.026 [computed]) pays the same toll (E7). Phenol (94.113 [computed]) pays it too (E8). The condensation ledger's byproduct column is where the water went — and the water came from the bond itself, which is why a condensation joint can shrink as it cures.

Divide two: what the finished joint can do. A thermoplastic is a solid that can be re-melted — the hot-glue gun's stick, the white glue's soften-in-heat film, the rubber cement's film that toluene-adjacent solvents (Ch 21's shelf) lift again. A thermoset is a network: once the chains are cross-linked, melting would have to break the network, and the network refuses — epoxy, cured silicone, Bakelite. The divide is the book's own fence between the reversible shelf and the permanent one, and it is why the two-part epoxy tub carries a mixing discipline that the white bottle never asks for: the network forms once, and the mix ratio is the chemistry.

The historical beat belongs here, and the Golden Book's canon holds it: Bakelite, 1907 — Leo Baekeland's phenol–formaldehyde thermoset, the first fully synthetic plastic [convergent]. Its first step is E8: phenol (94.113) plus formaldehyde (30.026) yields methylol phenol (124.139 [computed]) — and this chapter prints that mass knowing what Chapter 34 printed with it: salicyl alcohol, the willow's own phenol-glycoside half. One molecule apart, again — the isomer seam (ISO 2/2, Lab 35.1). Urea–formaldehyde glues follow from 1915 [convergent], melamine (126.123 [computed]) joins the family, and the plywood of the twentieth century is literally condensation chemistry holding wood together — the same E7 ledger as this chapter's adhesive census, printed at industrial scale.

Lecture 2 — The Water-Borne Shelf

The white bottle. "PVA glue," says the label — and the label is imprecise twice: the polymer is polyvinyl acetate (PVAc), not polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), and the bottle is not a solution but a dispersion — polymer beads suspended in water, milk-white, ~35 % solids in the model bottle (B1). The repeat is vinyl acetate, C₄H₆O₂ = 86.090 g/mol on the held-22 basis [computed] (a named basis seam: the databases print 86.089 [web-convergent]; the Atlas's table keeps its own arithmetic — the same class of seam as Ch 6's HCl). Its history is a two-chapter duet: acetic acid (60.052 [pin, Ch 4], the vinegar chapter's own) plus acetylene (26.038 [computed], the welder's gas) built the monomer for half a century (E2: 600,520 + 260,380 = 860,900 myu) [convergent]. The cure is physical: the water leaves, the beads fuse, the film locks. Nothing reacts — which is why cleanup is water, before the film dries.

The alcohol polymer that cannot be made from its own monomer. Vinyl alcohol, C₂H₄O, refuses to exist as a stable monomer — it flips to acetaldehyde the instant it forms [convergent]. So polyvinyl alcohol is made backwards: the acetate polymer is saponified with lye (39.997 [pin, Ch 24]), the acetate groups leaving as sodium acetate (82.034 [pin, Ch 24]; its trihydrate 136.079 rides the same page) — E3: 860,900 + 399,970 = 440,530 + 820,340 myu, exact. The polymer the shelf could not print directly, it prints by hydrolysis; Exercise 35.3 prices the whole corridor.

The hide glue. The oldest protein adhesive on the shelf is collagen, hydrolyzed to gelatin in the pot [recognition-only]; the trade grades it by gram strength, a number the canister carries and this chapter reads without re-deriving [recognition-only]. Its cure is the waterborne cure again: hot water leaves, the protein network grips — and the luthier's bench loves it precisely because the joint is reversible with heat and steam [convergent]. B3 keeps one honest division for it: a 25.0 g-in-125.0 g dip [model].

The flour paste. The pantry's own adhesive: starch (162.141 [pin, Ch 25] — Ch 13's fingerprint face, Ch 31's flag honored) cooked in water into a paste that has stuck paper to paper longer than paper has had printing on it; the bookbinder's wheat-starch paste is the same chemistry at archival grade [convergent]. The seam is the chapter's own doorway: each repeat is one glucose (180.156) minus one water (18.015) — the glue and the bread, one molecule apart (E1; P3, Lab 35.1) — exactly as Chapter 31 promised this chapter it would find.

Lecture 3 — The Reactive Shelf

The instant glue. Ethyl cyanoacrylate (125.127 [pin, Ch 25]) is the shelf's one-second chemist: moisture on the surface initiates an anionic chain reaction that walks the monomer into a polymer across the joint — the clock is Chapter 25's own property, cited here, not re-derived; this chapter owns the inventory. The cure is addition (E4): nothing leaves, and B5 prints the tube's whole ledger — 3.00 g = 23.9756 mmol of monomer, 0.0000 g of byproduct. The history: rediscovered by Coover in 1951, first sold 1958 [convergent]. Its methyl sibling (111.100 [computed] — exactly 1,111,000 myu, the battery's tidiest print) is the same door. Its debond is Chapter 21's acetone (58.080 [pin, Ch 21]), 20–30 minutes of patience [convergent] — and its legend, the skin bond, is the fence (Safety row 2 and row 7: never peel, never near eyes).

The two-part epoxy. The resin is DGEBA, the standard liquid epoxy: C₂₁H₂₄O₄ = 340.419 g/mol [computed], the bisphenol A (228.291 [computed]) chemistry the trade has poured since mid-century. The reactive heart is the epoxide ring — which is oxirane itself (44.053 [computed]), and here the chapter prints its own favorite twin: the vinyl-alcohol repeat has exactly the same formula (ISO 1/2, Lab 35.1). One C₂H₄O is a strained triangle that opens at the first nucleophile's touch; the other is a calm chain that dissolves in water — one formula, two personalities, and the whole chapter's argument that structure outranks composition. The hardener is ethylenediamine, C₂H₈N₂ = 60.100 g/mol [computed] — exactly 601,000 myu — carrying four amine-H per molecule; the mix discipline is one amine-H per epoxide [convergent], and B4 prices a 10.0 g resin dose: 29.3756 mmol of resin, 58.7511 mmol of rings, 0.8827 g of hardener, exactly. The cure is addition into a network (E6): 2×3,404,190 + 601,000 = 7,409,380 myu, and nothing leaves — the thermoset is poured, not precipitated.

The silicone seal. The acetoxy cure is the most domestic reaction on the reactive shelf: the model crosslinker diacetoxydimethylsilane (176.243 [computed]) meets water and trades its acetoxy groups for silanols (dimethylsilanediol 92.169 [computed]), releasing acetic acid — 60.052, vinegar's own number, Chapter 4's own pin — which is why a fresh bathroom seal smells like a chip shop for a day. E5: 1,762,430 + 360,300 = 921,690 + 1,201,040 myu, exact. The silanols then condense into the PDMS backbone (the repeat 74.154 [computed]; the element silicon 28.085 [pin, Ch 30], the concrete chapter's own), and B6 prices the smell: 10.0 g of model crosslinker → 6.8147 g of acetic acid byproduct. The neutral-cure alternative trades the vinegar smell for oximes/alcohols [recognition-only] — the label's own vocabulary, read here, not re-derived.

The 60-family. And the shelf's strangest coincidence, printed as Probe 1: three of this chapter's working molecules stand within half a percent of each other — urea 60.056, acetic acid 60.052, ethylenediamine 60.100 — 40 myu between the first two, 480 across the whole family. A condensation monomer, a byproduct acid, and an addition hardener, all wearing the same leading number: the mass alone tells you nothing about which shelf a 60.052 belongs to — the atom map does (Ch 34's lesson, honored here as P1).

Lecture 4 — The Hot and the Dissolved

The hot gun. The hot-melt stick is an EVA copolymer — ethylene (28.054 [computed]) and vinyl acetate in the same chain — and the label never prints its vinyl-acetate percentage, so this chapter doesn't either [recognition-only]. The chemistry is the Divide: the stick is a thermoplastic, poured at temperature and gripped by cooling — the joint cures in seconds because it doesn't react at all. The gun can always melt it again; the joint can always be un-made with a hair dryer's worth of heat [convergent].

The rubber shelf. Rubber cement's film is natural rubber — isoprene (68.119 [computed]) printed a million times — dissolved, in the trade's chemistry, in solvents Chapter 21 already owns; the neoprene contact cements carry chloroprene (88.537 [computed]; a named basis seam — the databases print 88.5355 [web-convergent], the held-22 Cl arithmetic prints 88.537, the same class of seam as Ch 6's HCl and this chapter's own vinyl acetate) [convergent]. The cure is evaporation: the solvent leaves, the film grips, and the second coat welded to the first by pressure. The solvent is the hazard and the unglue both — Ch 21's flame line inherited whole (Safety row 1), and Ch 21's shelf re-opens as this shelf's tool room.

The unglue, as a shelf of its own. Every adhesive is a decision that can be revisited somewhere: water for the white bottle's squeeze-out; heat for hide glue and hot-melt; acetone for cyanoacrylate; the solvents for rubber cements; and time, the universal debonder, for the shelf's older chemistry. The exceptions are the thermosets — epoxy and cured silicone refuse every polite solvent — which is exactly what Divide two promised at the top of the chapter. Ethylene and acetylene were both printed here already, as monomer parents; the shelf's family tree keeps its gases on the same page as its joints.

Lecture 5 — Reading the Label and the Fence

The adhesive label is a lexicon, and the Atlas reads it the way it read the cleaning label (Ch 33) and the medicine label (Ch 34):

  • "PVA" / "vinyl" / "wood glue": the waterborne dispersion of Lecture 2 — water cleanup, clamp the joint, thin film.
  • "Cyanoacrylate" / "instant": the addition shelf of Lecture 3 — moisture-initiated, skin-bonding, acetone-debondable; refrigerated storage extends shelf life [recognition-only].
  • "Epoxy — mix equal parts / 1:1 / 2:1": the two-part network of Lecture 3 — the ratio is the amine-H : epoxide stoichiometry, and the mix discipline is the chemistry, not a suggestion.
  • "100 % silicone — acetoxy / neutral": Lecture 3's seal — the acetoxy trades vinegar fumes for speed; the neutral trades speed for a chemistry this shelf only names.
  • Cure windows — minutes (cyanoacrylate), hours (epoxy's green strength), days (the full network), and the label's "fully cured" is the network's own clock, printed in days per millimeter [recognition-only].

And the fence, absolute, the trade's own: never glue skin — never near eyes. Cyanoacrylate bonds skin in seconds (its eyelash warnings are the industry's most-photographed label [convergent]); epoxy amines sensitize on repeated exposure [convergent]; the cured joint is chemistry but the uncured bead is a hazard. The chapter's entire arithmetic — every myu and every closure — is cheaper than one bonded eyelid, and the shelf knows it. The disposal line closes the fence: cure small residues, then bin them; uncured chemistry never goes to the drain (Safety row 8).

Lab 35.1 — Engine 23: The Single-Scan Register Machine (design, honesty record, battery)

Design. A fresh per-chapter drafting engine, sharing zero code with all twenty-two prior engines. Engine 19 compiled formulas to arithmetic; Engine 20 split seams recursively; Engine 21 memoized the parse in an interval table; Engine 22 compiled to postfix and evaluated a stack. Engine 23 never builds a structure at all — it is a single-scan register machine: one left-to-right pass over the formula's characters, each character an event dispatched to a small set of registers (a two-character symbol buffer; a fixed-point number register holding an integer part and up to two decimal places; a frame stack for parentheses; a pending-group register awaiting its multiplier; a segment accumulator; a segment-scale register set at the hydrate dot). No tokenization pass, no parse tree, no recursion, no memo table, no compiled program — the scan is the evaluation. All arithmetic is fixed-point integer: atom counts in ten-thousandths of an atom (1 atom = 10,000 units; the 0.05-hydrate's half-digit rides as 500 units), masses in myria-units (1 myu = 1e-4 g/mol), and the only integrality gate is on the total mass — a formula whose mass is not an integer number of myu refuses as NON-EXACT, exactly as the standing battery requires (CuSO₄·0.05H₂O: 160.50275 g/mol, refused at the gate). The leading coefficient ("2 C₂H₅OH") and the hydrate dot's segment coefficients ("·7H₂O", "·0.05H₂O") are register events, not syntax layers. The atom map prints in the held-22 table's own order — the engine's stated convention, its own.

The held-22 element table was re-transcribed independently before any computation (Ch 25's own page — the held twenty plus Mn 54.938 — plus Ch 30's Si 28.085: twenty-two entries, all integral at the myria scale). P is not among them, and this shelf never asks: the adhesion roster needs only C, H, O, N, Si, Na, Cl — the tenth zero-admission chapter, confirmed by a battery whose first row refuses phosphorus at the gate.

The table's own honest seam, printed as found. The re-transcription surfaced a seam the book has carried since Ch 24: the held table's silver entry prints 107.897, while silver's standard atomic weight is 107.868 [web-convergent]. Ch 19's earlier pages used 107.868, and Ch 32's RA-1 already named the basis seam (AgCl 143.321 on the old basis, 143.350 on the held one) without naming the deviation itself. Engine 23 holds the book's table — consistency with eleven chapters of printed masses — and this chapter prices no silver composition, so the seam costs nothing here. The deviation is recorded in the project's standing errata, to fold at the next touch of an Ag-owning chapter. [recorded, not fixed this session]

Ex 35.1 (●○○). A 100 g bottle of white glue carries 35 % solids [model]. (a) How many grams of PVAc and of water ride in the bottle? (b) How many mmol of vinyl acetate repeats is the solid fraction? (c) How many mol of water? (d) How many repeats per gram of solids? Hint: B1 is the ledger; the repeat is 86.090. Worked, two ways: (a) PVAc 35.0 g + water 65.0 g = 100.0 g, the bottle's own closure. (b) 35.0 g ÷ 86.090 g/mol = 406.5513 mmol of repeats (X1, Lab 35.1). The second way: per-gram pricing — 1 g of PVAc = 11.6158 mmol of repeats (1000 ÷ 86.090), and 11.6158 × 35 = 406.5513 mmol, the same number from the rate side (P9's convergence: the bottle priced by the division and by the rate agree to the ten-thousandth). (c) 65.0 g ÷ 18.015 g/mol = 3.6081 mol. (d) 11.6158 mmol/g — the per-gram repeat rate is the number that lets any bottle size be priced without ever re-dividing (P10's route economics at pantry scale).

Ex 35.2 (●○○). A 10.0 g spoon of flour carries 75 % starch [model]. (a) How much starch? (b) How many mmol of anhydroglucose repeats is the paste's whole dry chemistry? Ex 35.4 (●●○). Four seams from one shelf. (a) The 60-family: urea 60.056, acetic acid 60.052, ethylenediamine 60.100 — show the gaps in myria-units, and say why the mass alone cannot sort the shelf.
