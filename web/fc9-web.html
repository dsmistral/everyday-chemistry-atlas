The Field Companion · FC9 — Clay, Kilns, and Earth Building

The house is built out of the ground it stands on, and the ground is a shelf of chemistry waiting for water and heat. Clay is the shelf's only plastic mineral — a handful of streambank mud that remembers the shape your fingers gave it — and heat turns that memory permanent: first it gives up its structural water and forgets (metakaolin), then it rebuilds itself into needles of mullite that porcelain is made of. Limestone is the cycle rock: burned it forgets its carbon dioxide and becomes quicklime; slaked it drinks water back with the bench's most dramatic exotherm; left in the wall it breathes the air's own CO2 and becomes limestone again — a building material that closes its own ledger. Sand and silt are the skeleton, sorted for free by nothing but standing water — the jar test is gravity doing the sieve's work overnight. Wood ash is the forest's flux: the tree spent a lifetime collecting potassium and calcium, and the stove hands them to the potter as a glaze waiting for clay and feldspar. And the kiln is not a tool but a reagent: temperature, held honestly, is the one input every door of this shelf shares. This is the earth chapter — priced the way the Atlas prices everything: the jar's physics run as an exact ledger, the lime cycle closed in kilograms, models labeled as models, the potter's family words refused at the gate before any formula parses, and the Chapter 30 rule inherited verbatim: the reader reads clay, never fires it.

Lecture 0 — The jar of mud (before any notation)

Begin with the census, because the earth shelf's whole discipline is telling its doors apart — and because the first tool is a glass jar on a windowsill, not a reagent bottle. Fill it with water, stir in a spade's depth of the ground you are standing on, and draw what you see, twice: now, and in the morning. No numbers yet; the drawing is the lecture — and it is Chapter 5's three-jar experiment returned to the earth it came from [register: Ch 5's jar lecture].

  • Clay — the plastic mineral: the fraction that stays suspended overnight, the fraction that makes the whole jar cloudy at dawn. Kaolinite is its pure face — Chapter 30's own largest new species, Al2Si2O5(OH)4, 258.157 g/mol [computed, P1 — Ch 30's pin, re-grepped].
  • Silt — the middle: down in the first minute, soft as flour between the fingers.
  • Sand — the skeleton: down in seconds, the grit that gives a wall its shoulders and a glaze its body.
  • Lime and limestone — the cycle rock: the CaCO3 100.086 shelf [PIN Ch 24/30], burned and slaked and carbonated in a loop Lecture 3 closes to the kilogram.
  • Gypsum — the set-fast sibling: CaSO4·2H2O 172.164 and its half-water CaSO4·0.5H2O 145.1415 [computed, S8/S2 — the Ch 3/Ch 30 pins], the plaster that sets by drinking water, never by breathing CO2.
  • Ash — the forest's flux: the stove's pale residue, potassium and calcium collected over a tree's lifetime — Lecture 4's door.

The sorting test is the one the Atlas has taught since Chapter 4 — what does water do to it, and what does heat do to it — and on this shelf it runs with unusual honesty, because the jar performs the separation for free: gravity is a separation engine that runs all night in silence (Ch 5's own line, inherited). The jar test prices nothing by eye: Lecture 2 turns it into Stokes' law and the battery prices it exactly [computed, B1].

And one premise stated once, load-bearing everywhere: earth building is mixing discipline. Cob and adobe are not "dirt walls"; they are graded soil — enough clay to bind, enough sand to skeleton, fiber where the recipe wants tensile help [convergent: the earthen-building canon — a clay soil with enough clay content to act as the adhesive for sand and straw; pure clay is not wanted and not needed]. The jar test is the prospecting tool because it reads the grade: the layer heights are the soil's recipe, standing on the windowsill. The structural-safety rules ride with the premise — an earthen wall is engineered ground, never a pile — and the chapter's safety table carries them [register].

Lecture 1 — Clay: the mineral that remembers

Kaolinite, and the water it holds. The pure clay mineral is a stack of alternating layers — alumina and silica sheets, hydroxyls facing each other across the gap — and the formula Chapter 30 pinned is the whole bookkeeping: Al2Si2O5(OH)4, 258.157 g/mol [computed, P1]. Four of its oxygens ride as hydroxyls, and those are the water the kiln will collect: the battery's first balance is the dehydroxylation,

Al2Si2O5(OH)4 → Al2Si2O7 + 2H2O — 258,157,000 myu = 258,157,000 myu (258.157 g), EXACT [computed, E1]

— and its proportion is the shelf's first honest number: 2H2O per gram of kaolinite = 36.03/258.157 = 0.139566 g/g [computed, PR1] — Chapter 30 printed this as its 13.96% [Ch 30's own print, honored], and the engine's six-decimal value is the full-precision restatement. The dehydroxylation window is Chapter 30's own verified band, worn here unchanged: roughly 450–700 °C across the research literature — the pure-phase sources print 500–550, and the ceramics-teaching class extends the practical kiln window to 500–800 [convergent: Ch 30's research-class band, cited as its own].

The ledger, walked at two masses. The battery prices the water's departure exactly, at 700 g and at Chapter 30's own mug scale:

700 g kaolinite → 97.6964 g water + 602.3036 g metakaolin — EXACT [computed, X1] 500 g kaolinite → 69.7831 g water — EXACT, Chapter 30's 69.78 g mug case at full precision [computed, X1; Ch 30's print honored]

What remains is metakaolin, Al2Si2O7, 222.127 g/mol [computed, P2 — Ch 30's pin] — amorphous, a network scrambled by its own water's departure, the clay's memory wiped. And here the chapter's quietest isomer-class row does its work: Chapter 30 wrote the split as Al2O3·2SiO2·2H2O = 258.157 g/mol [computed, I1 — kaolinite as its oxide sum, the same number as P1, printed as the isomer row and never as a NEW claim] — and the two ways of writing the departure are the same ledger, because Al2O3 101.961 + 2 × SiO2 60.083 = 222.127 exactly: metakaolin is the oxide sum, waiting at temperature to be rebuilt.

Plasticity, and the gate that refuses it. The word for clay's workability is a family word: slip REFUSED [computed, R7 — a family or mixture NAME, not a formula, the tannin lesson held on the earth shelf]. The engine refuses the trade's own vocabulary at its gate before any formula parses — slip, grog, saggar, terracotta, stoneware, porcelain, all six in the battery (R7–R12) — because plasticity is a colloid's property, water films and sheet edges, not a stoichiometry. The recognition is the price: clay is plastic because its particles are microscopic plates that water lets slide; silt and sand, a hundred and five hundred times coarser, cannot be plastic at any wetness [register].

Mullite, the rebuild. Fire metakaolin further and it does not melt — it reorganizes: above ~950–1100 °C the crystalline silicates form, chief among them mullite [convergent: Ch 30's research canon], the needle-crystal skeleton that gives porcelain its strength. The battery prices the rebuild exactly:

with mullite, Al6Si2O13, 426.049 g/mol [computed, N3 — the NEW value, zero-hit verified; the word mullite is Chapter 30's prose, disclosed and re-grepped] — the value never before printed in this book, the word already its neighbor's sentence. By porcelain temperatures the leftover amorphous silica begins to crystallize to cristobalite — "By 1300 °C, cristobalite begins to form from the amorphous Si-rich phases" [convergent: the ceramics literature's own line, printed as found at Ch 30, honored here]. And the whole route closes at kaolinite's own scale: 3 kaolinite → mullite + 4 SiO2 + 6 H2O, 774,471,000 myu = 774,471,000 myu (774.471 g), EXACT [computed, X3] — the mug's mass balance from green clay to fired body, water and all.

Lecture 2 — Levigation: the trench and the jar

Stokes' law, the prospecting tool. Why did the jar sort itself by morning? Because a particle sinking in water is slowed by viscosity, and the slowing is ferociously size-dependent — v = Δρ·g·d²/(18η), settling speed as the square of diameter (Ch 5's law, stated with its own honesty). The pins are Chapter 5's own register, re-grepped in its owner's page: Δρ 1652 kg/m³ (quartz 2650 against water's 998), g 9.81 m/s², η 1.002e-3 Pa·s [register: Ch 5's Stokes pins] — and the battery re-derives the jar's whole table at full precision for a 0.1 m column [computed, B1]:

sand d = 5e-4 m: v 2.246e-1 m/s, t 0.4451652 s silt d = 5e-5 m: v 2.246e-3 m/s, t 44.51652 s clay d = 1e-6 m: v 8.985e-7 m/s, t 111291.3 s = 30.9142 h

with the checks that make it a ledger rather than a table: v × t = 0.1 m EXACT all three, and the time ratios silt/sand = 100, clay/silt = 2500 EXACT — the square law audited at its own ratios: tenfold finer, a hundredfold slower; fiftyfold finer, twenty-five-hundredfold slower. That is the whole jar test: the sand has landed before you have put the lid down, the silt within the minute, and the clay takes a night and a day — which is why the clear water above it is the clay miner's assay [register: Ch 5's three-jar lecture].

The honest domain, inherited verbatim. Stokes' law is a laminar-flow law, and Chapter 5 machine-solved its own domain boundary — trustworthy below roughly 61 µm (its bisection's d* = 60.7 µm) — and printed the honesty that comes with it: above the boundary the model's speeds overpredict [register: Ch 5's domain boundary, its own]. The silt and clay rows sit inside the boundary; the sand row sits outside it, and is printed as what it is — the register's own seconds (the sand settles in seconds regardless; the verdict stands, the speed is the model's). The chapter inherits the seam rather than laundering it: the jar test's rankings are exact everywhere, and its sand-speed is Chapter 5's own stated overprediction, worn openly.

Levigation, the trench version. The miner's word for the same physics is older than the law: let the water flow, and the stream does the sieving — "the rocks and sand are left behind in the trench" [register: the levigation corpus line, printed at Ch 5, honored here]. Wild-clay prospecting is this physics plus the plasticity test: dig, soak, stir, pour off the suspended clay water from the settled grit, and let it settle in its own second jar — the clay that clears is the clay that suspends, and the clay that suspends is the clay that plates [register: the wild-clay canon, recognition level]. The jar test on the windowsill is levigation with the flow stopped: the same separation, priced in time instead of distance.

Lecture 3 — The lime cycle: the rock that breathes

Calcination: the kiln's own rung. Burn limestone and it lets its carbon dioxide go: CaCO3 → CaO + CO2, 100,086,000 myu = 100,086,000 myu (100.086 g), EXACT [computed, E2] — quicklime 56.077 from limestone 100.086, the CO2 44.009 the other feed [PINs Ch 14/30, Ch 24/30, Ch 20/13]. The trade's own scale figure is Chapter 14's verified canon: 785 kg CO2 per tonne of CaO [convergent: Ch 14's kiln figure], and the cement chapter's carbon ledger is the same rung at world scale — calcination two-thirds of clinker's CO2, fuel the other third [convergent: the USGS feature, Ch 30's canon honored]. Lime is a burned rock before it is anything else, and the burning is a chemical wage the wall pays back below.

Slaking: the dramatic door. Quicklime drinks water with the bench's most famous exotherm: CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2, 74,092,000 myu = 74,092,000 myu (74.092 g), EXACT [computed, E3], slaked lime 74.092 [PIN Ch 13/30]. The water demand is the practical warning in disguise — 18.015/56.077 = 0.321255 g of slaking water per gram of CaO [computed, PR3]: Chapter 14 printed the same number as its 32.1% water demand [convergent: Ch 14's own], and Chapter 21 printed 0.32125 — a truncation the engine's six-decimal half-up value governs, printed as the truncation it is [register: the re-grep, both owners honored]. Carbonation: the cycle's brake, and its closer. The wall finishes the loop on its own timescale: Ca(OH)2 + CO2 → CaCO3 + H2O, 118,101,000 myu = 118,101,000 myu (118.101 g), EXACT [computed, E7] — the battery's round trip [computed, B2]: the slaked lime takes back the very CO2 the kiln took away, returns the slaking water to the air, and becomes limestone again — the lime round trip: 118.101 g = 118.101 g, EXACT (E7's pair). The chapter's oldest date is the canon's own: lime burning from 7000 BCE [convergent: Ch 14's datum] — the earth shelf's chemistry is the oldest the Atlas has priced.

Dolomite, and the gypsum beside it. Where the limestone carries magnesium, it is dolomite — CaMg(CO3)2, 184.399 g/mol [PIN Ch 30] — and it burns twice: CaMg(CO3)2 → CaO + MgO + 2CO2, 184,399,000 myu = 184,399,000 myu (184.399 g), EXACT [computed, E8], with the fractions audited: CaO 30.4107% + MgO 21.857% + CO2 47.7324% = 100% at full precision, EXACT [computed, X4] — magnesia 40.304 the third oxide [PIN Ch 30]. And the sibling that never cycles: gypsum sets by hydration, its half-water plaster drinking water back — CaSO4·0.5H2O 145.1415 [computed, S2] against gypsum's 172.164 [computed, S8], the pair that prices Chapter 3's own exercise (a kilogram of gypsum gives 843.0 g of plaster, Ex 3.4's figure, reconciled at Chapter 30's engine [register: Ch 3's print, honored]) — plaster is a one-way shelf: set, and it holds; it does not breathe CO2, and it must never stand where the lime's 512 °C rung or the kiln's bands live.

Lecture 4 — Feldspar and ash: the glaze door

Ash, the forest's flux. Lecture 5 — The kiln: temperature as the reagent

The band map, knowledge only. The chapter fires nothing — the rule is Chapter 30's own, inherited verbatim: the reader reads clay, never fires it; every temperature in this lecture is a knowledge temperature, not a household one [register: Ch 30's safety row]. Read as a map, the kiln is a series of doors, each with its own chemistry:

  • 512 °C — slaked lime gives its water back as steam [convergent: Ch 14]; slaked lime never lives near flame.
  • 573 °C — the quartz inversion: α → β, an abrupt volume step that moves "horizontally across" a piece through the kiln's gradient — "if one side of a piece is at 573 and the other at 600… could start a crack at the first weakness it encounters" [convergent: the ceramics reference class, Ch 30's own pages and its own quote, honored]. The sand in the mortar and the silica in the glaze both feel this door; the potter feels it as the first firing's most famous crack.
  • 450–700 °C (the research band; the practical kiln window 500–800) — kaolinite's dehydroxylation, Lecture 1's water leaving as steam through the chimney [convergent: Ch 30's band].
  • ~950–1100 °C — metakaolin rebuilds to mullite [convergent: Ch 30's research canon]; by 1300 °C cristobalite begins to form from the amorphous Si-rich phases [convergent: the ceramics literature's own line, printed as found at Ch 30].
  • 1000–1060 °C (cone 04–06) — earthenware matures: the porous, colored body [convergent: Ch 30's cone-chart class]. ~1150–1250 °C (the cone 2–12 band's middle) — stoneware: vitrified, non-porous. ~1250–1300 °C and above (cone 8–13) — porcelain: the whitest, hardest, most glassy body, fired where mullite and cristobalite finish their work [convergent: the same class].
  • ~1450 °C — the cement kiln's sintering without melting, where limestone and clay fold into clinker [convergent: the cement canon, Ch 30's own]: alite Ca3SiO5 228.314 [PIN Ch 30], 50–85% of the clinker; belite Ca2SiO4 172.237 [PIN Ch 30], 15–30%; gypsum <5 wt% added at the grinder "to avoid the flash setting of the tricalcium aluminate" [convergent: the clinker references' own line, printed as found at Ch 30]. The dust the trade regulates is the same shelf's: OSHA's 50 µg/m³ line, printed by the regulator itself [convergent: Ch 30's canon].

Beside the cones, the honest primitive cousin: the open pit fire, the potter's oldest kiln, reaches roughly 650–980 °C — and earthenware bodies convert somewhere in the 650–760 °C band [convergent: the pit-firing references, verified this session] — low-fire territory: pit firing makes earthenware, never stoneware, and the temperature is the whole difference between the body classes. The strätlingite model rides the binder wing as the chapter's one flagged hydrate: Ca2Al2SiO7·8H2O, 418.318 g/mol [computed, N4 — NEW, zero-hit verified, MODEL] — the old cement pastes' own calcium-aluminate hydrate, a bookkeeping composition in the same sense the labeled dimer was on the animal shelf, printed as the model it is [flagged model].

The Seger window. The glaze matures inside this map at the window where its UMF sits: flux unity at 1, alumina at ~0.64, silica at ~5 [computed: B4's frozen values restated] — enough silica to form the glass, enough alumina to keep it from running off the pot [register: the trade's working reading of its own convention]. When the band map and the UMF are read together, the whole shelf is one instrument: the jar test grades the body, the assay clears the clay, the kiln's doors dehydroxylate and invert and vitrify it, and the ash glaze's fluxes — the tree's own minerals — close their ledger on the surface. No temperature here is a household temperature, and the chapter's bench is a windowsill, a bucket, and a reading lamp: the discipline is the reading.
