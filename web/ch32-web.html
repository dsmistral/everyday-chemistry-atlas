Chapter 32 — Light-Sensitive Chemistry: Photons as Reagents

The ruined world loses instruments, glassware, and specialty shelves — but it never loses the sun. Light is the one reagent that arrives every morning without being synthesized, distilled, or bought, and the household has always used it as such without saying so: the faded curtain, the skunked beer, the blueprint, the photograph. This is the chapter where the Atlas treats light the way it has treated every other reagent — weighed, priced, and entered in the ledger.

Lecture 0 — The Window Ledge

The phenomenon, before any notation. Take two identical pieces of something colored — a page of newsprint, a sheet of construction paper, a strip cut from a plastic bag — and pin them side by side: one on the sunny side of the window, one inside a closed drawer. Wait a week. The sunny piece is bleached, yellowed, or embrittled; the drawer piece is essentially unchanged. Nothing touched either sample but light and air, and the sample that met the light changed.

The pencil-and-paper version costs nothing: two cups of the same diluted tea, one in the sun and one in the dark, tasted blind a few days apart. The sun-struck cup drifts toward flat and stale; the dark cup holds. You do not need to know what a photon is to know that something arrived in the sunlit cup that never arrived in the dark one, and that it reacted.

What you could have found this way. Before any formalism, the window-ledge experiment already states the three facts that organize the whole chapter. First, light is a reagent: it participates in chemical change, and the change does not happen without it. Second, it is a dose: the same paper fades faster in July than in December, faster at noon than at dusk — the reagent arrives at a rate and the reaction integrates it over time. Third, it is selective: the newsprint yellows while the window glass itself, meeting the same sun, does nothing — light spends itself only where a bond is weak enough to buy what a photon costs. Everything below is those three sentences made quantitative.

Lecture 1 — The Photon Ledger

Light arrives in countable units. The energy of one unit is fixed by a single number — the wavelength — through a relation you can rebuild from three measured constants rather than memorize:

E = hc/λ.

With Planck's constant h = 6.62607015×10⁻³⁴ J·s, the speed of light c = 2.99792458×10⁸ m/s, and the elementary charge e = 1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹ J/eV, the convenient form falls out of the division (Lab 32.2 computes it; it is not assumed):

hc/e = 1239.841984 eV·nm — a photon of wavelength λ (in nanometers) carries 1239.841984/λ electron-volts.

The household band, computed in Lab 32.2:

λ (nm)eVkJ/mol
254 (germicidal UV-C)4.8813471.0
310 (UV-B edge)3.9995385.9
365 (cheap UV lamp)3.3968327.7
400 (violet edge)3.0996299.1
420 (blue)2.9520284.8

How you could have found the same table the other way around. A mole is just a count, so a photon's energy prices out per mole: 3.0996 eV × 96.485 kJ/mol per eV = 299.1 kJ/mol. Now set that against the bond-energy shelves from the earlier volumes: a C–C bond costs about 347 kJ/mol to break, O–H about 463, but the colored organics and the metal–halide bonds of this chapter live in the 250–350 kJ/mol band. Violet light at 400 nm (299.1 kJ/mol) cannot afford a C–C bond but can very nearly afford the weaker bonds that hold a dye together or a silver ion to its chloride; UV-C at 254 nm (471.0 kJ/mol) can break bonds visible light merely threatens. Selectivity — the third fact of Lecture 0 — is the price list. This is why the germicidal lamp sterilizes and the reading lamp does not, why paper fades but glass endures, and why the photochemist reaches for the short wavelengths when a stubborn bond must be moved.

Two honest fences on this table. First, a photon is not a mole: one molecule meets one photon at a time, and the fraction of absorbed photons that actually cause change (the quantum yield) is a separate, empirical number — recognized here, not computed. Second, the wavelength band that reaches your bench depends on the glass in the way: ordinary window glass transmits the near-UV and violet freely but cuts most of the UV-B and essentially all UV-C [single-source, recognition-only]. The cyanotype and the photolith both work through window glass; the sterilizing lamp does not pass through it — the same fact read from either side.

The dose. The reagent arrives at a rate. A small 365 nm lamp delivering 5 mW/cm² onto a coated paper for ten minutes hands the paper a total of 3.0 J/cm² — and, dividing by the energy per photon, about 5.512×10¹⁸ photons/cm², which is 9.155 µmol of photons per square centimeter (Lab 32.2; the dose line's first printout rounded this to 0.009 mmol — the slip is left on the record and the exact figure re-derived beside it). Fading, printing, and skunking are all this arithmetic wearing different clothes: intensity × time, divided by what one photon costs.

Lecture 2 — The Silver Window

The oldest light-sensitive bench is a precipitation. Chapter 19 met the trade and handed it forward: silver nitrate meets table salt, and

AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃ (E3)

a curdy white solid, insoluble enough that the reaction runs to completion in the mixing bowl. Silver chloride's mass on the Atlas's held element table is 143.350 g/mol (Ag 107.897 + Cl 35.453; Engine 17, PINS). Name the basis: older references, including Chapter 19's own print, carry 143.321 — that figure sits on the older silver basis 107.868, not on the held table's 107.897; the difference is the last digit's home, and both bases are honest when labeled (the Chapter 31 review's binding lesson, applied here at the drafting step rather than the review).

Spread the white AgCl in a thin layer in sunlight and it does what no kitchen salt does: within minutes it darkens — lilac, then violet-gray, then black. The photolysis is the simplest light-reaction in the household canon:

2 AgCl → 2 Ag + Cl₂ (E1) 2 AgBr → 2 Ag + Br₂ (E2, the twin — silver bromide, 187.801 g/mol, the traditional photographic emulsion)

Each absorbed photon can knock an electron onto a silver ion; the neutral silver atom stays as an infinitesimal speck of metal, and the chlorine finds its way out as the element. A trace of the change happens everywhere the light reached; where the light was blocked, nothing happens. Wash away the unchanged halide and the black pattern of metallic silver stays: a print-out, the direct ancestor of every photograph.

The fix. A print-out made of residual AgCl keeps darkening for years. The trade's answer is the fixer: sodium thiosulfate, the photographer's "hypo" (Ch. 17's thiosulfate bench):

AgCl + 2 Na₂S₂O₃ → Na₃[Ag(S₂O₃)₂] + NaCl (E4)

The silver is not destroyed; it is dissolved into the stable, washable complex [Ag(S₂O₃)₂]³⁻ (Na₃[Ag(S₂O₃)₂], 401.101 g/mol — recognition-only here; the coordination face belongs to the complex-ion chapters). Anhydrous Na₂S₂O₃ is 158.097 g/mol; the shelf form is the pentahydrate at 248.172 g/mol — the pentahydrate is what the arithmetic of Ex 32.3 spends. Ten grams in 50 mL makes a fixer-class bath (0.806 M, Lab 32.2) [recognition-only as to working-strength preference].

Recovery, closing the loop. Spent fixer holds its silver as the complex; the classic household recovery is Ch. 19's own displacement — copper metal pulls the silver back out:

Cu + 2 AgNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2 Ag (E6)

(the nitrate face here; in fixer the same displacement operates on the complex, more slowly — recognition). From 1.00 g of AgCl the silver fraction is exact bookkeeping: 107.897/143.350 = 75.2682 %, so the theoretical metal is 0.7527 g, whichever route you take it by — the mass fraction directly, or E1's stoichiometry (Ex 32.5 runs both ways; they must agree). The thermal door E5 (2 Ag₂O → 4 Ag + O₂, silver oxide 231.793 g/mol) exists for the oxide, but the displacement is the cheaper bench.

Sourcing, honestly. Silver nitrate is the one reagent of this lecture with no honest household synthesis: silver metal is a buy (jewelry scrap, mint coins) and nitric acid is Ch. 20's chapter, but the corpus's own audit marks silver nitrate effectively book-only at the everyday bench [the survey's honest note]. The retail face that survives the premise's test is the pharmacy's silver-nitrate caustic pencil [single-source, P1 read-the-label — verify the assay at purchase; these are Applicator-class items, dilute]. Everything else in the lecture — salt, copper, hypo (Ch. 17) — is already on the Atlas's shelves.

Lecture 3 — The Iron Window

The cyanotype is the Atlas's honest photograph. It needs no silver, no gelatin, no darkroom: two iron salts and paper. Sensitize paper with a mixture of ferric ammonium citrate (the Fe³⁺-citrate, a green powder of deliberately loose, variable composition — the Atlas treats it as a reagent of declared assay, not a formula [flagged sketch]) and potassium ferricyanide K₃[Fe(CN)₆], 329.247 g/mol. Dry the paper in the dark. Lay leaves, lace, or a drawing on translucent film over it; set it in the sun.

The light writes on the ferric salt: the citrate carries the charge, and absorbed violet/near-UV light reduces Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺ — only where the light reached. A rinse in plain water then develops everything at once, because ferrous iron and ferricyanide precipitate instantly as the deepest blue the household bench ever makes:

4 Fe²⁺ + 4 [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻ → Fe₄[Fe(CN)₆]₃↓ + [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ (E8)

Prussian blue — Chapter 22's intervalence pigment, pinned there at 859.239 g/mol and reproduced by Engine 17 from the held table digit for digit: 7 Fe × 55.845 + 18 C × 12.011 + 18 N × 14.007. The iron in the pigment is 45.4955 % by mass (Lab 32.2). The spent side of the ledger is the ferrocyanide ion K₄[Fe(CN)₆] (368.345 g/mol), which stays dissolved and washes away. Note what the equation's charge ledger says (Ex 32.4): the left side carries 4×(+2) + 4×(−3) = −4 and the right side 0 + (−4) = −4 — a redox balanced in charge as well as atoms, which is the cyanotype's whole secret: the light-driven Fe³⁺→Fe²⁺ conversion is repaid by [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻→[Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻.

How you could have found the fence before the chemistry. The same principle as Lecture 0's third fact, run in reverse: paper coated and kept in the drawer stays greenish-yellow; paper exposed behind an opaque leaf develops blue everywhere except the leaf's shadow. The image is the dose ledger made visible — a contact print, the sun's own negative.

The safety fence, non-negotiable. Potassium ferricyanide and ferrocyanide are low-hazard solids as solids and in neutral water — but they are cyanide complexes, and acid is the one thing they must never meet: acidifying them can liberate HCN gas. The rule is absolute in this book: cyanide-complex salts never contact acid, never contact bleach, and go to disposal in their own labeled container (Safety Charter, row 4 below). The cyanotype wash water at neutral pH is ordinary waste water; the discipline is about preventing the accident, not about the working solution. The blueprint lineage is exactly this chemistry at engineering scale — the copy clerk's sun, one hundred and eighty years of paperwork.

Lecture 4 — The Kitchen Photolyses

Chapter 31 closed by pointing at this lecture: three doors in the ordinary kitchen where light is the active reagent.

The milk bottle. Milk in a clear bottle on the doorstep goes off-flavored in hours of sun; the same milk in the dark keeps its clean taste for days. The reagent is riboflavin — vitamin B₂, C₁₇H₂₀N₄O₆, 376.369 g/mol on the held table — which absorbs strongly at visible/near-UV wavelengths and, instead of merely fading, passes its excitement along to other molecules: proteins and the sulfur notes of milk acquire the cardboard-y, light-struck taint [convergent — the phenomenon is named across dairy literature and the household canon; the mechanism at recognition level]. The grocer's answer is the opaque bottle; the Atlas's answer is the same one the cyanotype takes: keep the dose at zero.

The skunked beer. Green and clear-bottled beer turn "skunky" in light; brown glass largely prevents it. The chemistry is precise enough to carry numbers: hop bitterness rests on the isohumulones (C₂₁H₃₀O₅, 362.466 g/mol, formula basis [single-source]), and light plus a trace of riboflavin (again the sensitizer!) cleaves them down to 3-methyl-2-butene-1-thiol — MBT, C₅H₁₀S, 102.195 g/mol — the identical compound a skunk sprays, detectable by the human nose at vanishingly tiny concentrations [convergent for the phenomenon and the identification; the sensory threshold is recognition-only]. Two lessons general straight out of this chapter's theory: riboflavin as a borrowed photon handler (it absorbs what the target molecule cannot, then hands over the energy — sensitization, the mirror of the cyanotype's citrate), and the brown bottle as filtration in the package — glass that itself plays the dose ledger's gatekeeper.

The vitamin photolyses — and the one that runs the good way. Riboflavin itself photodegrades (the milk keeps its B₂ only in the dark); vitamin C in clear-bottled juice fades in the light [convergent, recognition level]. But the household's most famous light reaction builds rather than destroys: in skin, UV-B (roughly 290–315 nm) converts 7-dehydrocholesterol toward previtamin D₃, which is why sunlight is a nutritional input and why the UV-B band is the one band this chapter treats with respect rather than only caution [single-source, recognition-only — the sterol chemistry belongs to the biochemistry shelf]. Light is a reagent; reagents have no morals — the ledger decides whether the line is a gain or a loss.

Lecture 5 — Reading the Image

A developed cyanotype is a physical record of photon bookkeeping, and the print can be read like any ledger. Hold the paper up to a lamp: the pale areas pass light, the deep blue areas absorb it. The instrumentless meter is the transmittance T — the fraction of light passed — and its logarithm is the optical density:

OD = −log₁₀ T (Lab 32.2: T = 10 % → OD 1.000; T = 1 % → OD 2.000; T = 0.1 % → OD 3.000; and back: OD 0.3 passes 50.12 %, OD 2.0 passes 1.00 %.)

Why the logarithm? Because attenuation is multiplicative — two panes of OD 1 glass stack to OD 2, not to 20 % — and the bookkeeping habit of this Atlas (Ch. 1's rule, every log since) is to take the quantity that adds. Every printing decision in this chapter is one of three moves in this ledger: expose (add photons where you want the change), mask (block them — the leaf's shadow, the negative, the drawing on film), or filter (let the source or the package do the masking — brown glass, window glass's UV-B cutoff).

The mask arithmetic generalizes as far as you like: a sunscreen labeled SPF 30 is a mask transmitting 1/30 = 3.3333 % of the UV-B dose, blocking 96.6667 % (Lab 32.2) — the same ledger entry as the leaf's shadow, priced for the drugstore shelf. And the negative is the ledger's most elegant trick: expose a contact print through a first print and you reverse every entry — blue where white was, white where blue was. The photograph, the blueprint, and the suntan are all one idea wearing three costumes: a dose of photons, spent where a bond could afford it, recorded by the material that paid.

Lab 32.1 — Engine 17: The Innermost-First Rewriter (design, honesty record, battery)

Design. A fresh per-chapter drafting engine, sharing zero code with all sixteen prior engines. Where its predecessors parsed (recursive descent, state tables, reverse walks, stack machines, token loops), Engine 17 rewrites: a formula is never tokenized at all. The evaluator scans for the first close-bracket — which by construction always closes an innermost, bracket-free group — then multiplies the group's flat contents by the trailing subscript and splices the scaled string back in place. Each rewrite deletes one bracket pair; termination is therefore structural, not a hope. Compositions are carried as prime-vectors (each element a distinct prime: H 2, C 3, N 5, O 7 … Si 79; a composition is a map prime → exponent, equality is exact map equality, and the canonical serialization is prime-ascending). Masses are exact integers in myria-units (1 myu = 1e-4 g/mol) — every held-22 mass is integral at that scale — and hydrate multipliers are exact rationals under a divisibility gate. The held-22 element table was re-transcribed independently before any computation.

Honesty record. Run 1: the engine's arithmetic was fully clean — every mass, composition, balance, charge, and photon gate correct — and all three failures were the harness's own (self-test expectations written in input order instead of the engine's canonical prime-ascending order; H₂ misfiled as a refusal case — a legitimate formula — and replaced by Au; the must-fail flag inverted). Run 2: one residual harness expectation still misfiled (K/Al/S typed in input order; the engine's H₂₄O₂₀Al₁S₂K₁ is the correct ascending key). No engine arithmetic moved across runs. Exercises

Ex 32.1 (●○○). A cheap UV lamp emits at 365 nm. (a) What is the energy of one of its photons, in eV and in kJ/mol? (b) Could this photon break a bond costing 330 kJ/mol? One costing 500 kJ/mol? Hint: the photon ledger, Lecture 1. Worked, two ways: (a) Directly: 1239.841984/365 = 3.3968 eV; × 96.485 = 327.7 kJ/mol (P-IDs: the photon ledger; P9's convergent check — the Lab 32.2 row prints the same). The second way: ratio from a known row — 365 nm is 400/365 of the way shorter than the 400 nm row's 3.0996 eV, and E scales as 1/λ, so 3.0996 × (400/365) = 3.3968 eV, same digits. (b) 327.7 < 330: no (not by the direct photon; only a sensitized or multi-photon path could); 327.7 < 500: certainly not — that price sits at the 254 nm shelf (471.0 kJ/mol, still short of 500).

Ex 32.2 (●○○). 0.50 g of silver nitrate is dissolved and mixed with excess table salt. What mass of AgCl precipitates? Hint: E3 is a 1:1 precipitation. Worked, two ways: Moles first: 0.50 g / 169.901 g/mol = 2.9429 mmol; 1:1 gives 2.9429 mmol AgCl × 143.350 g/mol = 0.4219 g (P3 metathesis-precipitation; P12 yield bookkeeping at the theoretical line). The second way: mass fraction along the reaction — the precipitate's mass is the silver nitrate's mass scaled by (143.350/169.901) × (1 mol/1 mol) = 0.84380; 0.50 × 0.84380 = 0.4219 g, same digits, no moles needed. (Lab 32.2 prints the first way.)

Ex 32.3 (●●○). A print-out holds 1.00 g of unreduced AgCl. What is the minimum mass of hypo pentahydrate (Na₂S₂O₃·5H₂O) that can dissolve it all, per E4? Hint: count the thiosulfate, not the water. Worked, two ways: Stoichiometry: 1.00/143.350 = 6.9759 mmol AgCl; E4 demands 2 mol thiosulfate per mol AgCl → 13.9519 mmol; × 248.172 g/mol = 3.4625 g pentahydrate. Second way, via the anhydrous salt first: 13.9519 mmol × 158.097 = 2.2059 g Na₂S₂O₃; the pentahydrate is anhydrous × (248.172/158.097) = 2.2059 × 1.5699 = 3.4625 g — the hydrate conversion checked on its own axis (P12; P6's concentration logic). Both agree with Lab 32.2's row.

Ex 32.4 (●●○). The cyanotype precipitation (E8) is unusual in this book: it balances charge as well as atoms. Verify both ledgers, and say in one sentence why the charge ledger is what proves the reaction is a redox. Hint: Ex the ledger, not the chemistry. Worked, two ways: Atoms by prime-vectors (the Engine 17 map prints C24N24Fe8 both sides — equality by exact exponent-map compare, no arithmetic at all). Charge: left 4×(+2) + 4×(−3) = −4; right 0 + (−4) = −4. The one-sentence answer: charge moves only if electrons move — Fe drops from +2 (staying +3 would leave nothing to pay) while [Fe(CN)₆] drops from −3 to −4, so the ferric-citrate photoreduction must have supplied the electron that the ferricyanide accepted. (P9 instrumentless verification; the atom map is the machine's, the charge ledger the reader's.)

Ex 32.5 (●●● — the summit). From 1.00 g of AgCl you isolate 0.60 g of silver. (a) What is the theoretical silver mass? (b) What is the recovery, and (c) name two independent routes to the theoretical number and show they agree. Hints: Lecture 2's fraction; E1's stoichiometry; E6's displacement — pick any two and reconcile. Worked: (a) 107.897/143.350 = 75.2682 % → 0.7527 g. (b) 0.60/0.7527 = 79.71 % recovery. (c) Route 1, the mass fraction directly (the silver never leaves the solid in E1's arithmetic — 2 AgCl → 2 Ag + Cl₂ carries the whole silver inventory across). Route 2, the stoichiometric count: 6.9759 mmol AgCl → 6.9759 mmol Ag × 107.897 = 0.7527 g; the mole route and the fraction route are the same arithmetic seen from the ledger's two columns (P12; the two-way rule is the exercise's actual point). Lab 32.2 prints the reconciliation verbatim.
