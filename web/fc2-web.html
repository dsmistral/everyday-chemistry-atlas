The Field Companion · FC2 — Signal and Light: Being Found, Seeing at Night

The Field Companion's first chapter taught the oldest reaction; this one teaches the oldest use of it. A fire that nobody can see is heat; a fire that searchers can see is rescue — and the difference between the two is not luck or size but three numbers the eye works by: contrast, motion, and scale. This chapter reads the signal problem as chemistry of an honest kind: the mirror that redirects a kilowatt it never made, the smoke that is a chemical choice (white droplets or black carbon, chosen by what the fuel is and how starved the flame), the sound that fades by an exact law the meter can price, and the lamp that is a fuel-metering device with a wick for a valve. The fence holds as it held for thirty-six chapters and one field chapter: the trade's signal devices — the smoke grenade, the flare, the white phosphorus, the tracer, the firework — are recognized, never synthesized (P18), and the refusal itself is taught as a skill. What remains is the kit a ruined world actually owns: the sun, a mirror, three fires, and a candle whose chemistry the book has already priced.

Lecture 0 — The problem of being seen: a pencil-and-paper census of visibility

The phenomenon, before any notation. Sit down with the notebook — pour nothing, light nothing — and take the census the situation actually demands: if a search party is working the valley this afternoon, what will make them see you? Not your presence: a person in a landscape is roughly a meter of upright matter, and at a kilometer that meter spans about one milliradian — thinner to the eye than a hair held at arm's length. The honest census lists what the rescuer's eye can actually register, and it is short: difference (a patch that does not match its background), change (something that moves when everything else holds still), and size (a shape big enough, or wide enough, to survive the distance). Everything in this chapter serves one of those three, and every one of the three has a chemistry under it. The white smoke column is a difference. The waving cloth is a change. The cut-and-placed shadow line of a ground symbol is a size. The mirror flash is all three at once — a moving, sun-bright difference the size of the solar disk.

The chapter's thesis, stated once at the door: being seen is not being big; it is being different at the moment someone is looking — and the field chemist's tools for manufacturing difference are light, smoke, and sound, each one priced at this book's meter. FC1 owned the making of fire; this chapter owns the aiming of it — at an eye across a valley, at the pilot's window, at the night around the camp. The two lectures on smoke and lamps are combustion chemistry continued (the same reactions, read for what they emit rather than what they heat); the two lectures on mirrors and sound are physics the household owns without any bench at all.

Two fences before the kit is unpacked. First: the trade's signal shelf is recognition-only (P18) — the orange smoke grenade, the red signal flare, the white-phosphorus marker, the tracer, the firework salute are all real, all effective, and all absolutely out of scope for the maker's bench; they are also, in a ruined world, exactly the items whose rusting canisters the scavenger will meet, and the chapter teaches their faces (colors, behaviors, hazards) precisely so they can be recognized and left alone. The engine prints the refusals themselves as battery rows (Lab FC2.1, R2). Second: every flame discipline from FC1 stands unrelaxed — the fire tetrahedron, the fuel bands, the demonstration-tier rules — because a signal fire is still a fire, and a smoke column built in dry grass is a wildfire with a message written on it.

Lecture 1 — Contrast, motion, scale: the eye's three terms of trade

The eye is not a camera; it is a difference engine. Three of its terms matter here, and each one converts directly into bench chemistry.

Contrast: the Weber bargain. The eye separates a patch from its background only when the two luminances differ by a noticeable fraction, not an amount — the classical result (Weber's law in its vision form) is that against a broad, even background the detectable difference sits near two percent of the background's luminance [convergent]. The operational translation is unkind and useful: a white shirt against snow is invisible; a dark one reads. Against bright sky, dark smoke reads and white smoke dissolves; against dark timber, the reverse. The signal-maker's first decision is therefore not "make smoke" but read the background — and choose the plume the background will refuse to swallow. The two smokes are not the same substance gone wrong; they are different chemistry (Lecture 3), and the choice between them is the first contrast calculation the field chemist makes.

Motion: the periphery's alarm. The eye's periphery is a motion detector first and a shape-reader second — the reason a still, correctly-colored object can go unseen for hours while a waving scrap of the same cloth pulls the gaze in seconds. The techniques the canon registers (SAS, Bushcraft 101 [register]) all exploit it: wave the cloth, sweep the mirror, and — at night — never stand still inside the fire's glow if a light moving along the ridge might be a searcher's lamp answering yours.

Scale: the alphabet drawn on the ground. For the ground-to-air case the world's survival canon settles on a short alphabet of high-contrast symbols, each several meters across: V (require assistance), X (need medical assistance), and the universal distress rule of three — three fires, three columns of smoke, three whistle blasts, three flashes [register; convergent in the international signal guides]. The canon's own geometry rules are honest ones the Weber logic explains: the symbol's strokes must contrast with their background (green boughs on snow, dark logs on ice, shadow-trenches in grass), and a three-foot SOS is invisible from five thousand feet while a thirty-foot one reads — scale is contrast that survives distance [convergent]. The bench's contribution is material chemistry: what makes the darkest dark (charred logs) and the brightest laid line (fresh-cut boughs, split white wood) — both of them products of FC1's pyrolysis ladder, read here as pigments.

Lecture 2 — The heliograph: redirecting the only free kilowatt

The sun delivers roughly a kilowatt to every square meter of clear ground — the one radiant power source the ruined world keeps without maintaining — and the signal mirror's trick is that it redirects that power rather than generating any. A flat mirror turned to face a distant point sends the reflected image of the solar disk downrange; the flash is that image crossing the target. The geometry is fixed by the Sun's own size: the solar disk spans about 0.53° — thirty-two arc-minutes — as seen from Earth [convergent], so the reflected cone opens with a half-angle of 0.265°, and a heliograph spot at one kilometer is a patch about 4.63 meters wide [computed; L5 in the battery: the tangent series evaluated exactly at θ = 0.265° = 3763/813600 rad]. That width is the mirror's honesty: aiming matters, but the target does not have to be laser-precise — at a kilometer the flash covers four and a half meters; the commercial mirror cards claim visibility "over 10 miles" [convergent], which is the same arithmetic compounded by the eye's enormous dynamic range. The model behind the 4.63 m is stated, not hidden: a perfect flat mirror, no surface-scatter divergence, the Sun's disk alone limiting the beam [flagged model].

The meter the whole chapter runs on: candela, lumen, lux. The candle owns the unit — by deliberate history, a common wax candle's luminous intensity is roughly one candela [convergent] — and the chain from candle to searchlight is three steps the engine prices exactly (Lab FC2.2): a source of one candela radiating into the full sphere (4π steradians) delivers 12.5664 lumens (the battery's L1, with π = 355/113 labeled); the light falling on a surface — the illuminance — falls off as the inverse square of distance, so the same isotropic candle gives 1.00 lux at 1 meter, 0.25 lux at 2 meters, 0.01 lux at 10 meters (L2). The dimensions of that last quantity are their own lesson: illuminance is luminous intensity per area, L⁻²·cd¹ (battery D3, walked on the lattice, not asserted).

Why the mirror beats the candle, quantified. The candle audit (L3/L4 in the battery) prices the honest household flame: a candle in the standard consumption class — about six grams of wax per hour [flagged: consumption class; the measured literature runs five to seven g/h, convergent] — burning a 42 MJ/kg fuel [flagged: band input] is a 70.0 W chemical source, and of that seventy watts the light itself is 4π cd ≈ 12.5664 lumens, which at the eye's peak conversion of 683 lumens per radiant watt [convergent — the SI's own defining constant for the candela] is about 0.018 radiant watt: 0.0263 percent of the flame's power is light (L4). The mirror, meanwhile, redirects sunlight — kilowatts per square meter of it, more than a thousand candle-powers per hand-sized patch on a good angle — at a chemical cost of zero. Being seen by day: the mirror wins by four orders of magnitude, and it never burns the camp down. The candle's role is the night half of the problem, and it gets its own lecture (L5).

The aiming technique belongs to the canon [register]: the sighting hole (a hole in the mirror's center, through which the target is watched while a small bright spot — the "aimer" — is brought onto the same spot from the front face), and the sweep rule for aircraft: never hold the flash on a cockpit; sweep it across the aircraft's line of flight so it crosses the windows once [convergent in the mirror-technique guides]. The safety row for this lives in the table at the chapter's close.

Lecture 3 — Smoke: the plume is a chemical choice — white droplets or black carbon

Smoke is not "fire's smell"; it is a cloud the fire manufactures, and its color is a composition the fire's operator chooses. Read honestly, a smoke column is an aerosol with two different recipes.

White smoke is a water story. The pale plume of damp fuel — green vegetation, wet leaves, the hastily-fed fire — is dominated by condensed droplets: steam the flame could not keep hot, plus fine ash and tars, nucleating into a cloud that scatters all wavelengths and reads white. It is dense against dark timber and nearly invisible against bright sky — the contrast arithmetic of Lecture 1 again. The field canon's smudge technique — green boughs laid on a hot fire the moment the aircraft is heard [register] — is droplet chemistry used deliberately: the boughs' own moisture, driven off fast, makes the plume fat and white. The time constant matters and is honest: the boughs land on a fire that is already hot (FC1's fuel-band arithmetic), because a cold fire makes no plume at all.

Dark smoke is a carbon story. The black column is soot — elemental carbon born in a flame that had fuel but not enough oxygen, growing as vapor-phase carbon rings stack into particles that glow yellow in the flame zone and scatter black in the plume. The book's model of it is printed as a model: ethanol starved of two-thirds of its air books down to C2H6O + O2 → 2 C + 3 H2O, the closure exact at 780.670 myu (battery B7) — bookkeeping-exact, mechanism-disclaimed [flagged model]: real soot is a tangled agglomerate, not clean graphite, but the ledger is right — the oxygen that never arrived is the oxygen that did not become CO2 and H2O, and the carbon left over is the black you see. The recipe follows from the ledger: dark smoke wants fuel-rich and oxygen-poor — an oil-fed smudge (a rag of fat, lamp oil on a tin), never a clean hot draft. Per gram, the trades' arithmetic is the battery's X-ledger: a fat's complete-combustion air debt (X5: tristearin borrows 2.9252 g of O2 per gram) becomes, in the starved flame, carbon instead of CO2 — the same fuel, read for opacity instead of heat.

The P18 lecture: the trade's smokes, recognized and refused. The smoke grenade's screen, the flare's trail, white phosphorus' persistent burning flakes, the tracer's line, the firework salute's burst — the engine refuses all five by name at the gate (Lab FC2.1, R2, exact strings), and this chapter refuses them on the bench: recognition-only, faces known so the scavenger leaves them unfired [P18]. White phosphorus especially earns its place in the recognition set: it ignites on contact with air and burns flesh through to the bone — a hazard of the battlefield's leftover store, not a chemistry to attempt [register-class knowledge, stated at recognition level]. The household's own materials are honest about their limits too: plastics, foams, painted or treated wood, and anything pesticide-sprayed are refused as smoke fuels — their plumes are dioxins and acid vapors, not signals (Safety row 3). Natural fuel, chosen color, fed at the right moment: that is the whole art, and it is enough.

Lecture 4 — Ground-to-air and sound: the alphabet and the alarm

The day symbols belong to the eye; the near-range channel belongs to the ear, and both are more chemistry than they look.

The symbols, drawn as chemistry. The V and the X and the SOS are made of material the household's own reactions produce: charred logs for the dark stroke (FC1's char rung, read as pigment), split white wood or fresh boughs for the bright one, and — in snow — the trench itself, whose shadow is the ink [register for the shapes]. Three fires set in a triangle is the international distress answer that needs no drawing at all, and three smoke columns are the same word written with plumes [convergent in the signal guides]. The placement rule the canon prints — symbols on high open ground, strokes 3–6 meters, contrast against background — is Weber arithmetic in field dress [register; convergent].

Sound, priced by the meter. Sound is the pressure wave the air carries at 343 m/s at 20 °C [convergent], and its fading obeys the inverse-square law familiar from Lecture 2's light: each doubling of distance costs 6 dB [convergent]. The battery prices the whistle: a hundred-decibel-class pea whistle at arm's length is about 60.0 dB at 100 meters — the drop is 20·log10(100) = 40.0 dB exactly (L7) — which is still a clear signal against a quiet valley's 30-ish dB background. The echo turns the same speed into a rangefinder: a cliff that answers in 2.000 s stands 343 m away (L6: 343 m/s × 2.000 s ÷ 2) — the one measurement in this chapter that measures the world instead of the kit. And the international ear reads threes here too: three whistle blasts, three seconds apart, repeated [register].

The channel plan. Smoke for the day search, mirror flash for the day search with aircraft, fire glow and lamp for the night, sound for the last kilometer — the plan's honest core is that each channel is cheap in one resource and dear in another, and the meter makes the trade visible: the mirror spends nothing, the smoke spends fuel and attention, the whistle spends nothing, the lamp spends the night's fuel reserve. Choose the channel that matches what you can afford to lose — the same release-rate thinking FC1 taught as P17, now applied to attention as the resource.

Lecture 5 — The night light: candles, rushlights, oil lamps, and the wick's law

Every lamp in the household's history is one machine: a fuel-metering device whose flame's size is the fuel's arrival rate — P17 release-rate control, wearing a different name. The wick is the valve, and its operating principle is capillarity: fuel climbs the fine fiber channels against gravity, and the climb's height is the Jurin law the battery prices exactly — 0.297 meters for a 50-µm pore at full wetting (W1, with γ = 0.0728 N/m, ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s²) [computed; flagged model: circular pore, contact angle zero — real wicks are braided labyrinths, and the number is the idealization's ceiling]. That ceiling is why wicks are bundles of fine fibers and not one coarse channel: the household's whole wick craft — the candle's braided cotton, the rushlight's pith, the oil lamp's cord — is pore engineering, and the wick's own material is cellulose (C6H10O5, 162.141, Ch 25's pin), the reagent network's fiber [computed].

The candle, audited. The candle is wax plus wick, and both are already on the book's pins: paraffin wax is C25H52 (352.691, Ch 23's pin) and the natural-wax shelf's beeswax is myricyl palmitate (C46H92O2, 677.240, Ch 23's pin again); stearin — the classic candle hard — is Ch 23's stearic acid (C18H36O2, 284.484), whose fat-shelf neighbors oleic acid (282.468), triolein (885.453) and tristearin (891.501) join the story as the oil-lamp fuels [all computed battery rows]. The flame's own chemistry is FC1's pyrolysis ladder in miniature: wax melted at the wick, vaporized in the dark zone, cracked and burnt in the luminous zone — and the yellow the candle is loved for is soot incandescence, the Lecture-3 carbon glowing in the flame zone. The audit's two honest numbers close the loop from Lecture 2: 70.0 W of chemical power at the standard six-grams-per-hour consumption class [flagged: class input, 42 MJ/kg band], of which 0.0263% is light — which is why the candle lights a room but cannot signal a valley.

Why the yellow flame is the signal flame. The X-ledger ranks the fuels by the air they borrow: ethanol 2.0837 g of O2 per gram, stearic 2.9244, tristearin 2.9252, paraffin 3.4476, dodecane 3.4752 (X1–X5, all [computed]) — and the bottom of the list explains the top of the lecture: ethanol burns so clean (oxygen already built into its own molecule, C2H6O, 46.069, Ch 21's pin) that its flame barely soots, and a flame that does not soot barely radiates yellow — the alcohol lamp is nearly invisible in daylight, the worst signal flame in the kit and one of the best reading flames. For being seen, choose the fuels high on the debt list — the waxes and oils whose hydrogen-rich, oxygen-poor bills leave carbon glowing in the flame; for seeing by, the clean-burning shelf wins (less smoke in the tent, less soot on the chimney). The two purposes pick opposite ends of the same ledger, and the ledger — not folklore — is why.

The oil lamp and the rushlight. The kerosene lamp prices at 63.9 W at five grams per hour (L8, at kerosene's 46 MJ/kg higher-heating value [convergent]) — in the candle's own power class, from a fuel that pours. The kerosene class sits in the middle of the debt ledger (it is a dodecane-class cut of the barrel — see the N1 story in the Ledger) and its flame, wick-trimmed and blue-capped, is a reading flame; starved on purpose it becomes the dark-smoke burner of Lecture 3. The rushlight is the same machine at zero cost: a rush's pith (cellulose again — the wick is both valve and fuel here) dipped in kitchen fat, burning the fat Ch 23 already owns at fat-class debts. And the solvent shelf's flammables — isopropanol (60.096), acetone (58.080), Ch 21's pins — are recognized and fenced: they are not lamp fuels, and the wrong-fuel row in the Safety table is written in exactly their direction [computed pins; the fence is procedural].

One meter, three lights. Candle, lamp, rushlight: 70.0 W, 63.9 W, and whatever the fat pot affords — all but three-hundredths of a percent heat, all night-long drain on the same fuel store FC1 inventoried. The lamp's P17 lesson, restated for the dark hours: the flame's rate is a decision, made with the wick's trim, the wick's height, and the fuel's choice — and the meter, as always, is the contract.

Lab FC2.1 — The parallel-rounds rewriter, and the honest run history

Engine 29 is this chapter's verification machine, built fresh for FC2 and sharing zero code with the twenty-eight engines before it. Its idea is one sentence: every innermost bracket group in a formula is expanded at once, and the parser then walks a flat string — no recursion, no descent, no parse tree; one regex-global round rewrites all innermost groups (both parentheses and square brackets) simultaneously, scaling each group's element tokens by the group's trailing multiplier, looping until the string is flat. The guards are the design's spine: a round that makes no progress while brackets remain refuses the formula as UNCLOSED/UNMATCHED BRACKET (R4's (CH3); more than twenty-four rounds trips the NON-TERMINATION gate. The flat string then splits on the hydrate dot (·), each segment carrying an optional decimal coefficient parsed as an exact rational; bad characters and unknown symbols are refused with their positions (R6's @ at position 4, R5's W), and — the design's one deliberate severity — a single integrality gate on the total mass: atom counts may stay rational, but the mass must be a whole number of myu, so CH4·0.35H2O is refused at 223482.5000 myu (R3) — honesty about fractional metering rather than a silent round. The element table is the held {C,H,O} trio (C 120110, H 10080, O 159990 myu — the thirteenth zero-admission chapter: every other symbol, silver and tungsten included, is refused at the table). The P18 restricted-name gate fires first in the compose (the five field-companion refusals, R2, exact strings); the dimension lattice carries {M, L, T, cd} with the canonical display order (D1–D3); π is 355/113, labeled wherever it is used; every displayed decimal is one half-up rounding of an exact rational, made once, at the stated precision.
