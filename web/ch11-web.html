Vol I · Chapter 11 — Redox and Metals: The Activity Series, Displacement, Corrosion

Every chapter so far has treated the electron as a rumor. Chapter 7 burned fuels — the great oxidation of carbon — but priced only the heat. Chapter 8 moved protons; protons are charge without electrons. Chapter 9 swapped ions partner for partner and was, chemically, the no-electron chapter: metathesis rearranges, it never reduces. This chapter finally moves the electron itself, and the household turns out to be the arena where it moves most: every coin, nail, wire, foil, and battery terminal is a redox ledger mid-entry. Metals are the reduced state, and the world is the oxidized state; every metal object in your house is uphill against the chemistry around it, held there only by kinetics — the rust you fight is the world collecting its debt at slow speed. The ruined-world premise is strongest of all here: a supply chain loses metal stock first, but it can never lose metal chemistry — the activity series is a law, not an inventory, and a nail, a copper pipe, and a bottle of drain opener contain between them the whole electrochemical cast. The corpus supplies the practitioners: the cobalt episode whose performer states the activity-series reasoning at the bench — "the cobalt is much less reactive than aluminum... the cobalt should precipitate out" (N98) — the zinc-powder cell whose hydrogen current is priced as a competing loss and whose product is pyrophoric when dried (N76), the lithium episode whose water-drop cascade and nitrogen tarnish show the series' hot end in one gesture (N100), the cesium displacement that closes the alkali chain (N105), the thermite faces that mark the chapter's hard boundary (N79/N81, knowledge-only), the brass etch where corrosion is run as a method (N68), and Chapter 9's own malachite — the self-made ore sitting in the cabinet, waiting for this chapter to smelt it back to metal (N59; Thompson's signature experiment, closed at last). The chapter's four questions, in bench order: which way do electrons flow, what does the flow build or destroy, how do you make the flow work for you, and how do you stop it when it is eating your house?

Lecture 0 — The house is a metal library (and the world wants it back)

Walk the house and read it as an oxidation-state ledger. The aluminum foil: Al(0), thermodynamically desperate to be Al(III) oxide — the passivation film is the only reason the roll survives a week. The copper pipe: Cu(0), which is why the pipes will outlive the plumber but turn green where the rain gets them. The steel wool: Fe(0), a week from FeOOH if you leave it wet. The galvanized nail: Fe(0) wearing a Zn(0) coat — the zinc wants to corrode, and that is its job. The penny: since 1982, a copper costume over a zinc body [convergent — 2.500 g, 97.5% Zn, copper-plated]. The battery in the drawer: lithium and copper separated by an electrolyte, an activity series wound into a can. The corrosion on the old shovel: not damage but completion — Fe(III) oxide hydroxide, the ore the shovel was made from, going home.

So the first reframe: corrosion is not a house falling apart; it is ore forming in real time. Iron was never found as iron; it was found as Fe₂O₃ and reduced at fierce cost in a furnace. Every iron object is borrowed order. The same is true of aluminum (bauxite), copper (malachite — Chapter 9 made it deliberately), zinc (sphalerite), and — at the extreme end — the alkali metals, which exist in nature only as salts and which N100's water-drop cascade shows paying their full debt in a heartbeat (2 Li + 2 H₂O → 2 LiOH + H₂, BALANCED, the most violent household redox the corpus performs at camera scale, knowledge-only).

And the second reframe: reduction is the skill. The ruined world's metal problem is not finding metal — the world is ankle-deep in oxides, carbonates, and old batteries — it is un-oxidizing it. Every metal you will ever use at the bench is made one of two ways: thermally (carbon or aluminum stealing the oxide's oxygen at high heat — the smelting and thermite routes, mostly beyond the home charter) or electrochemically (a more active metal or an electric current pushing the electrons back — the displacement and electrolysis routes, one of which is this chapter's bench, the other Chapter 12's). The corpus's element-isolation series — zinc powder from roofing strips (N76), cobalt from spent phone batteries via aluminum foil (N98), tin crystals from stannous chloride (N99/N103), chromium and vanadium from pottery oxides at the thermite boundary (N79/N81) — is exactly this choice performed nine times over: which reductant, at what price, against which oxide? That is the chapter, previewed by its practitioners.

Lecture 1 — The electron ledger: oxidation states from the household shelf

The intuition first. You could have found oxidation states with a kitchen scale and a suspicious mind. Take copper to the stove: reddish metal goes in, a black powder comes out, and the powder is heavier — per gram of copper you started with, the black stuff weighs 1.252 g [computed: CuO is 79.887% Cu]. Something invisible joined the copper and would not leave. Charcoal burns to a gas that escapes; copper "burns" to a solid that stays and gains. That invisible something is oxygen, and the ledger entry is an oxidation number: the copper has gone from 0 to +2. Heat the black powder harder in a flame and a red-tinged solid appears — Cu₂O, where copper sits at only +1 [computed: 88.819% Cu]. Same two elements, two different ledgers, two different compounds — the numbers are the chemistry.

The rules, and how little there is to memorize. Oxidation states are bookkeeping charges assigned by four habits: (1) a free element is 0; (2) oxygen is −2 except in peroxides (Chapter 10's H₂O₂, where it is −1 — the peroxide bottle is the exception sitting in the medicine cabinet); (3) hydrogen is +1 with nonmetals; (4) the whole formula sums to 0 (or to the ion's charge). Everything else is algebra — and the algebra, run on the household shelf, is a verification engine:

  • Rust, FeOOH: x + (−2)(2) + (+1)(1) = 0 → Fe is +3 — the yellow-brown of rust is the ferric entry [computed]
  • Malachite, Cu₂(OH)₂CO₃ (Chapter 9's ore): 2x + (−2)(2) + (+1)(2) + (+4)(1) + (−2)(3) = 0 → 2x = +4 → Cu is +2 — the green of weathered bronze, and the arithmetic agrees with the color chemistry [computed]
  • Magnetite, Fe₃O₄: 3x − 8 = 0 → Fe averages +8/3 — not a fraction by accident: magnetite is one Fe(II) plus two Fe(III) per formula unit, the mixed-valence that Chapter 3's 72.36%-Fe pin already priced [computed; Ch 3 pin cross-checked]
  • The cobalt battery cathode, LiCoO₂ (N98): Li is +1, O is −2 → Co is +3 — and this single entry explains the whole leach: the performer's muriatic bath must reduce Co(III) to water-soluble Co(II), and something must absorb those electrons (the catch of Lab 11.2)
  • Tin's two chlorides (N99/N103): SnCl₂ has Sn at +2, SnCl₄ has it at +4 — the stannous/stannic pair, two oxidation states of one metal both purchasable, the corpus's tin chain living one lecture ahead of the theory

OIL RIG and the half-reaction. Oxidation is loss of electrons, reduction is gain — the mnemonics can be earned on the nail: iron in acid (Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻, oxidation) while protons collect the electrons (2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂, reduction). Write each half-reaction with its electrons and you can balance any redox equation by the same ledger habit the atom-balance gate uses in Lab 11.1: electrons on the books must cancel across the two halves, atoms must cancel within each. The method earns its keep at once on the chapter's hardest bench equation — N98's on-screen leach, which does not balance as printed and whose honest repair is Lab 11.2's catch.

The naming that follows the ledger. Ferrous/ferric, cuprous/cupric, stannous/stannic — the old names are oxidation states before the notation existed, and the household inherits them: the blue-green of Cu²⁺ solutions, the pale green of Fe²⁺, the yellow-brown of Fe³⁺, the pink-to-blue hydration theater of CoCl₂ (N98's own color datum: the blue concentrate vs the dilute "light purple pink" — the same oxidation state, water coordination only; the chapter's color language is mostly state language, occasionally hydration language, and the bench learns to say which).

Lecture 2 — The activity series, rebuilt from the kitchen sink

How you could have found it. Drop scrap metals into acid and watch. Zinc roofing strip (N76's sourcing: sold to prevent moss growth — the hardware store as reagent catalog, P1) fizzes hard in muriatic; iron fizzes slowly; copper pipes sit untouched, indifferently, for years. Magnesium (fire starters, water-heater anodes) reacts even with the kitchen's weak acid; aluminum foil seems dead in vinegar but explodes into hydrogen if you scratch it or add lye. Order the metals by fizz and you have built the activity series from scratch — every entry a purchase away, no table consulted. The series this builds [convergent — the standard table; the E° ledger of Lab 11.1 is its arithmetic]:

Li K Ca Na Mg Al | Zn Cr Fe Co Ni Sn Pb | H₂ | Cu Ag Hg Pt Au

(with hydrogen's position found by the acid test itself: everything left of H₂ releases it from acid, everything right of it does not — the series' own built-in reference point).

The electron bookkeeping under the order. The series is a ranking of willingness to be oxidized — a ranking the electrochemists measure as standard reduction potential, E° [convergent — CRC-class tables; values as tabulated]:

The reading rule is one subtraction: E°cell = E°(reduced side) − E°(donating side); positive means the electron flows that way on its own. Zinc over copper: 0.342 − (−0.763) = +1.105 V — the Daniell pair, spontaneous hard enough to be the classic battery (Chapter 12 will wind this exact subtraction into a cell). Iron over hydrogen: 0.000 − (−0.447) = +0.447 V — the nail fizz. Copper over hydrogen: −0.342 V — negative, never: copper does not liberate H₂ from non-oxidizing acids, which is why muriatic can live in a copper plumbing world and why N98's battery teardown anticipated "the copper foil surviving" the leach. Sort the metals by E° and you recover the fizz-order exactly — Lab 11.1 does this as a machine check (the E-sort is the series, 17 entries, monotone).

The three household paradoxes the series explains:

  1. The aluminum paradox. Al is a top-five active metal (−1.662 V) yet behaves as an inert one at the sink — because its own corrosion product seals it: Al₂O₃ forms in nanometers and stops the ledger. Break the film and the series reasserts itself: in alkali (drain opener), the amphoteric oxide dissolves and aluminum pays its full debt at once — 2 Al + 2 NaOH + 6 H₂O → 2 NaAl(OH)₄ + 3 H₂ (BALANCED), 1.362 L of H₂ per gram at RTP [computed], 3.63× the per-gram yield of zinc in acid (0.375 L/g [computed]) — which is precisely why drain openers ship aluminum shavings in the can. N76's zincate bath is the same amphoteric two-stage dissolve performed one row down the table (zinc's oxide also dissolves in lye, the video's precipitate-then-redissolve sequence).
  1. The penny paradox. The post-1982 cent is zinc wearing copper. File the edge, drop it in muriatic, and the costume rule of the series plays out: the Zn (−0.763 V) dissolves furiously, the Cu shell (−0.342 V relative to hydrogen) stands — the penny hollows into a copper ghost while fizzing 0.913 L of H₂ at RTP from its 2.4375 g zinc core [computed, both figures]. A two-metal object in acid is a demonstration of the series wearing a coin as its apparatus.
  1. The gain-weight paradox. Displace copper with iron — the nail into blue Root-Killer solution (Ch 9's CuSO₄ pin) — and the nail gains weight while dissolving: each mole of Fe (55.845 g) that leaves takes a mole of Cu (63.546 g) in exchange, a +7.701 g/mol gain, +13.79% on the iron consumed [computed]. The lesson is the ledger's finest: mass conservation never fails; only the element changes. The copper is heavier than the iron it displaces — the nail eats the solution's copper and grows. (The same arithmetic run with zinc: −1.834 g/mol, a loss — the series does not promise profit, only direction.)

Lecture 3 — Displacement: metal from salt, at room temperature

The activity series is a court: a metal higher on the list takes the compound of a metal lower on the list, and the lower metal walks free. The bench form is the most satisfying reaction in the household repertoire because the product separates itself:

Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu (BALANCED) — a clean nail in Root-Killer solution; the blue drains out of the water, the red-brown builds on the nail, and the green of Fe²⁺ replaces the blue of Cu²⁺ ion for ion. Everything in Chapter 9's solubility training applies (all parties soluble except the metal itself), but now you know why it runs: E°cell = +0.789 V [computed]. The same court, other metal pairs:

  • Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu (BALANCED): 1.105 V, the Daniell pair in a jam jar — this exact subtraction is what a battery is (Chapter 12's opening act, forward-flagged).
  • 2 Al + 3 CoCl₂ → 2 AlCl₃ + 3 Co (BALANCED): E°cell = +1.385 V [computed] — and this is N98's cobalt episode stated in equation form. The performer's own words are the best lecture this chapter gets: "the cobalt is much less reactive than aluminum... the cobalt should precipitate out" — the activity series predicted, then performed: a black powder of cobalt growing on aluminum foil in a battery-leach solution, 3.276 g of cobalt per gram of aluminum [computed], one of the corpus's cleanest P8 firings.
  • Cu + 2 AgNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2 Ag (BALANCED): 0.457 V — the silver tree, the classic demo; knowledge-only here (silver nitrate is not an OTC reagent; the geometry — fractal silver crystals growing on copper wire — is worth owning as recognition).
  • The alkali extreme: lithium displacing potassium or cesium from molten salts (N78/N105) — the same court, 3.040 V and beyond, and the molten condition is the honest footnote: in water the lithium would never get past its own reaction with the solvent (N100's cascade). The corpus's alkali-by-lithium chain is the series' top end performed where the solvent cannot interfere.

The per-gram ledger. Run the court on price and the series becomes a shopping list. Per gram of sacrificial metal, copper recovered at RTP: zinc 0.972 g, iron 1.138 g, magnesium 2.615 g, aluminum 3.533 g [computed, all]. Read with the E° table, the pattern is one rule: the more active the donor, the more copper per gram — activity is exactly the willingness to surrender electrons, and copper per gram is electrons per gram wearing a mass. But the cheapest electrons are not the bench's best: aluminum's electrons cost 8.994 g of metal per mole [computed] — the best rate in the cast — yet aluminum sits passivated at the sink, so the bench's workhorse remains zinc, whose electrons cost 32.690 g per mole but always show up on time. This trade — thermodynamic price versus kinetic availability — is the chapter's P10 lesson performed by the reagent table, and it returns in Lecture 5 as the galvanic-sacrifice ledger.

What displacement never does. The court runs one way. Put copper in FeSO₄ and nothing happens — the E° subtraction is −0.789 V, negative, the books refuse. The corpus's honest-uncertainty genre (N98's "is it cobalt metal or an oxide?" moment, resolved by the magnet test and the arc furnace) is the bench's own admission that recognition of the displaced metal is a separate skill from running the reaction: the black powder on the foil could be cobalt, could be oxide — the magnet and the melting behavior arbitrate (P9, the instrumentless-verification habit, Chapter 13's opening).

Lecture 4 — Corrosion: the slow fire, read as electrochemistry

The same court, now involuntary. Corrosion is displacement where the "salt" is the world itself and the donor metal never consented. The rusting nail is a battery whose electrodes are a centimeter apart and whose wiring is the metal itself. Write the two half-reactions of ordinary wet rusting [convergent, the electrochemical textbook mechanism]:

  • anode (the nail, giving): Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ (−0.447 V)
  • cathode (a droplet's edge, receiving): O₂ + 2 H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4 OH⁻ (+0.401 V [convergent — multiple standard tables])

E°cell = 0.401 − (−0.447) = +0.848 V [computed] — iron in wet air is a +0.848-volt cell that runs for years because its internal resistance is the rust layer itself. The Fe²⁺ meets the OH⁻ as Fe(OH)₂, air oxidizes it through Fe(OH)₃, and the final stable ledger entry is the oxyhydroxide: 4 Fe + 3 O₂ + 2 H₂O → 4 FeOOH (BALANCED). Rust as FeOOH — goethite and lepidocrocite, α- and γ-forms, the dominant crystalline products of atmospheric corrosion [convergent — mineralogical and corrosion-literature agreement; magnetite Fe₃O₄ and amorphous matter accompany them in real rust layers].

The rust ledger, priced. Per gram of iron consumed [computed]: oxygen demand 0.4297 g (the fire comparison is exact — combustion and corrosion are the same reaction at different speeds), water demand 0.1613 g, product mass 1.591 g — the nail gains 59.1%. A 10.00-g nail eaten fully makes 15.9 g of rust; Chapter 3's Fe₂O₃ 69.94%-Fe pin cross-checks the same arithmetic in oxide form. When a steel ship "wastes away," the ledger's honest form is that it gains weight while losing structure — corrosion's paradox mirroring the displacement paradox of Lecture 2.

Why rust grows where it does — the differential-aeration read. The cathode half needs oxygen, so it runs where the oxygen is: the droplet's rim, the waterline, the scratch in the paint. The anode is the oxygen-starved spot beside it. Rust therefore piles up around the oxygen entry point while the metal loss happens under the blanket — blistered paint and the classic waterline ring are the differential-aeration cell made visible. This one mechanism books the household's most expensive lessons: rust under paint blisters (the scratch was the cathode's door), rust inside pipes at the welded seam, rust under the washing-machine rim where a thin film holds oxygen against stagnant water. (The electrochemical formalism — its full cell notation and current language — belongs to Chapter 12; this chapter owns the mechanism's chemistry and its recognition.)

The corrosion styles of the household cast, each an oxidation state with a habitat:

  • Copper: Cu → Cu₂O (cuprite, the brown-black first tarnish) → in clean, CO₂-wet air the green carbonates malachite Cu₂(OH)₂CO₃ and azurite — Chapter 9's own compound, arriving as weather; in SO₂-bearing city air, brochantite, the basic copper sulfate that owns most historic green roofs [convergent — patina studies; the SO₂ dependence is the urban/rural color difference]. With vinegar instead of air, the same copper makes verdigris, the basic copper acetates — corrosion in kitchen conditions. Copper corrodes slowly and self-arrests; its corrosion product is its own paint.
  • Brass (N68's cartridge cases): the two-metal alloy means the zinc is an anode to the copper in every pore of the surface — acidified peroxide dissolves the copper (the green of Cu²⁺ rising off the case) and preferentially works the zinc; "dezincification" is the activity series running inside an alloy. The episode's green-then-blue solution and its careful uncoated-brass caveat are Lecture 4 and Lecture 5 in one fingerprinting method.
  • Zinc: white, powdery Zn(OH)₂/ZnCO₃ — the "white rust" of new galvanized sheet — which, like copper's products, seals. N76's hazard datum is the same chemistry at fan speed: zinc powder, with its surface multiplied a millionfold, oxidizes so fast the wet paste heats and whitens — corrosion with the heat unable to escape, "so fine that it is pyrophoric when dried." Corrosion is exothermic; usually the bill is collected slowly enough that no one feels it.
  • Aluminum: passivation again — Al₂O₃ in a film four nanometers thick, forming in milliseconds, transparent, hard, and chemically locked except against alkali (the drain-opener exception of Lecture 2) and a few notorious enemies (mercury salts, chloride-rich crevices — the knowledge-only rows of the hazard chapter).
  • Lithium (N100): the tarnish datum that doubles the lesson — lithium's surface goes dark in nitrogen, not just oxygen: 6 Li + N₂ → 2 Li₃N (the video's re-tarnish after the fresh-surface chop test). Corrosion is not "reacting with air"; it is reacting with the world, and the world includes N₂ — a lesson the alkalis teach and no other household metal repeats.

Lecture 5 — Arrest and exploitation: from galvanized nails to the copper summit

Three ways to stop a slow fire. The whole of corrosion engineering, and the whole of the household's defense, is three moves:

  1. The barrier — paint, oil, grease, wax, the tinning on a can: keep the electrolyte from the metal. Honest limitation: a barrier is a promise with a scratch width; the differential-aeration cell loves a scratched barrier, which is why blistered paint rusts worse than bare steel.
  2. The sacrifice — put a more active metal in electrical contact and let it corrode: galvanizing (zinc on steel — zinc is anodic to iron by 0.316 V [computed]), the magnesium (or aluminum, or zinc-aluminum) anode rod screwed into every tank water heater [convergent — the plumbing standard; the rod is more electrochemically active than the steel tank and corrodes in its place], the zinc roofing strip sacrificing itself into the rain (N76's moss-control sourcing, read here as electrochemistry). The sacrifice ledger prices it: protecting a mole of iron's-worth of steel costs a mole of zinc — 1.171 g Zn per g Fe-equivalent [computed]; magnesium costs less per mole of electrons (12.152 g per mol e⁻ vs zinc's 32.690 g [computed]) which is why the rod is magnesium and the coating is zinc — the best donor metal depends on whether you are buying grams, moles of electrons, or years, exactly the P10 edge-cost shape.
  3. The passivation — teach the metal to grow its own barrier: aluminum's film (Lecture 2), stainless steel's chromium oxide, copper's patina. The trick the household can watch: some corrosion products are paint, and choosing a metal is partly choosing what it corrodes into.

Corrosion as a reagent route. The Atlas's move: if the house corrodes anyway, aim it. The N68 etch is corrosion as forensic method (the green solution is copper acetate forming — a reagent being made by the method that also develops the print). The rusted shovel is not trash; it is an Fe₂O₃ supply: Fe₂O₃ + 6 HCl → 2 FeCl₃ + 3 H₂O (BALANCED) — 10.0 g of rust-scale with 37.5 mL of the Chapter 8 muriatic pin (10.0066 M) books 20.32 g of ferric chloride [computed], the etchant and printed-circuit reagent, made from the world's least loved compound (Chapter 15 will deepen the HCl side). Displacement likewise is not only demonstration; it is metal production — N98's cobalt came off aluminum foil at room temperature, no furnace, no arc: the quiet workhorse form of P8.

The summit: Chapter 9's ore, closed. Thompson's signature home-lab experiment is to smelt copper from self-made ore — and this book has now been carrying the ore since Chapter 9. The 0.401919-mol copper chain (the N58/N59 flagship, pinned session after session) ends here, two steps at kitchen scale:

  1. Calcine the malachite (P7, the Chapter 7 discipline): Cu₂(OH)₂CO₃ → 2 CuO + CO₂ + H₂O (BALANCED) — the green ore goes black; 44.435 g of the ore yields 31.971 g of CuO, exhaling 4.924 L of CO₂ at RTP and 3.620 g of water [computed, all]. The carbon dioxide is Chapter 10's gas, recognized on the way out.
  2. Reduce the oxide with hydrogen (Chapter 10's other gas returning as a reagent): CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O (BALANCED) — flowing hydrogen over the hot black powder turns it to warm reddish copper while the last bead of water exits the tube. The demand is 0.401919 mol of H₂ — 9.847 L at RTP [computed] — and the number is the same 9.847 liters Chapter 9 printed for its escaping CO₂, because both ledgers move one gas-mole per copper-mole at the same molar-volume pin. The cycle closes on a rhyme: what the carbonate run exhaled, the reduction run must now inhale. Yield: 25.540 g of copper — 57.47% of the ore, the malachite's copper assay kept honest through fire [computed; the 57.5% Ch 9 pin cross-checked].

The summit's route comparison is a P10 exercise performed on the real chain (Ex 11.5): displacing the same copper from solution costs 26.277 g of zinc — and generating the 9.847 L of hydrogen by dissolving that same copper-equivalent in acid would cost 26.277 g of zinc too [computed — one mole of 2-electron reductant either way; the electron ledger does not care which door the electrons leave through]. The difference is all edge cost: one pot at room temperature versus a calcination plus a gas-handling train. The zinc route pays in solution left behind (FeSO₄/ZnSO₄, recoverable); the hydrogen route pays in copper powder, purer and furnace-free.

The hard boundary, stated from the corpus. The series has a hot end, and the corpus performs it — pottery-pigment chromium oxide met aluminum powder (N79: 10 g Cr₂O₃ with 3.3 g Al — 7.1% under the stoichiometric aluminum [computed, Lab 11.2's check], and the reaction still needed an electric arc furnace to finish, the video's own honest-adequacy datum "not hot enough to appreciably melt the chromium"); vanadium pentoxide met aluminum (N81 — whose practitioner's-blog recipe ratio, 2.02:1 V₂O₅:Al by mass, Lab 11.2 reproduces exactly as the stoichiometric 2.022:1 [computed]); iron oxide met aluminum (the folk "3:1 thermite," honestly 2.959:1 [computed]). These are knowledge-only faces in this Atlas: thermite-class aluminothermics sit squarely on the energetics binding line, and Chapter 28 owns their hazard recognition. What this chapter takes from them is the arithmetic — the same electron ledger, now with adiabatic fire attached — and the humbling datum that even at 3000 °C, a 7% undercharge of aluminum leaves the job unfinished. The ledger forgives nothing.

11.1 ●○○ — The roof-strip corner. A 2.00-g corner of N76's zinc roofing strip goes into excess dilute muriatic. 11.2 ●○○ — The oxidation-state shelf. Assign the metal's oxidation state and name the evidence: (a) FeOOH in rust; (b) Cu in malachite Cu₂(OH)₂CO₃; (c) the average in Fe₃O₄, and why it is a fraction; (d) cobalt in LiCoO₂, and why the leach must reduce it. Hints: the four habits of Lecture 1; sum the formula to zero; the fraction is a weighted average. Answer: (a) +3 (x − 4 + 1 = 0); (b) +2 (2x − 4 + 2 + 4 − 6 = 0 → 2x = 4); (c) +8/3 — one Fe(II) + two Fe(III) per formula, the mixed valence behind Ch 3's 72.36% pin; (d) +3 (Li +1, O −2) — and Co(III)'s oxides do not simply dissolve as Co(II) chloride without an electron sink: the gate that balances N98's equation (Lab 11.2's Cl₂ catch). Ways: the algebraic ledger; the color/chemistry cross-check (malachite's green is the Cu²⁺ chromophore; rust's yellow-brown the Fe³⁺ — the state and the hue agreeing).

11.6 ◆ — The green-then-blue case. An unfired brass cartridge case sits 15 minutes in a freshly mixed 10:7 bath of 3% peroxide and white vinegar (N68's exact recipe). The solution goes green near the case within 10 minutes, then bluer as it concentrates; the case emerges mottled with "very nice ridges" developed on its surface; the same bath on a fired case develops nothing readable; on a coated brass fitting it does nothing at all. Name the chemistry, the pattern, and the method's honest limits. (No hints — this is the pattern-hunt.) Answer: corrosion run as a verification method — P9 riding on P8, with P1's two-item sourcing and P10's fallback chain. The chemistry: the peroxide is the oxidant booster (E° for H₂O₂/H₂O sits high above copper's couple — the etch runs where plain vinegar would take days), the vinegar supplies acid and the acetate that carries copper away as the green Cu²⁺ — verdigris forming in real time, the Lecture 4 copper face; the brass's zinc works as the internal anode (dezincification assisting the dissolve). The pattern: an instrumentless verification — the latent fingerprint's ridges etch differentially, the surface itself becoming both reagent and readout (P9), on the displacement/corrosion chemistry this chapter built (P8's quiet face — the metal is being displaced out of its solid by an oxidant solution). The honest limits, all corpus-sourced: fired cases destroy the fragile prints before chemistry ever gets a turn (the reality datum — the method's real forensic use is the unfired round the criminal forgot); coated brass never lets the bath reach metal (Lecture 5's barrier logic — hence the superglue fallback, P10's method chain); and the fresh-mix discipline (stability datum) is the recipe's own shelf-life lesson. The diagnosis: the green-then-blue is the hydration theater of Chapter 9's copper(II) — recognition, displacement, and corrosion in one drugstore bath.
