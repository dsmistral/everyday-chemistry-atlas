Vol IV · Chapter 30 — Glass, Ceramics, and Lime: The Oxide Currency of the Fired World

Lecture 0 — The Materials Shelf (the world priced in oxides)

Volume IV opens at a different shelf. Volumes I–III were about substances you can pour: solutions, gases, precipitates, currents. This chapter is about the substances you cannot pour at all — the window, the jar, the mug, the tile, the brick, the driveway — and the discovery is that they are the same chemistry wearing its most permanent clothes. The window is sand whose network was melted and frozen too fast to crystallize. The mug is clay that gave up its water and re-formed its lattice. The driveway is a rock the house fired for itself, one tonne at a time, at a temperature no kitchen will ever see. And the whole of it — all three trades, glass and ceramics and lime — keeps its books in a single currency this chapter names up front: the oxide currency. Every batch material on the materials shelf is an oxide or an oxide wearing a CO₂ coat: sand is SiO₂; soda ash is Na₂O wearing a CO₂ coat (Na₂CO₃ = Na₂O + CO₂, exact, and the furnace is where the coat comes off); limestone is CaO wearing the same coat (CaCO₃ = CaO + CO₂, exact); the clay is Al₂O₃ and SiO₂ wearing a water coat. Chapter 14 taught the coat trick for one stone; this chapter runs the whole shelf on it. The premise, in one line: the fired world is a ledger of oxides, and the ledger closes to the microgram. [the composition bands convergent: the overview class prints 70–75 wt% SiO₂ / 12–16 wt% Na₂O / 10–15 wt% CaO for soda-lime glass; the datasheet class prints 69–74 / 12–16 / 5–12 / 0–6 MgO / 0–3 Al₂O₃ — both classes cited, both honest.]

The second half of the premise is the discipline, inherited from every chapter before this one: pricing the fired thing is safer than firing it. This chapter runs zero benches — no glass is melted, no clay is fired, no kiln is lit, no quicklime is slaked on any page of it. Its labs are ledgers: the batch arithmetic of a hundred grams of glass, the steam arithmetic of a mug, the carbon arithmetic of a tonne of clinker, every number closed by the engine before the page prints it. The household's role here is the reader's oldest one — recognition: read the window, the label on the bowl, the gray powder in the garden shed, and know what chemistry each one is. Nothing here is a procedure, and the one thing this chapter must never let the reader walk away with is the idea that its temperatures are suggestions. They are not; they are the kiln's own, and the kiln is not in this book.

Lecture 1 — Glass, the Amorphous Rock

How you could have found this. Take the hardest thing on the materials shelf — the window — and ask the book's oldest question: what is it made of? The answer is the materials chapter's first surprise: mostly the same sand as the beach. Silica, SiO₂, is one of the great network-formers: every silicon holds four oxygens, every oxygen bridges two silicons, and the result is a continuous three-dimensional framework that is very hard to pull apart — which is why pure silica glass melts only around 1715 °C [convergent band; the reference class prints 1713–1725], a temperature no household and few industries want to pay. Glass itself is that network interrupted: melt the sand with fluxes, and the melt freezes on cooling before the atoms can find their way back to a crystal — an amorphous solid, a liquid's disordered network with a rock's rigidity. That is the whole trade: the glassmaker is a chemist who breaks a network just enough to work it, then freezes the working.

The ladder, not the cliff. A crystal melts at a point; a glass softens along a ladder, and the ladder's rungs are the industry's own names [convergent bands, the reference class]: the strain point (~500–515 °C for soda-lime; the viscosity anchor 10^14.5 poise), below which internal stresses are frozen in for good; the annealing point (~540–550 °C), where the network relaxes and stresses anneal out in minutes; and the softening point (~730 °C), where the sheet will slump under its own weight. Every jar that leaves the factory has been annealed — held at the annealing point and cooled slowly — because unannealed glass carries frozen-in stress that will find its own weak day. The same ladder, run at the maker's pace, is the whole of the tempered-glass trick in Lecture 3.

Why soda, and why lime. Pure silica's network is too strong to work cheaply, so the recipe breaks it deliberately. Soda, Na₂O, donates oxygens that cannot bridge — non-bridging oxygens — and the network comes apart at those points: softening drops by nearly a thousand degrees. But soda alone is a mistake the trade made once and wrote down: a soda-silica glass's network is so broken that water attacks it — the soda's own oxide drinks (E13: Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH, 79.994 g/mol exact, gated), and a glass that drinks weather fails it. The trade's answer was already on Ch 14's shelf: lime, CaO, which bridges through the broken spots and stitches the network back to durability. The canonical pairing is exactly the one Ch 14 forward-flagged to this chapter: the glass triangle — sand, soda, lime. And the batch arithmetic of that triangle is Lecture 2's whole subject: the oxide currency, run at the furnace, closing to the microgram.

Lecture 2 — The Recipe (sand, soda, and lime — and the waterglass lesson)

The batch, priced. The canon's rounded weight-percent bands for soda-lime — the glass of windows, bottles, and jars, "the most prevalent type" [convergent] — close to a teaching batch of exactly 100.0000 g: 73 g SiO₂, 15 g Na₂O, 7 g CaO, 4 g MgO, 1 g Al₂O₃ [within the datasheet-class bands; the overview class prints wider CaO bands — both classes cited, the recipe's linearity flagged in Lecture 5]. Now run the oxide currency: the sand is the SiO₂ (73 g of it); the soda arrives as soda ash, Na₂CO₃, which is Na₂O wearing a CO₂ coat — so 15 g of Na₂O costs 15 × (105.988/61.979) = 25.6509 g of soda ash, and the coat, 10.6509 g of CO₂, leaves at the furnace (E1: Na₂CO₃ + SiO₂ → Na₂SiO₃ + CO₂, 166.071 g/mol, gated exact); the lime arrives as limestone, CaCO₃ — 7 g of CaO costs 7 × (100.086/56.077) = 12.4936 g of limestone, its coat 5.4936 g of CO₂ leaving through E2. The stabilizers (4 g MgO, 1 g Al₂O₃) ride in already oxidized — in a real batch they ride in as dolomite and feldspar impurities; the ledger credits them directly, and the dolomite route pays its own freight in B4. The whole batch: 116.1445 g in = 100.0000 g of glass + 16.1445 g of CO₂ out. The ledger B1 closes to the fourth decimal, and it teaches the quietest fact in the materials trade: the glassmaker is a carbon accountant whether or not the bill is printed. [all computed at the engine; the composition bands convergent.]

The waterglass lesson — the mistake priced. What if the trade had stopped at soda? Then E1's product would be the whole story: sodium silicate, Na₂SiO₃, 122.062 g/mol exact — and that substance has a household name: waterglass, "a glassy solid soluble in water" [the reference canon's own phrase], the classic adhesive and detergent-builder of the pre-polymer world. Soluble is the diagnosis: a soda-silica network is so depolymerized that water can take it apart (E13's thirst, worn again); the lime is what makes glass insoluble, weather-proof, a rock. The same substance that fails as window glass succeeds as waterglass — and Lecture 5's successor chapters will meet it again in the detergent aisle, where its solubility is the whole point (Ch 33's flag, raised). The lesson in one line: the property is the network, and the network is the recipe. [convergent; E4's arithmetic engine-gated.]

Lecture 3 — Thermal Shock and the Label on the Bowl

Why glass cracks when the stove is cruel. Heat one face of a pane and it expands while the far face does not: the mismatch is a stress, σ = EαΔT/(1−ν), and glass has no mercy past its skin strength. The first-order shock index, run at typical engineering values (all constants printed in B2 — the model is an upper bound, edge flaws unmodeled, and its absolute K are stated, not gated against the canon bands), prices the whole trade in three numbers: soda-lime's safe shock ΔT ≈ 64.8 K at the model (canon band ~55), borosilicate's ≈ 192.4 K (canon ~166), and the ratio 2.97 ≈ the canon's own "~3×" [the lab-supply class's own selling point: borosilicate 3.3's expansion, 3.3×10⁻⁶/K, is roughly one-third of soda-lime's]. The kettle pour — boiling water into a 20 °C vessel, ΔT = 80 K — stresses soda-lime to 61.7 MPa, over its ~50-MPa skin: it cracks; borosilicate to 20.8 MPa, comfortably under: it survives. That one pour is why the laboratory bowl and the kitchen bowl are different objects. [computed at the engine from stated constants; the canon bands convergent.]

The history that put a German invention in every kitchen. Borosilicate glass was first made by Otto Schott — the glass technologist who systematically mapped composition to property [the history canon]; the modern laboratory grade is the defined borosilicate 3.3 of DIN ISO 3585, α = 3.3×10⁻⁶/K, softening ~820 °C, the trade names (DURAN and its kin) all the same recipe [convergent]. Pyrex was developed at Corning in 1908 and introduced to the public in 1915 [the Pyrex-history canon, convergent] — and for most of a century "Pyrex" meant borosilicate in the kitchen. Then the label changed under the name: starting around the 1950s, plant by plant, the US line moved to tempered soda-lime [the Pyrex-history canon and the maker's own confirmation, convergent — 1998 belongs to the later brand era, not the glass] — cheaper to form, stronger against drops (4–6× annealed [convergent]), and on paper a ~170-K shock class — B2's 194.4 K, three times annealed's 64.8 K — yet the magazine's own head-to-head (the quote below) still broke the tempered dishes where the borosilicate ones held: drop strength and shock tolerance are different tests. The consumer press caught the gap in testing: in the magazine's own lab, "None of the European dishes made of borosilicate broke, except one practice-run Arcuisine Elegance dish that had been through two baking cycles in our lab." [the consumer-press record, quoting the magazine's lab, printed as found]. The lesson is the chapter's cleanest recognition skill: read the label, know the glass. A US "Pyrex" measuring cup is tempered soda-lime — it survives the drop but not the burner; the European one is borosilicate — the burner but not the drop. Neither is a lie; they are two engineering choices, and the label is the only way to tell them apart.

Tempering: strength you cannot cut. Tempered glass is the annealing ladder run backward and frozen: heat the pane to ~620 °C [single-source, flagged] and quench the surfaces with air; the skin freezes first, the core freezes later and shrinks, and the finished pane carries ~100 MPa of permanent surface compression over a tensile core — the compression must be overcome before any crack can open, which is the 4–6× strength and most of the shock resistance. The price is the pane's own character: it cannot be cut, drilled, or edged after tempering — the first crack reaches the tensile core and the whole pane dices. The reader meets it everywhere (shower doors, car windows, phone faces) and the recognition is one line: a break into dices is a tempered break, and a chip is a future failure. [convergent.]

Lecture 4 — Clay and the Kiln

The second amorphous trade. Clay is the fired world's second network story, and it begins as a mineral with the chapter's longest formula: kaolinite, Al₂Si₂O₅(OH)₄, 258.157 g/mol — the book's largest single new species, priced in the battery and folded at the engine (E7: Al₂Si₂O₅(OH)₄ → Al₂O₃ + 2SiO₂ + 2H₂O, 258.157 g/mol both sides, gated exact). Heat clay through its dehydroxylation window — roughly 450–700 °C across the research literature [the research class's band; the pure-phase sources print 500–550, and the ceramics-teaching class extends the practical kiln window to 500–800, the band the engine's E7 note and B3 header wear] — and the hydroxyls pair off and leave as steam: 13.96% of the kaolinite's own mass (2×18.015/258.157, engine-exact), which is why a 500-g green mug — priced throughout as pure kaolinite, a stated basis; real bodies carry non-clay fillers that lose proportionally less — weighs 430.22 g out of its first firing, 69.78 g of it gone as water through the kiln's chimney (B3). What remains is metakaolin, Al₂Si₂O₇, amorphous — a network scrambled by its own departure — and on further firing it re-organizes: above ~950–1100 °C the crystalline silicates form, chief among them mullite, the needle-crystal skeleton that gives porcelain its strength [the research canon, convergent]; at porcelain temperatures the leftover amorphous silica begins to crystallize to cristobalite ["By 1300 °C, cristobalite begins to form from the amorphous Si-rich phases" — the ceramics literature's own line, printed as found].

The 573-degree surprise. Inside nearly every clay body sits free quartz sand, and quartz has a secret the kiln must respect: at 573 °C the α-form inverts to the β-form in a reversible instant, changing volume abruptly as it does [convergent, the ceramics canon's own number]. In a kiln with any thermal gradient, the inversion's volume step does not happen everywhere at once — the ceramics reference's own statement, printed as found: "if one side of a piece is at 573 and the other at 600, then as the piece cools further the volume change will move horizontally across it and could start a crack at the first weakness it encounters." Every firing schedule in ceramics slows down through 573 °C, up and down, both passes — the trade's oldest piece of thermal hygiene, and the ◆ exercise's whole diagnosis.

The three firing classes, recognition-only. The kitchen's fired things sort by temperature, and the chapter reads them without ever firing them [convergent bands, the cone-chart class]: earthenware matures at cone 04–06 (~1000–1060 °C) — the mug's porous, colored body; stoneware at the cone 2–12 band's middle (~1150–1250 °C) — vitrified, non-porous, the dinner-plate class; porcelain at cone 8–13 (~1250–1300 °C and above) — the whitest, hardest, most glassy body of all, fired where mullite and cristobalite finish their work. The numbers are the kiln's own, stated here as knowledge only: the chapter's rule stands — the reader reads clay, never fires it.

Lecture 5 — Cement, the Fired Rock (and the mortar that pays its carbon back)

The rock the house fires for itself. Concrete's binder is the most-made manufactured substance on Earth, and it is a material this chapter can price end to end because its recipe is the oxide currency at industrial scale. Limestone and clay are ground, and the kiln — at ~1450 °C, sintering without melting [the cement canon's own phrasing] — folds them into clinker, the marble-sized nodules whose minerals the trade names in its own shorthand (cement-chemist notation: C = CaO, S = SiO₂, A = Al₂O₃, F = Fe₂O₃, H = H₂O [convergent]): alite, C₃S (Ca₃SiO₅, 228.314 g/mol, 50–85% of the clinker [convergent]), the early-strength workhorse; belite, C₂S (Ca₂SiO₄, 172.237, 15–30%), the late-strength slow set [the clinker canon, convergent]; and the two minority phases that need watching — the aluminate C₃A so reactive that the grinder must add gypsum, <5 wt%, "to avoid the flash setting of the tricalcium aluminate" [the clinker reference's own line, printed as found].

The hydration ledger. Add water and the clinker's minerals pay out: alite hydrates in the trade's own shorthand 2C₃S + 6H → C₃S₂H₃ + 3CH — and that shorthand is the chapter's engine-gated E9: 2Ca₃SiO₅ + 6H₂O → Ca₃Si₂O₇·3H₂O + 3Ca(OH)₂, 564.718 g/mol both sides, exact. The products are cement's two gifts: calcium silicate hydrate — C-S-H — the glue, and portlandite, Ca(OH)₂ — the alkalinity that later feeds the carbonation ledger. Priced per 100 g of alite (B5): water 23.6713 g in; C-S-H 74.9936 g + portlandite 48.6777 g out; closure 123.6713, exact. Belite's own row (E10, 416.534 g/mol gated) gives the slow set its arithmetic: per 100 g, water 20.9189 g in; 99.4101 g + 21.5087 g out; closure 120.9189. One honesty stands over both rows, and it is the chapter's first flag: the C-S-H of the ledger is a stated proxy — real C-S-H has variable Ca:Si and water content; the model composition is bookkeeping, and the book keeps it labeled as such. [the notation and phase shares convergent; every closure computed at the engine.]

The carbon ledger — the biggest number in the chapter. The clinker's stone comes from limestone, and limestone wears the CO₂ coat: per the USGS's own accounting, "Calcination accounts for almost two-thirds of the total CO2 emissions from cement production. The other one-third comes from the energy used to heat the kiln." [USGS's own feature, printed as found] — and cement as a whole is, after fossil fuels and land-use change, the third-largest source of anthropogenic CO₂ [the emissions canon, convergent]. The engine prices the stone's own share (B6): a tonne of clinker carrying ~650 kg of CaO [convergent band, the recipe's linearity flagged] required 1,160.1 kg of limestone and released 510.1 kg of CO₂ at calcination alone — 0.7848 kg per kg of CaO, reconciling Ch 14's own 785-kg-per-tonne quicklime pin at 0.1% (the same stone, the same coat, the same arithmetic; the pin cross-check that closes the chapter's longest inheritance). Add the fuel side's canon band (~260–300 kg more) and the tonne of clinker carries ~790 kg of CO₂ against the canon's ~800 band.

And the mortar pays it back — partly. Here is the materials chapter's most beautiful ledger, and it is lime's, not cement's. Slaked lime sets by carbonation: Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O (E11, 118.101 g/mol, gated) — the mortar pulls the very CO₂ out of the air that its making released, returning the stone to stone [the lime-mortar canon: non-hydraulic lime "sets by carbonation and so needs exposure to carbon dioxide in the air"; lime "re-absorbs the carbon dioxide" emitted in its production — convergent]. The engine prices the payback exactly: 0.5940 kg of CO₂ returned per kg of slaked lime (44.009/74.092, exact) — a 500-g mug's-worth of honesty about limits: the payback is real and partial, the fuel side of the ledger never comes home (burned carbon stays burned; carbonation has no line for the kiln's fire), and the chapter states both halves. Add a pozzolan — powdered brick, volcanic ash, the Roman trade's own addition — and the mortar also sets by hydration and gains compressive strength [the lime-mortar canon]; the recognition is the history: the Pantheon's dome is pozzolanic lime concrete, standing because its mortar both hydrated and carbonated, and its stone's carbon came home over two thousand years.

And the recognition the chapter leaves the reader is the volume's own: the fired world was never dead matter — it is the wet chemistry's most patient ledger, sand and soda and lime and clay closing their books at 1450 °C and cooling, crack by crack or crystal by crystal, into the shapes the house lives with.

30.1 ●○○ — Half the batch. (a) Scale the B1 batch to 50 g of glass. (b) Why does the CO₂ halve exactly? (c) Why do the stabilizers (MgO, Al₂O₃) ride into the ledger "already oxidized"? (c) In a real batch they arrive as dolomite and feldspar impurities; the ledger credits the oxides directly and B4 prices the dolomite's own freight honestly (+4.3677 g of CO₂ per 100 g of glass).

30.2 ●○○ — The slaking of a hundred grams. (a) How much water does 100 g of quicklime take, at the pins? (b) What mass of slaked lime results? (c) At Ch 14's ΔH band, how much heat comes out? Hints: E6; B1's arithmetic; Ch 14's −64.8 kJ/mol. Answer: (a) 100 × (18.015/56.077) = 32.1255 g of water. (b) 132.1255 g of Ca(OH)₂ — closure exact (100 + 32.1255 = 132.1255). (c) 100/56.077 = 1.7833 mol × 64.8 kJ/mol = 115.6 kJ — the exotherm that makes slaking a scale-discipline line, never a household bench.

30.3 ●●○ — The mug's steam. (a) A 500-g green mug: what leaves, and what remains? (b) Why does the body survive the loss structurally? (c) What happens if the kiln is pushed too fast through the steam's exit window? Hints: B3; E7; Lecture 4. Answer: (a) 69.78 g of water (13.96% of the kaolinite fraction — the mug priced as pure kaolinite, the stated basis) leaves as steam; 430.22 g of metakaolin body remains. (b) Metakaolin is amorphous — the lattice's order was lost with the water, and the network re-organizes on further firing into mullite's skeleton; the mug is not 430 g of powder because the body's particles are welded where they touch. (c) Steam trapped in a fast-heating dense body spalls it — cracks, blowouts, the kiln's own loss column; slow ramps are the cure, and the ◆ exercise's cousin.

30.4 ●●○ — The mortar's payback. (a) 10 kg of slaked lime in mortar: how much CO₂ comes home, and how much stone is returned to stone? (b) Why does the fuel-side CO₂ never come home? (c) Why did the Roman trade add pozzolan to its lime? Hints: E11; B6; Lecture 5's ledger. Answer: (a) 10 × (44,009/74,092) = 5.9398 kg of CO₂ re-absorbed (the factor's 4-dp view: 0.5940); 15.9398 kg of CaCO₃ — the stone's mass, come home. (b) Carbonation only runs the calcination arrow backward (E11 is E5 reversed with the lime slaked); the kiln's fire is a one-way street — burned carbon stays burned, and the payback is partial by chemistry, not by neglect. (c) Pozzolan makes the mortar set by hydration as well as carbonation — gaining compressive strength and setting even underwater; the Pantheon's dome is the standing proof.
