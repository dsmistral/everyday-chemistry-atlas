Vol II · Chapter 17 — Sulfur and Its Compounds: The Whole Oxidation Ladder on One Shelf

Lecture 0 — The shelf that holds an entire element, read before anything is burned

Chapter 15 climbed the strong-acid ladder to sulfuric acid and found it at the top, already bought; Chapter 16 walked the oxidizer shelf and priced the electron demand the world sells by the bottle. This chapter walks to the one element whose entire chemistry is sitting in an ordinary house, at every oxidation state at once — the −2 face in the drain's rumor, the 0 face in the garden dust, the +4 face on the wine shelf, and the +6 face in the medicine cabinet and the wall itself — because sulfur is the only element the household sells as a ladder, and the bench that learns to read the ladder learns the whole discipline of oxidation state in one aisle. The thesis, and it is the volume's hinge stated a third time: an oxidation state is a price tag — the element's −2 and 0 faces are fuels and poisons the Charter benches, its +4 face is the bench's workhorse reductant and gas-generation workhorse, and its +6 face is the safe storage state the whole shelf is quietly trying to become — and the discipline this chapter teaches is reading which state a label holds, stepping the element UP the ladder honestly (the +4-to-+6 climb the volume's economy prices), and never once stepping it down into the faces the book refuses to make.

Sit at the kitchen table with the notebook and pour nothing. The pencil-and-paper census, before any apparatus:

  • The garden shelf — dusting sulfur and wettable sulfur at the ~90% class, and the winery's sulfur wick or sulfur candle, the traditional fumigant burned inside a dry barrel (the practice's own rule, stated in its own literature: never in a wet barrel [convergent] — the wick is Lecture 3's burner door run by tradition, and the exit gas is the +4 face).
  • The wine shelf — Campden tablets (the 0.44 g class, metabisulfite plus binder [convergent]; both the potassium and the sodium formulation are sold under the same name — a label convention that hides the assay twice), and the bags of potassium or sodium metabisulfite behind the home-brew counter, labeled in the preservative trade's own currency: % available SO₂.
  • The aquarium shelf — the dechlorinator bottle, whose crystals are sodium thiosulfate pentahydrate, the fixer's own salt sold for the price of fish care, with a dose table where a label's assay should be.
  • The mineral shelf — Epsom salt (USP magnesium sulfate heptahydrate, the medicine cabinet's +6 face), the drywall itself (gypsum, calcium sulfate dihydrate — the house is built partly of the +6 state), the alum block of Chapter 1's flagship bookkeeping, and the root-killer jug of copper sulfate pentahydrate.
  • The matchbook — recognition only, and it is the binding line's own object: the match head is sulfur plus the oxidizer Chapter 16 benched, the world's oldest and smallest demonstration of the rule this chapter now states from the fuel's side.

That is five faces and four shelves, and — the census's quiet observation — every one of the solids is closer to the +6 state than the label says it is: the metabisulfite bag oxidizes in its own package toward sulfate, the sulfite solutions of the trade decay in their bottles, and the Epsom and the gypsum have already finished the climb and will keep for a century. The element's shelf-life is its oxidation state. The chapter's five questions, in bench order: what does each label's number mean (the assay conventions of the preservative and pet trades), which state does the meter say I hold (the ladder at the E° bench), how does the +4 face get made safely (the two generation doors and the gas train), what does the +4 bench do (sulfites, thiosulfates, and the iodometric titrations), and how does the element climb to +6 honestly, and then keep (the three-oxidant ladder, the honest 2%, and the keepers' tray rules)?

One boundary before any apparatus is unpacked, and it is the book's binding line for this entire chapter, inherited from Chapter 16's oxidizer line and the durable energetics resolution: the element and its reduced faces are benched, and the gases are the line's own objects — sulfur is the world's classic fuel and never meets the oxidizer tray (no composition, no mixture, no ignition recipe appears anywhere in this book; the match head is printed as recognition knowledge through Chapter 28's lens, and the dust the same); the +4 face is a toxic gas and is generated only at match scale, into a train, with the absorption bottle as the system's own end (generate in situ and consume in situ — pattern P13's founding discipline, never a cylinder of stored SO₂); and the −2 face, hydrogen sulfide, is recognition knowledge only — this book prints its chemistry and its equations, schedules no procedure that makes it, and stores nothing that could. The chapter prints the arithmetic of the whole ladder because a complete book prices its doors; it opens none of the forbidden ones.

Lecture 1 — The sulfur census: labels written by three different trades

How you could have found it. Buy one item from each shelf, read the label, and try to answer the only question that matters at the bench: how many moles am I holding, and in which oxidation state? The sulfur shelf answers in four mutually incompatible conventions, none of them written for a chemist.

Convention one: plain weight percent of the element. The garden shelf is honest almost by accident — dusting sulfur is sold as sulfur, ~90% with the remainder clay and conditioning agents, and flowers of sulfur at the 99% class. The census notes the quiet fact that the most honest label in the house belongs to the most hazardous shelf-mate: pure fuel, plainly labeled.

Convention two: available SO₂. The wine shelf sells metabisulfite by the mass of SO₂ the salt can deliver — the preservative trade's own oxidizing-equivalent convention, and the exact structural twin of the pool shelf's available chlorine of Chapter 16: a number in units of a gas, attached to a salt, meaning "this much sulfur dioxide per gram of product." The arithmetic is one honest division (Lab 17.1, Run 5): the sodium salt carries 2×64.058/190.095 = 67.40% available SO₂, the potassium salt 2×64.058/222.311 = 57.63% — the wine literature's "67" and "58" classes — and the trade's preference for the potassium salt is a solubility and hygroscopicity choice, not an assay one: the lighter sodium cation buys more gas per gram.

Convention three: the dose table. The Campden tablet and the aquarium dechlorinator both hide their assay behind a use: one tablet per gallon of must, so many drops per tank — a label convention that assumes you will never ask how many grams of what. The census's translation (Run 5's Campden coin): one 0.44 g tablet of the sodium class carries 0.2965 g of available SO₂. The aquarium bottle's crystals are the fixer's own pentahydrate, and the dose table is the only assay the label will ever print.

Convention four: the mineral shelf's hydration water. Epsom salt is sold as MgSO₄·7H₂O (USP, 246.466 g/mol — the Chapter 4 pin), gypsum as CaSO₄·2H₂O (172.164, Chapter 9's mineral), the alum block as KAl(SO₄)₂·12H₂O (474.372 — Chapter 1's flagship, the session-28 corrected value, re-pinned in this chapter's own engine), and the root killer as CuSO₄·5H₂O (249.677, five chapters' pin class). The +6 shelf is the book's hydrate bestiary: the water of crystallization is the assay, and the census's rule is Chapter 4's — weigh, don't scoop.

The census itself, in the bench's currency. The solids stay wt% (no honest way to give the garden dust a molarity); the made solutions are stated in molarity where they are made to volume, and in molality where no density exists — and this chapter deliberately contains no density interpolations: Chapter 16's ρ seam taught the lesson, and the sulfur bench's pots (gas absorbed in water) are priced on the mass basis throughout, a convention the lab blocks state where it matters (the saturated rung: SO₂ at 11.6 g per 100 g of water at 20 °C [convergent, with the rung's temperature spread 23.0 at 0 °C and 5.9 at 40 °C] computes to 1.811 mol/kg — the honest ceiling every pot in this chapter runs far below).

Three census lessons, each load-bearing. First, the whole ladder is on the shelf at once. The same element sits at −2 (nowhere safely, only the drain's chemistry), 0 (the garden dust), +4 (the wine and aquarium shelves), and +6 (the medicine cabinet and the wall) — and the shelf's danger ranking, its reactivity ranking, and its shelf-life ranking are the same list read in opposite directions: the most reduced face is the most reactive and the worst keeper; the sulfate minerals are inert and near-eternal. Second, the +4 shelf is already spending itself. Metabisulfite oxidizes in the bag, sulfite solutions decay in the bottle (Lecture 5's air clock), and the assay discipline of Chapters 15/16 transfers whole: the dated titration (Lecture 4's iodometry) is the only sentence that knows what the wine shelf holds today. Third, the element's ladder is the network's spine. The garden dust is the burner door's feed; the wine shelf is the SO₂ door and the sulfite bench; the aquarium bottle is the iodometric titrant the analytical chapters will standardize against; and the +6 climb ends at the acid Chapter 15 already priced — the sulfur bench is the volume's own reagent network, walked node by node.

Read the table the way the census ranked the shelf, and the ladder appears in the numbers themselves. Every household oxidizer sits above the +4 couple — air at +1.059 V of spread, iodine at +0.365, chlorine at +1.188, hypochlorous acid at +1.312, peroxide at +1.606, the oxone bucket's peroxymonosulfate at +1.65 — which is why sulfur dioxide is the classic reducing agent: the +4 state is an electron bank every oxidizer in the house can draw on, and the ΔG prices (Lab 17.1, Run 4) run from −87.8 kJ (the gentle iodometric draw) to −318.4 kJ (the Caro-couple draw) per couple. The −2 couple sits even lower (+0.14, and the −2-to-+4 spread is barely +0.03 V — the mutual-destruction recognition datum of Lecture 5), which is why sulfide is the shelf's most generous donor and its most dangerous: the same electron generosity that makes the +4 face a useful reductant makes the −2 face a poison and a fuel. And the fixer couple (+0.08) is the iodometric titration's own arithmetic — thiosulfate's two inequivalent sulfurs (average oxidation state +2; the tetrathionate product averages +2.5) donate at a gentle, stoichiometric rate, which is precisely what a titrant's reductant must do: fast enough to complete, slow enough to be counted.

The lecture's one idea, and it deserves its own paragraph. An element's chemistry is not one substance but a spread of prices, and the meter reads the spread, not the element. Sulf dioxide at +4 is a reductant against the whole oxidizer shelf; the same element at −2 is a reductant against everything including the +4 face; at +6 it is spent — the sulfate ion sits at the bottom of the sulfur bench's own reactivity and is the safest thing on the shelf. This is Chapter 16's "roles, assigned by the subtraction," stated one level up: not only does a reagent's role flip with its partner (Chapter 16's lesson); an element's role flips with its own oxidation state, and the shelf stores the element by choosing the state. The wine shelf stores sulfur at +4 because that is the highest state that still does something (antioxidant, antimicrobial — an electron donor by design, dosed in milligrams); the mineral shelf stores it at +6 because that is the state that does nothing; and the winery burns the element in a dry barrel precisely because the 0 state is the one that can be made on demand into the +4 face, by nothing but air and a match.

Lecture 3 — The two doors of the +4 face: generating SO₂ the honest way

The +4 face is a gas — sulfur dioxide boils at −10 °C [convergent] and never becomes a liquid on the household bench — so the question is never what to store, only how to generate, and the ruined world sells the answer twice, by two different patterns. The gas itself is the strictest reagent this volume has scheduled: toxic (the suffocating, choking recognition class — the smell is its own warning label at low concentration and a false friend at high), heavier than air at 2.618 g/L (Run 5 — the Chapter 10 lesson applies: it pours down-ramp and collects in low places, which is the safety architecture of the train, not a footnote), and aggressively soluble — the absorption bottle that ends the train is not an option but the system's own terminus. The Charter's rule governs the whole lecture: fume-generating steps outdoors or with improvised ventilation, at match scale, into a train whose last bottle is the sink.

Door one: the wine shelf's salt and the acid jug (pattern P13's founding face, N01). The metabisulfite the preservative trade sells is the gas in mineral form: add acid and the anion's equilibrium walks straight out of solution — Na₂S₂O₅ + 2 HCl → 2 NaCl + H₂O + 2 SO₂ (BALANCED) — a gas generated in the pot, from solids, never handled as a bulk cylinder, which is exactly the discipline pattern P13 was promoted to teach. This is the corpus's N01 (NurdRage, Make Sulfuric Acid (metabisulfite/oxidizer method), 2009-06-28 — P13's dissolved-gas founding face, pinned in Chapter 15): the metabisulfite plus muriatic acid liberate SO₂ in situ, and the same pot then oxidizes the dissolved gas with the drugstore peroxide bottle — the generation and the consumption never separated by more than a stir bar. The ledger prices the door exactly (Lab 17.2's B1): 10.0 g of the sodium salt carries 0.10521 mol of available SO₂ — 2.574 L of pure gas at RTP, and the acid demand is only 3.836 g of HCl. Run into 250 g of water, the pot holds 0.4208 mol/kg — 23.2% of the saturated rung, which is the absorption arithmetic's own honesty: the door fills a pot to a fifth of what water could hold, and the train's exit bottle is the insurance, not the plan.

Door two: the burner (the from-scratch door, N51). Burn the element itself — S + O₂ → SO₂ (BALANCED), ΔH° = −296.8 kJ per mole of oxide [convergent, the standard enthalpy class] — and the garden shelf's dust becomes the gas with nothing but a match. This is the corpus's N51 (The Science Furry, How to make sulfuric acid from sulfur, 2021-11-15, transcript-level): the amateur apparatus is the pattern's own lesson — sulfur burning in the bottom of a cut test tube, an aquarium air pump pushing the exit gas into water (pattern P11's improvised-hardware face: cut tube, aquarium pump), the SO₂ absorbed as sulfurous solution and then oxidized by the same drugstore peroxide. The honest datum the register carries is the chapter's own inheritance: the product measured 2% acid by density — the from-scratch route's own verdict on itself, delivered by the practitioner's hydrometer. The ledger re-derives it (Lab 17.2's B2): 2.00 g of sulfur burned into 200 g of water delivers 0.06238 mol of SO₂ — 3.996 g, at 0.3119 mol/kg — and the climb to +6 demands 0.06238 mol of peroxide: 2.122 g of pure H₂O₂, which is 70.73 g of the 3% bottle (or 6.53 g of Chapter 16's percarbonate box, the solid peroxide source the oxidizer chapter promised this bench by name). The product: 6.118 g of sulfuric acid in a 274.7 g pot = 2.23% w/w — the arithmetic ceiling reproducing the register's density-measured 2% almost digit for digit, with the model's honesty label printed where it belongs ([flagged sketch]: the ceiling assumes full absorption; real pots lose gas at the pump's draft, and N51's own 2% is the field datum the ceiling brackets).
