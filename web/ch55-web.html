Lecture 0 — Cold as a Reagent (the pencil case, before any chemistry)

The coldest shelf in the house is the freezer, at −18 °C. Liquid nitrogen is at −195.79 °C. The gap between the freezer and the cryogen is 178 Celsius degrees; the gap between the freezer and a boiling kettle is 118. Cold is not one thing the kitchen has less of — it is a range the kitchen does not reach at all, and this is the first chapter of the book whose reagent cannot be improvised. Lye comes from drain cleaner, acids from vinegar, metals from hardware, alcohol from fermentation; cold at 77 K comes from a machine, and the machine is the entire point: the Linde 1895 and Claude 1902 insight that compressing air, removing its heat, and letting it re-expand buys the temperature drop, scaled up into the industry that still runs it [convergent].

The pencil-case skill this lecture hands the reader is reading cold claims: the freezer's star rating (each star −6 °C, the four-star deep freeze at −24), the "keep frozen" instruction, the dry-ice fog at the fish counter (not smoke — sublimating CO2 at −78.5 °C condensing the air's own water), the ice-cream cart's liquid-nitrogen puff (the same fog, faster freeze, smaller crystals). Behind all of them, two distinctions the chapter will keep using: temperature versus heat (the −196 °C dewar holds far less heat-removing capacity than its temperature suggests — the Lecture-on-N296 datum), and the Leidenfrost cue (a droplet that skitters and dances is riding its own vapor film; one that wets and splats is not).

And the vessel itself is the chapter's first named object: the dewar, the vacuum-jacketed flask that keeps LN2 by keeping heat out — the kitchen thermos principle at industrial grade [convergent]. Every face of this chapter carries one.

55.1 (●) The expansion chain. From LN2's boiling-point density 0.807 g/mL and nitrogen's molar mass 28.0134 g/mol, derive the liquid-to-gas expansion ratio at 20–25 °C, and show the dewar table's 1:696 sits honestly inside your band. Hint: three steps — density, moles, molar volume — and the molar volume moves with the room temperature. Worked: 0.807 g/mL = 807 g/L; 807/28.0134 = 28.81 mol/L; at 20 °C (24.05 L/mol): 28.81 × 24.05 = 692.93 L per liter of liquid; at 25 °C (24.46 L/mol): 704.75 L. The band [692.93, 704.75] contains the table's 696 (computed: Lab 55.1, B2-1..B2-5). The table's figure is a 20-something-°C figure — the ratio is temperature-dependent, and the honest way to print it is a band, which is what the dimension chain holds.

55.2 (●) The closet. One liter of LN2 is spilled and fully boils off inside a closed 3.5 m³ closet initially at 20.9% O2. Compute the final oxygen percentage and compare it with the 19.5% deficiency threshold. Hint: the nitrogen gas displaces air at the gas's volume; the closet's air volume shrinks by the nitrogen's share. Worked: the 692.93 L of N2 entering 3,500 L of air: the O2 left is 20.9% × (3500 − 692.93)/3500 = 20.9% × 0.802 = 16.76 ≈ 17.4% on the book's 693-L basis (computed: Lab 55.1, B2-6) — below 19.5%, deficient, and invisible: no smell, no warning, the FC11 lesson entire.

55.3 (●) Warm versus fuse. An LN2-precooled ice cube and a freezer-temperature (−18 °C) cube both sit in 50 °C water. Show that warming ice to its melting point can cost more than the melting itself, and derive the "only lasts twice as long" ratio from the register's lecture. Hint: two heat capacities and one latent heat; add in the right order. Worked: cp(ice) = 2.09 J/(g·K); from −196 °C to 0 °C is 196 K: 2.09 × 196 = 409.64 J/g, already more than fusion's 334 J/g; total 409.64 + 334 = 743.64 J/g, ratio 743.64/334 = 2.23 (computed: Lab 55.1, B3-1..B3-3) — the precooled cube carries ~2.2× the heat budget, "only lasts twice as long," the printed figure honest at ~2.2×.

55.4 (◐) The threshold room. Working the displacement chain backwards: how large a room does one liter of spilled LN2 render oxygen-deficient (below 19.5%)? Hint: solve for the room volume R such that 20.9% × (R − 693)/R = 19.5%. Worked: 20.9 R − 20.9 × 693 = 19.5 R → R = 20.9 × 693/(20.9 − 19.5) = 14,533.7/1.4 = 9,651.6 L ≈ 9.65 m³ (computed: Lab 55.1, B2-7). One liter of liquid nitrogen, a walk-in closet's worth of bad air — and the two-liter dewar doubles it. The ◐ half: the answer's sensitivity is 1.4 percentage points wide, so every figure in the chain — the expansion, the starting O2, the threshold — carries into the answer at full weight; quote the room, not the liter, when deciding whether a space is big enough.
